Chapter Seventy-Three

Torn Peach, Broke Ji-yeon

FRIDAY · THE LAST DAY OF SEPTEMBER Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Unit 101 — Song Ji-yeon’s flat, late morning

The CCM building shrank in the rearview mirror, taking Ham Eun-jung and her monthly meeting with it, and Song Ji-yeon pointed the car toward Cheongdam-dong.

Friday. Nothing waiting at the company that needed his face, so he wasn’t going to give it one.

He walked into his flat a little after eleven. Breakfast had been a throwaway fried-egg sandwich at Eun-jung’s place, and now lunch was knocking, with six-odd hours still separating him from dinner. He’d cook something quick — two dishes, whatever the fridge offered — because a skipped meal always left his day feeling like a sentence missing its verb.

A stir-fried beef plate and a plate of greens later, he was sitting down when the phone lit up on the dining table. Sulli. A KakaoTalk message.

KakaoTalk · Sulli

Sulli

Oppa, I’m really sorry about yesterday… Vic-unnie didn’t mean any of it. She just worries about me too much. Please don’t take what she said to heart.

He read it once, set the phone face-up beside his plate, and picked up his chopsticks. Steam was still rising off the beef.

✦ ✦ ✦

MEANWHILE Gangnam, Seoul SM Entertainment — a practice room

The wall-shaking music cut out, and the practice room surfaced into ordinary silence. f(x) had just run their new choreography into the ground.

Victoria stood scrolling a delivery app, ordering lunch for three, thumb working, eyes never lifting.

“Amber, Luna, Soo-jung — what do you want?”

“Jajangmyeon!” Amber swiped the sweat off her forehead, dropped onto the floor, and tipped her head back to think. “And tangsuyuk!”

“Jajangmyeon for me too!” Luna’s hand shot up. “Add an order of fried dumplings!”

Krystal sat to one side, toweling the sweat off her neck. She didn’t answer. Her eyes had gone, on their own, to the corner of the room.

On the sofa in the southeast corner sat Sulli — alone, knees hugged to her chest, phone clutched in both hands. The light of the screen sat on her face. She stared at it like she was waiting for a message, or like she was staring at nothing at all; either way, she might as well have been in a different room from the rest of them.

Krystal looked away, over at Victoria tapping the order, and asked, quietly.

“Vic-unnie… aren’t you going to ask Sulli what she wants?”

Victoria’s thumb paused on the screen. Half a beat. Then it moved again.

“If she’s hungry, she can say so herself.”

The tone was level — perfectly level, no anger in it anywhere. And that levelness was the loudest wrong thing in the room.

Krystal pressed her lips together and let it go. But she already knew this wasn’t nothing.

Yesterday Sulli and Vic-unnie had gone out to dinner with Song Ji-yeon, and the two of them had come back wrong. She didn’t know what had happened at that table. She knew that last night, back at the dorm, Sulli had shut herself in her room and hadn’t touched so much as a single one of her favorite snacks. And Vic-unnie — outwardly the same as ever, but Krystal had known her too long. The rhythm of her speech. The pace of her walk. Even the half-second pauses in her glances. Everything was a half-tone off.

This morning, on the whole ride to the company, the two of them had exchanged zero words.

At the practice room, Sulli had folded herself into the corner, and Vic-unnie had been very carefully not looking in that direction.

Which left exactly one conclusion: a cold war. And the cause of a cold war like this one had a name, and the name was a man.

Even Amber and Luna could feel it. Amber, still on the floor, snuck a glance at Victoria, another at the corner, then leaned into Krystal’s ear.

“Soo-jung-ah — what happened between Vic-unnie and Sulli? It’s been weird since breakfast.”

“I don’t know either.” Krystal kept her voice down to match. “They went out to dinner yesterday and came back like this. I asked Sulli — nothing. I asked Vic-unnie — nothing.”

“It’s off, right?” Luna leaned in too. “All through run-throughs today, Vic-unnie never once made eye contact with Sulli. Not once. That’s not normal.”

Krystal bit her lip. She looked at Vic-unnie, head still down over the delivery app, then at the girl curled around her phone in the corner, and something in her chest pulled tight.

She didn’t want to see the team like this. The five of them had come a long way from debut — Vic-unnie carrying the leader’s load in a country not her own, the rest of them working their whole lives not to add a single gram to it. And now a crack had opened between the leader and a member, and the crack was making the whole floor tilt.

She thought about it, and couldn’t help trying one more time.

“Vic-unnie, maybe… just ask her? She’s barely eaten since this morning—”

“I said — if she’s hungry, she’ll say so.”

The interruption came down clean and final, a door shut with weight behind it.

Victoria didn’t want a cold war either, for the record. But the girl would not listen. On the entire ride home yesterday she had sat there defending that awful man, sentence after sentence. As far as Victoria was concerned the verdict wasn’t complicated: Song Ji-yeon was a fraud wearing a nice jacket, and Sulli — her sweetest, most obedient Sulli — had decided he walked on water. They had fought. Properly fought. And she still could not understand why the one member who never made trouble had chosen a man, of all hills, to make her stand on.

So there was a banked fire sitting in Victoria’s chest, and it muttered one more curse at Song Ji-yeon while her thumb went back to the delivery app — jabbing noticeably harder now.

Krystal opened her mouth, read the no-entry sign on Victoria’s entire posture, and swallowed the rest of the sentence.

The practice room went quiet. Nothing left in it but the hum of the air conditioner, and now and then a faint buzz from the corner.

Amber and Luna traded a look: nothing to be done here.

They both knew something serious had happened between Vic-unnie and Sulli. Neither would say. So there was nothing to ask, and nowhere to put the asking — which just left the heavy air pressing down on a room that was supposed to be the easy part of their day.

In the corner, Sulli sat with her knees hugged, goosebumps rising on her bare arms under the air-conditioning. She didn’t reach for her jacket.

She just stared at her phone. Typing. Deleting. Typing again.

After Vic-unnie and Song Ji-yeon-oppa’s dinner had ended the way it ended, she hadn’t slept properly all night. She’d lain in her dorm bed turning over the same useless question: how did it come to this? Vic-unnie and oppa were the two people in the world best to her. Why couldn’t they stand in the same room without it catching fire?

Now she was caught on both hooks at once — afraid Vic-unnie would really go and make trouble for oppa, and afraid oppa would file last night under reasons to think less of her.

She knew Vic-unnie was on her side. She knew it completely. Vic-unnie had carried the leader’s load since the day they debuted — in a country not her own, managing five girls’ lives, absorbing the company’s pressure, standing in front of the media and the fans with her arms out. That suspicion of hers, that armor — it wasn’t a character flaw. It was survival instinct, ground into her by years of this industry.

But being on the receiving end of that distrust still hurt.

Song Ji-yeon-oppa was a friend she had chosen. From the chance meeting at the Korea Exchange, to every time he’d shown up for her after — the support, the meals, the late-night chats — she could feel the sincerity in him, plain as warmth off a cup. Oppa was a genuinely good person.

She did not want her leader looking at him through the lens reserved for con men.

Because when Vic-unnie looked at him that way, it made Sulli feel small too.

She’d wanted to text an apology last night. She’d sat there with the blank message field and realized she didn’t know what the words were. Sorry? Sorry for what? Sorry my leader thinks your friend is a fraud? Even in her own head it sounded deranged.

She’d hesitated all night. All morning. And now she took one deep breath and ended it, because she was not going to let this put a wall between her and oppa.

She bit her lip, typed fast, checked the message three times for typos, and hit send before the courage could evaporate.

Then she gripped the phone in both hands, nails pressing crescents into her palm, heart hammering like something with hooves was loose in her chest.

How would he answer? Would he think she was annoying? Would he file her with Vic-unnie, under unreasonable?

She stared at the screen. One second. Two. Every one of them a full geological era.

Bzzt.

Her heart relocated to somewhere just under her chin. She breathed in, unlocked the phone.

His reply was already up: It’s fine — I didn’t take any of it to heart. I understand your leader completely. She’s doing her job.

The breath went out of her all at once. No starch in it, no polite distance — the exact voice he always used with her, all the way back to the first message.

He really wasn’t angry!!!

The practice room’s refrigerated air somehow felt warmer. Sulli smiled at the screen — a full, unguarded smile — and her thumbs were already moving.

✦ ✦ ✦

On the other side of the river, Song Ji-yeon was eating his beef one-handed, phone in the other, the corner of his mouth climbing on its own.

This kid has loyalty. All-in, no hedge, right into the teeth of her own leader.

KakaoTalk · Sulli

Sulli

That’s a relief!! I was scared you’d be mad at us… Oh right — I’m not changing the stock account password, so please keep looking after it for me! I trust you, oppa! Even if it all somehow goes to zero, I won’t blame you one bit.

She’d laid the word trust down flat and heavy, exactly where he’d see it. That was her answer to everything that had happened at that dinner — Vic-unnie could disapprove; Sulli was not moving.

Two days of watching numbers go up and to the right had also, admittedly, helped the medicine go down. She couldn’t read a candlestick chart to save her life, but she could read more money than yesterday. If she’d gone and changed the password because her leader had raised an eyebrow — what kind of person would that make her?

He thought for a moment, then typed.

KakaoTalk · Sulli

Song Ji-yeon

Noted. Keep the password. It’s not like I’m after your allowance money. But next time your Vic-unnie wants to book a dinner with me, tip me off in advance so I can show up in a suit — save her the trouble of deciding I sell bootleg DVDs on a street corner.

A three-second pause. Then, from the corner of an SM practice room, one very undignified snort of laughter escaped into the air-conditioning.

“Pfft—”

Bootleg DVDs? Oppa was ridiculous. The entire morning’s knot — every hour of tossing and turning compressed into that one snort — came untied.

KakaoTalk · Sulli

Sulli

Hahahaha oppa you’re TERRIBLE!! If Vic-unnie ever finds out I showed you that message I’m dead!! But deal — next time she books you, I’m your early-warning system!

Song Ji-yeon

Then congratulations, you’re now my mole inside f(x). Code phrase for future contact: “fine weather today.”

Sulli

Ne!! Mission accepted!! Oh — and the stocks are in your hands, oppa. When I’m rich, BBQ on me!

Song Ji-yeon

Deal. I’ll wait for you to get rich.

He said it lightly. Privately, a different decision had already been notarized somewhere behind his sternum: there would be no next dinner with Song Qian. None. However rich the Goddess Handbook’s bond rewards might run, he had precisely zero intention of courting that particular goddess. Self-appointed judges were not his type.

The screen lit once more. He speared a piece of beef, chewed, and typed one-thumbed.

KakaoTalk · Sulli

Song Ji-yeon

Gotta go — food’s getting cold.

Sulli read those four words, and her stomach — finally unclenched — registered its own existence. Loudly.

She hadn’t eaten a single thing since waking. And now that the anxiety had let go of her, hunger arrived like a landlord: all at once, with paperwork. She stared at food’s getting cold as if she could see a whole steaming table through the screen, and swallowed.

She hadn’t been hungry before. She was suddenly, catastrophically hungry now.

“Okay, oppa! Talk later!”

She pocketed the phone, stood — and her sweep of the room snagged on a scene.

Not far away, around the low table, sat Vic-unnie, Soo-jung, Amber, and Luna. Fresh delivery boxes stood open between them — jajangmyeon, tangsuyuk, fried dumplings, the works — and they were handing out chopsticks, chatting, laughing.

Four people, having a perfectly good time. Nobody had called her over.

Sulli stood a few steps off, stomach growling harder. She bit her lip, hesitated exactly one heartbeat — and then thickened her skin and walked over anyway.

Vic-unnie spoils me the most. Last night was a onetime accident. She can’t still be mad.

She crossed to Victoria’s side, squatted down, tilted her whole small face up, and deployed the weapon.

“Vic-unnie~”

Victoria, mid-way through passing Luna her chopsticks, paused. She looked over. Said nothing. Her eyes asked the question on her behalf.

“I want jajangmyeon, Vic-unnie.” Soft. Sweet. Approximately one hundred percent calculated. She added the fine print: “I haven’t eaten anything… I’m so hungry…”

Victoria’s gaze rested on her face for a moment, and one corner of her mouth twitched upward — a millimeter, half a second, gone.

You little brat. So you can come around.

She didn’t say anything of the sort. She set down the chopsticks, pushed her own untouched bowl of jajangmyeon in front of Sulli, dropped a piece of tangsuyuk into it, and made one small sound deep in her throat.

“Mm.”

The face stayed glacial. The hands had already confessed everything.

Sulli’s eyes went bright as theater marquee lights. She was halfway through a joyful reply when her brain, mid-bite of noodle, served her an unscheduled thought.

She looked at Vic-unnie, eating beside her with her head bent. Then she thought about the oppa on her phone, the one currently typing “food’s getting cold.” One of them ran on ice. The other ran on bad jokes. Put them in a room together and you could power the building off the friction alone.

Fine. Verdict reached: Vic-unnie and oppa are simply incompatible by constitution. Solution: don’t put them in a room.

Oppa was her friend, after all. Not Vic-unnie’s.

No meetings, no incidents. Foreign policy, solved. She slurped her noodles, and the last of the morning’s stone in her chest dissolved entirely.

Today, on reflection, was not so bad.

✦ ✦ ✦

EARLY AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Unit 101 — the study

Back in Cheongdam-dong, Song Ji-yeon slid his plate into the dishwasher and pressed the button. The machine hummed its low industrial hum.

The Sulli thing was a small eddy, already downstream. He and Song Qian weren’t close and were never going to be. His entire impression of the woman came from a handful of variety appearances in his last life, watched from a couch in LA. Without the Handbook’s appetite for coincidence, the two of them would have lived out this whole second life on separate planets.

An unconnected idol the Handbook had never bound — that was not a person his attention owed anything to.

He walked into the study, woke the computer, and logged into his brokerage.

POSITION · SEWON ELECTRONICS (023910) · DAY 4
EntryMonday’s open
Chart since entryFour consecutive green candles
Unrealized gain+26%
Card projection (5 sessions)+37.8%

That red line ran up the screen like a vine that knew where it was going, and just looking at it loosened something between his shoulder blades.

He’d never fully trusted the Stock Forecast Card — not in his body. Text on a system panel, no matter how confident, stays theoretical; it doesn’t grab you by the collar. But the balance line in his account was not theoretical. It was money, arriving, in the present tense. And only now, with the account fat and climbing, did the thing finally convert from belief to knowledge: the card works. This machine prints.

He closed his eyes and let the solid feeling spread all the way out to his fingertips.

Now it was just a matter of sitting still until Monday’s opening bell, dumping the entire position, and closing the trade with a bow. Even if the tape gave a little back between now and then, the profit would clear ₩3.6 billion — and that was one card, one week. Samsung had been a warm-up lap by comparison.

Liquidate. Draw the next Forecast Card. Hunt the next target. Money rolling over money rolling over money.

He thought back to the first Stock Forecast Card — the one he’d clawed out of the Eun-jung activation after actual difficulty — and the memory of his own reaction to it. He’d sneered. Sneered. If time travel permitted, he would go back and slap that man across both cheeks.

Sitting inside a gold mine, complaining about the decor.

Really, if you ran the tape, Eun-jung was his lucky star and it wasn’t close: their first meeting had switched the entire Handbook on; the first Goddess Ability he’d ever completed had run through her; and the card she’d come attached to was out here over-delivering like it was embarrassed for the others. Lucky star wasn’t even a superlative anymore. It was a job title.

He pulled up Sulli’s account for a second look. Her entry had come two days late, so her climb was a tamer cousin of his — but she was up ten percent, two days, ₩60 million in the green.

ACCOUNT · CHOI JIN-RI · DAY 2
EntryTwo days late, at his hand
Principal₩300,000,000
Leverage applied2× (₩600,000,000 deployed)
Unrealized gain+10% · ≈ ₩60,000,000

And how did a ₩300 million principal produce ₩60 million in two days on a ten percent move?

Because he’d put her on two-times leverage.

Why was he that confident? Don’t ask. If you asked, the honest answer was that the man was broke — every spare won he owned was already riding the Sewon line, and when your own pockets are empty, the polite thing to do is make the brokerage lend her the rest.

He closed the computer. The screen went dark — and before he could push back the chair, the phone on the desk rang.

Caller ID: Her Majesty the Mother.

He watched the name bounce on the screen. What was she calling about?

He answered, and before a single syllable could leave him, Kang Seol-ah’s voice arrived through the speaker at complaint velocity.

Phone · Kang Seol-ah → Song Ji-yeon

“Yah! Song Ji-yeon! Oh, so you DO still take your mother’s calls! Are you planning to live out there in the wild forever — never come home again? Your father and I are about to forget what you look like!”

Urgent and bright at once — the complaint in its fully mature form, the kind only a Korean-immigrant mother can raise, twenty-two years of practice behind every syllable.

Song Ji-yeon held the phone a diplomatic distance from his ear, waited out the opening barrage, and brought it back.

He could picture her without trying: in the kitchen, phone wedged on her shoulder, sorting vegetables into a bowl with one hand, brow furrowed, mouth filing complaints, eyes doing all the actual talking — the eyes said hurry home.

He rubbed his nose, guiltily.

Now that she mentioned it… his last visit home had been something north of twenty days ago. That stretch had been all ONGI store conversions on one side and the entire Ham Eun-jung situation on the other, and “home” had quietly fallen off the calendar. Which was, objectively, his fault.

“Mom. I was wrong. Genuinely, categorically wrong.” The surrender came pre-folded. “I’ve been busy — the company’s young, everything lands on my desk.”

“Busy, busy, busy — that’s the only word you know! When your father was your age, you were already big enough to bus tables at the diner! And you? Not even a shadow of a girlfriend! Gone ten days, fifteen days at a stretch — you are trying to send your mother to an early grave!”

“Mom, come on. I’m out here making the money.”

“The money! What is money for if no wife ever comes home with it? What are you doing, saving it for Seollal?”

That one plugged him straight into the wall.

His mouth opened — actually, about that, I do have a girlfriend — and his better judgment tackled the sentence in mid-air.

Not yet. Wait until things with Eun-jung settled a little more. Because if his mother learned he had a girlfriend, the campaign that followed would make the nuclear-marriage offensive of the past few years look like a warm-up act. She would demand the girl at the dinner table daily. Hourly, if the technology allowed it.

“Mom, don’t rush me. I’m still young.”

“Young?! You’re twenty-two! The Kim boy next door is a year younger than you and his baby already calls her Grandma! When do I get my turn? When, Song Ji-yeon?!”

The nagging arrived one grievance at a time, each with its own luggage. But somewhere under the noise, a reminder surfaced: he actually did have business with his father.

The SAVORY F&B handover. That conversation deserved a table, not a phone line — and the right moment was standing right in front of him, wearing an apron.

“Mom — don’t be angry. I’ll come home today. There. Before dark, word of honor.”

He’d been planning to go home this weekend anyway, for exactly that conversation. ShiningYouth existed on paper now; the T-ara acquisition was marching; from here on, the center of gravity of his life tilted toward entertainment. The hotpot business had entered its steady expansion phase — stable, self-propelled, no longer a machine that needed his hands on it daily. His attention had somewhere better to be.

And Song Min-seok had spent half a lifetime in food and beverage — built the family trade up from one small diner, one pan at a time. On experience, on network, on scar tissue, the old man outclassed him in every column. Father coming out of semi-retirement to take back SAVORY F&B’s daily operations was, by a wide margin, the best configuration available.

That freed every ounce of Song Ji-yeon’s bandwidth for the thing that actually had no ceiling.

And his father would understand. Nobody alive wanted to see the boy climb higher and farther than Song Min-seok did.

On the other end of the line, the words I’ll come home today completed their journey, and Kang Seol-ah’s mood executed a flawless one-hundred-eighty-degree turn. The grievance evaporated; what replaced it was joy with nowhere to hide.

“Really?! Your own words — no take-backs! I’ll make extra — all your favorites. Come early!”

“Got it, Mom.”

“What do you want? The sweet-and-sour ribs — should I make them? Or samgyetang? You said the ribs were good last time; I’ll double them. And?”

“Anything, Mom. Your call.”

“Then I’m putting a pork-hock soup on too. Look at you — I can tell from your voice you’ve been eating badly out there. Thin as a monkey.”

“Mom, where exactly am I thin? I’m seventy kilos.”

He glanced down at himself while he said it. A hundred and eighty-five centimeters, post-enhancement, muscle packed dense — the classic build: slim in a shirt, considerably less slim out of one.

“Seventy?! Then you need to eat MORE. Your father was eighty at your age! You haven’t even reached the starting line!”

“Yes, yes. All yours, Mom. I just finished lunch — I’ll call you when I leave this evening.”

“Good, good. Drive carefully — no speeding! Call me when you’re close so I can start the wok!”

“Got it, Mom. Hanging up.”

He looked at the dead phone in his hand and shook his head, smiling despite himself.

In a mother’s eyes, the son is eternally underweight. It is the one constant of the universe.
