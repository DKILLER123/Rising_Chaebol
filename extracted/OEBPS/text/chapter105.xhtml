Chapter One Hundred Five

Sulli’s Dream

SATURDAY EVENING · CONTINUED Cheongdam-dong, Seoul The Japanese dining bar — and the booth on the diagonal

Ever since the two JYP trainees had settled into the booth on the diagonal, Song Ji-yeon’s attention had kept drifting back to that corner of the room like a compass needle that had found its north and stopped consulting the map.

Beside him, Park Ji-yeon had just lifted a slice of salmon sashimi with her chopsticks — and looked up in time to catch him at it.

She traced the line of his sight. It landed squarely on the two girls from the greeting.

Heads bent close, nearly touching over one shared menu. The ponytailed one pointed at some page, eyes curving into crescents as she said something; the other leaned against her shoulder to look, nodding, shaking her head, until both of them collapsed into low, giggling heaps.

Young, undeniably. Radiant, undeniably.

No wonder oppa kept staring.

“Oppa, you are such a flirt.” An elbow landed in his ribs, followed by a pout, the whole message marinading in vinegar. “Two of the most popular idols in the country are sitting at your own table, and your eyes are out here window-shopping.”

Hmph.

She was, for the record, this year’s number-one girl-group visual. A beauty of her caliber at his elbow, and his eyes still wouldn’t behave.

Certified. A total, certified heartbreaker.

Eun-jung set her teacup down and followed the conversation — and his gaze — across the room.

She wasn’t jealous, exactly. It was more that he really was watching the two trainees too intently.

And staring at strangers like that, at close range, was simply rude.

Song Ji-yeon surfaced from his thoughts to find Park Ji-yeon studying him with a whole face full of ah, so oppa likes this type of little sister — and he couldn’t help laughing.

“It’s not— hang on. Aren’t they both still minors? Also: doesn’t one of them look exactly like a shiba inu?”

Park Ji-yeon went blank.

“AH?! Oppa, you stared at her this whole time because she looks like a shiba?!”

Eun-jung startled too, and his arm received a quick corrective poke, the voice dropping low.

“Hey — you can’t talk about people like that with them right there. We’re this close. What if they hear?”

He blinked, uncomprehending — and then it caught up with him.

Right. It was 2012. The whole animal-resemblance taxonomy that fandom would one day build into an entire industry of its own hadn’t gone mainstream yet. Telling a girl, tonight, in this restaurant, that she looked like a dog… was not guaranteed to land as the compliment he meant it as.

“No, no — it’s a good thing! It’s praise! Er… how do I explain this…” He rummaged through his vocabulary like a man hunting a lost sock in the dark. “It means she’s cute, and her smile is — what’s the word — healing. That kind of feeling.”

He gave up mid-sentence. In his past life he had never gone more than ankle-deep into fandom waters; he had simply never needed to explain the shiba to anyone before, and it turned out the shiba was remarkably difficult to explain.

“In the States, shibas are the dogs people drive three counties to adopt. Round faces. Permanent smiles. You see one and your hand rises on its own to pet it. It is absolutely a compliment. Top-shelf.”

Park Ji-yeon tilted her head, deeply suspicious.

“You’re sure it’s a compliment? What if the girl doesn’t enjoy being compared to a dog?”

A slow look went over at the second trainee, and the verdict arrived at its own pace.

“Mm. Now that you mention it — the shiba really does fit. Look at the one holding the menu, Ji-yeon. Round face. Those eyes that tilt up at the corners. And when she smiles, they curve right up into… mm. That soft, squishy, sunny sort of feeling. There is a genuine shiba in there.”

Eun-jung was the one of them with actual dog mileage — Mushroom’s entire custody arc had run through her hands — which meant she spoke puppy natively, and her translation landed instantly harder than his.

Song Ji-yeon’s eyes lit up like a man who had just located his people.

“See?! I told you she was a shiba!”

In his past life, the internet had occasionally served him side-by-side comparison charts of that exact girl and shiba puppies, usually captioned in fonts that had given up.

It wasn’t even a resemblance. It was a mirror.

Park Ji-yeon, hearing her own unnie back the theory, looked over one more time and finally parsed it in the abstract.

“Whoa… unnie, now that you say it — she really does look like a shiba.”

Crisis defused. The window-shopping line of inquiry was quietly retired, and only then did Song Ji-yeon breathe again.

The truth about the drifting eyes was something else entirely: he had been running an acquisition file.

ShiningYouth Entertainment was about to stand on its own legs. He had just spent a fortune acquiring T-ara — but a company built to last could not live on one girl group forever.

The original plan had been patience. Let T-ara deliver real results in Japan and China; let the company’s name ring out properly; only then start quietly recruiting trainees with real futures attached — the kind that detonate after debut.

Tonight’s collision had him seriously considering moving the calendar.

Those two were core members of a future legendary girl group. Dance skill: unquestioned. Ceiling: unquestioned. Intercept them now — sign them, rebuild that group under his own roof — and ShiningYouth’s future was, roughly, half-secured on the spot.

That group had stayed at the top for ten straight years. Ten — without one member contract scandal in the entire run. Could he name a longer-running girl group in this industry? He genuinely could not.

Then his reason came back online and rated the fantasy: not yet.

They were JYP trainees, and a company like JYP does not lose trainees politely. And what was ShiningYouth at this exact moment? A day-old company in a freshly rented, entirely empty building. With what, precisely, would he be bidding against JYP?

Besides — rebuilding that group meant rebuilding all nine. The other seven were somewhere out there, whereabouts unknown, possibly not even on the Peninsula yet. Years early, either way you counted it.

Rice is eaten one bowl at a time. A road is walked one step at a time. And a hot mouth burns.

He let the strategizing fold itself away, and aimed the grin at Park Ji-yeon instead.

“I hear that in this country’s industry, when a sunbae runs into her hoobaes at a restaurant, the sunbae picks up the bill. You’re the sunbae at this table, Ji-yeon. Can’t get caught short. Did you bring enough money?”

A NOTE ON TABLE MANNERS · THE SUNBAE’S BILL

In the Korean entertainment industry of 2012, the custom was real: sunbae meets hoobae at a restaurant, the sunbae pays. Rank, in that world, came with obligations attached.

The fine print, less quoted: the obligation ran to hoobaes who had already debuted. Trainees — unsigned, absent from every roster — did not count. They bowed; the ledger did not move.

Park Ji-yeon had never heard of the rule in her life.

She was the group’s maknae. Her unnis had handled every bill since her first trainee dinner; the mechanics of paying had simply never been her department. Suddenly discovering that she was now somebody’s sunbae, she felt a flicker of pure, unseasoned panic.

But a sunbae, in public, does not fold. The chin came up with magnificent bravado.

“Obviously I brought enough! Treating two hoobaes to one meal? I can carry that.”

Eun-jung watched the two of them volley, hiding her laughter behind her hand — and did not correct the arithmetic. One meal’s cost sat comfortably inside every budget at that table.

Let the dragon enjoy being the sunbae for one night.

The meal wound down rich and contented, plans for the new building weaving through the last of the conversation.

At the register, Song Ji-yeon had no intention of actually letting the dragon pay. He flagged the server, settled his own table’s bill — and, in the same quiet breath, covered the corner booth’s check along with it.

Then the three of them were out on the street, having exchanged nothing with the two trainees beyond the bows. No acquaintance existed to interrupt. It had been a collision over dinner, nothing more.

SATURDAY NIGHT Seoul — Apgujeong, then Cheongdam-dong The home-bound run

The night had picked up the first true cold of deep autumn, and the streetlamps drew three shadows out long and thin across the pavement.

“Come on. I’ll get you both home.” The Mercedes woke under his key.

At the car, a pair of hands captured Eun-jung’s, and the full arsenal deployed.

“Eun-jung-unnie~ sleep over at my place tonight! Please, please, please~”

Eun-jung patted the back of one captured hand and shook her head.

A full day trailing him through a four-story building — stacked on top of a night of terrible sleep at the campsite. Her whole body ached, and she was honest about it.

“Not tonight, Ji-yeon. I’m genuinely at my limit. I want my own bed and my own quiet.”

She turned.

“Oppa, would you mind dropping me in Apgujeong?”

“Mind? Get in.”

Park Ji-yeon was visibly deflated and visibly understanding, and climbed into the back seat without a fuss.

The car slid into the night, bound for Apgujeong, and before long it stood quiet at the foot of Eun-jung’s building.

“Eun-jung. Rest properly tonight — send me one message when you’re up.”

“Ne, I will. Oppa, Ji-yeon — get home safe.”

A small wave, a soft smile, and she was gone through the lobby doors.

The car moved again, and Cheongdam-dong rose around it. Soon enough, the Daewoo Royal County garage swallowed them, and the two of them stood at the elevator watching the floor numbers climb.

“Oppa, it’s still early. Want to come up and get destroyed at a few rounds of games?” Still bright-eyed, offering it up like a dare.

He pressed two fingers to his brow and surrendered in advance.

“Pass.” No hesitation, no mercy. “I’m honestly dead on my feet. Tonight’s agenda has two items — a very hot shower, and a very horizontal bed. Your games will have to go on without me.”

The cheeks puffed out — but she knew the past two days had wrung him dry, and she let it go without a fight.

“Fine. Good night, oppa. See you tomorrow~”

The doors opened, and each of them went home to their own floor.

SUNDAY MORNING Seodaemun-gu, Seoul Ewha Womans University — the main path

Morning light, thin and gold, lay across the old brick and stately pediments of the campus. Barely a dozen hours after dropping the dragon at her own door, Song Ji-yeon was strolling through it.

The reason he was here at this hour was a phone call that had arrived the moment he opened his eyes.

Phone · Sulli → Song Ji-yeon

“Oppa, good morning! Are you free today?”

“Just woke up. What’s got you calling this early?”

“The day before yesterday you sold all our stocks for us! Unnie and I made so, so much money!”

“Hara-unnie wants to treat you to a huge thank-you dinner — but she’s too embarrassed to ask herself, so she begged me to do the asking!”

“Oh? Clever routing. A request that comes through you can’t be declined.”

“You’re teasing me! So that’s a yes, oppa?”

“Since you’ve framed it that way, how could I refuse? Where?”

“Ewha! Hara-unnie has an outdoor shoot this morning — an ELLEgirl pictorial, right here on campus! Could oppa come and wait at school? Then the second she wraps, we all go eat!”

“Ewha… Ewha Womans University?”

“Yes! Oppa’s never seen a Korean campus, right? Perfect excuse to tour the country’s most famous women’s university~”

“…Fine. I’ll drive over and wait at the front gate.”

“Perfect! See you soon, oppa!”

His eyes had still been full of sleep and his voice still rough at the edges when he’d answered. The peach’s excitement had come through the line packed into every syllable, like air in a balloon one size too small.

He cleaned up, grabbed the keys, and drove to Ewha, where Sulli met him at the gate.

They walked the campus together, unhurried, and found a bench along the main path. Some way off, on the wide lawn, Goo Hara’s shoot was running at full bustle.

It was Sunday, and the campus was anything but empty.

Along the tree-lined path, two women in track suits jogged past in step, breath puffing white. From a distant basketball court came the irregular thunder of a dribbled ball and the bright, sparrow-colored noise of cheering. Small knots of students sat cross-legged on the grass, talking about nothing urgent.

No running footsteps. No one sprinting with a fortress of textbooks in their arms. The whole campus was breathing the slow, luxurious air of a day off.

Sulli had her chin propped in both hands, and her eyes tracked every laughing group that passed, the longing in them undisguised.

“Lucky…” The word drifted out of her, half to herself.

“What’s lucky?”

“Look at them. Sunday, and they get to sleep until their bodies wake up on their own. Roll out of bed whenever, wander out at noon, and just… drift.” Her eyes were shining, and one hand came up to point at a cluster of girls drifting past, mid-laugh. “No manager counting down their morning. No camera following them. They laugh when something’s funny, they run when they feel like running. Oppa — look at the ones playing basketball. The ones jogging. A whole Sunday that belongs entirely to them. It’s so nice.”

His eyes went where the finger pointed. On the court, a handful of young men were scrambling over a loose ball; at the far end, a ring of joggers circled patiently.

“It’s Sunday.” He smiled. “Nobody’s commuting to class. Everyone’s out here doing exactly what they feel like. If it appeals to you that much, we’ll take a loop over there ourselves before we leave.”

“I’m so jealous of them. Even their Sundays are their own.”

She swung her legs where they hung from the bench, and the wanting in her voice had no bottom to it.

“Sleep in. Call a friend the second you’re up. Play ball, go for a run, or just sit on some grass and talk about nothing. I’ve never once had that.”

He turned his head and looked at her.

“And you? You’re an idol. You act. Don’t you like the life you have?”

“It’s not that I don’t like it!” The headshake came fast, the words chosen carefully after it. “The stage lights. The fans screaming my name. I love those — I really do. It’s just…” A pause, small and honest. “…whenever there’s a gap between takes, or a wait before a scene, I keep catching myself thinking — what if I could just be a normal girl for a while? Pack a bag of books. Walk through a school gate.”

Her gaze drifted after another cluster of girls chasing each other down the path, laughing, and her voice came down to almost nothing.

“Oppa. You finished university, right? What is it actually like?”

“University…”

He followed her gaze and let the memories of another life surface.

“It really was a lovely stretch of years. All the crushing weight of getting in just… ends. Nobody checks on you. The hours are yours. I did my degree at USC, back in Los Angeles — business school, the family trade translated into credit hours. So for me it was case studies and spreadsheets until my eyes glazed over, and then dragging my roommates out for pickup basketball, or sprinting across campus because the dining hall closed at eight and we’d lost track of time again. Finals week meant coffee-fueled all-nighters and cursing our life choices. But that feeling underneath all of it — the schedule belonging to nobody but you —” a small exhale of a laugh — “I do miss it.”

“U-S-C…” She repeated the letters slowly, as if tasting each one, eyes perfectly round. “It sounds so impressive! Oppa, you were that good at school? And a business degree — are you like those genius chaebol heirs in dramas? Running empires from their phones?”

“Not even close. One ordinary guy with a business degree and twenty-two years of waiting tables.”

He waved it off, a little embarrassed.

And it wasn’t modesty, either. If he hadn’t crossed the Pacific this time around, at this very hour he’d be in Los Angeles, buried in the middle floor of the SAVORY group’s American operations — vendor audits, payroll spreadsheets, expansion memos. The heir track, cubicle edition.

“Oppa, stop being humble.”

Both elbows went to her knees, chin into her palms, and she studied him with genuine intensity.

“So — were classes exhausting? Did you get along with your roommates? On a Sunday like this one, what did university students actually do?”

The barrage made him laugh. Weren’t these questions a child could answer?

Then again — she’d entered SM young enough that her trainee years might outweigh her school years. For that matter, was she even done with high school yet?

“You really do go straight for the fine print.” He played along. “Classes: the ones you like can’t tire you out, and the ones you don’t — that’s what the back row and a hood are for. Roommates: a pack of large, rowdy boys, permanently wrestling, permanently borrowing food. And weekends — campus on a Sunday made this place look quiet. Pickup basketball until dark. Games until sunrise. Sleeping until noon was a moral duty. And when the herd got hungry, it migrated off-campus in search of food, as one.”

Sulli listened the way children listen to fairy tales, and the light in her eyes climbed another notch.

“It sounds wonderful. A whole weekend that belongs completely to you. I’ve never once had a Sunday that… relaxed.”

“Then put a university on the list, and go claim one.”

Her head came up. “Could I really?”

And then it dipped, the brightness dimming at the edges.

“But… my schedule is packed solid. There isn’t room in a week for school…” A beat, quieter. “…I’ll try, though. Oppa.”

He heard the doubt sitting underneath it. His gaze went out to the lawn — the joggers, the loose ball on the far court — and he let a moment pass before answering.

“You can. I’d bet on it. Is there a school you dream about?”

She flinched, just slightly, as if a finger had touched a bruise she’d been keeping out of sight. It took a long while for the answer to come, and when it did, it came soft.

“…There is. I started acting when I was little. And even now, being an idol — what I want, more than anything, is to become a real actor. A good one.”

“An actor?”

He nodded slowly to himself. He knew that about her. She’d entered the industry through Ballad of Seodong, playing the young Princess Seon-hwa — and then she’d kept growing, taller and taller, until child roles closed behind her and the industry rerouted her into idolhood.

“Mm.” The nod came hard, and her eyes were doing something new — clearing, steadying, focusing somewhere far past the lawn. “I want roles that settle into people’s hearts and never leave. I want to stand in front of a camera and tell someone else’s story properly, all the way to the end. That’s why I want a real university — a good one, with a real acting program. I want to learn it from the ground up. And I want…” her voice dipped, almost shy now, “…the campus part too. The parts that would belong to me.”

She looked up, and the last of it came out careful — like she was handing him something made of glass.

“Oppa… do you think it’s still possible for me? Being an idol, and studying, and testing into a university — am I being ridiculous?”
