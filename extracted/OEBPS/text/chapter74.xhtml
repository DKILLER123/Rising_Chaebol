Chapter Seventy-Four

What, Too Scared to Meet the In-Laws?

FRIDAY AFTERNOON · HALF PAST THREE Mapo, Seoul the riverside road west — his parents’ house

At three-thirty, Song Ji-yeon took the river road west with the afternoon light sliding across the hood, bound for his parents’ place in Mapo.

Rush hour hadn’t woken up yet. The drive ran smooth, and soon the city traded towers for a quiet residential lane, and the lane ended at a detached house with a small courtyard — where he parked, pushed through the gate, and let himself in with the entitlement of an only child.

In the courtyard, Song Min-seok was folded into a recliner with a newspaper and reading glasses. On the stone table beside him: a squat clay tea set and a saucer of peanuts. The overall posture of the man could only be described as thriving.

“Dad.”

Song Ji-yeon took in the scene — newspaper, tea, peanuts, the full retirement festival — and felt his resolution harden. Forty-something was the age a man should be charging, not marinating. Letting a man waste his prime years in a lounge chair would be practically unethical.

It was not that he couldn’t stand seeing his father comfortable. It was not that at all.

Song Min-seok heard his son, lifted his head, lowered the newspaper with ceremony, and smiled.

“You’re here. Your mother’s holding down the kitchen.”

Their relationship ran on the classic first-generation blueprint. A father and son who loved each other at a respectful distance, while the mother ran the entire emotional switchboard. Son comes home — first protocol: route him to the mother.

Song Ji-yeon smiled, ignored protocol, and dropped into the recliner opposite. He reached over, poured himself a cup from his father’s pot, and drank. Oolong — his father’s religion. To a twenty-two-year-old’s tongue it tasted approximately like a health regimen. He was not a convert.

Song Min-seok observed his son settling in across from him instead of going to find Kang Seol-ah, and understood immediately that business had arrived. He gave a small rueful breath, lay back, and raised the newspaper until it covered half his face like a shield.

“Out with it. What do you need from me.”

Song Ji-yeon nodded, quietly impressed. Like father, like son — he so much as cleared his throat and the old man already had the whole speech.

“Dad, I want to run something by you.” He grinned, set the cup down, and went straight at it. “SAVORY F&B is stable — more than stable. I want to hand the hotpot business to you. Full keys.”

“I’m founding an entertainment company. Going forward, most of my energy goes there.”

“Entertainment?” The newspaper came down an inch; the brow above it creased. Song Min-seok sipped his tea. “The restaurant business is doing fine. Why entertainment, all of a sudden? That industry runs deep — black water. Have you actually studied it?”

“I have.” A nod, and the patient version of the answer. “But the ceiling over there is higher. The stores are on rails now — seven doors, all company-run, all humming. With you holding the wheel, I stop worrying entirely. You’ve spent half a life in kitchens, Dad. You’d steer it better than I do.”

And while he was opening a company anyway, there was a private little appendix to the plan. He carried a decade-plus of memories from his last life; he couldn’t recite every girl group the future would produce, but the giants he knew by heart — the divine visual Bae Joo-hyun, bunny-toothed Im Na-yeon, Chou Tzuyu, Lisa, Kim Ji-soo, Song Yu-qi, Yoo Ji-min, Jang Won-young. Names the whole industry would one day orbit.

Open a label, run the trainee system properly, and snipe a few of those at the draft. Even if the Goddess Handbook never bound a single one of them, the fame alone would pay for the building. Downside: difficult to locate.

Song Min-seok didn’t answer right away. He peeled a peanut, tossed it into his mouth, and chewed it like the matter deserved a full audit. His gaze drifted out to the persimmon trees along the courtyard wall, where the afternoon wind was moving the leaves.

The courtyard held its quiet. Nothing in it but leaves.

When the audit finished, the verdict came out level.

“You’ve thought it through. Then go do it.”

No drama anywhere in the voice — but then, Song Min-seok had never needed drama to say he was proud. The son had taken SAVORY F&B off his hands and run it redder than the old man ever had; the accounts knew it, the staff knew it, the father knew it. If the boy now wanted to point that engine at a different mountain, the father’s vote was already in.

The only genuine casualty: the recliner. Its full-time occupant was about to be recalled to active duty.

“Thanks, Dad.” The corner of Song Ji-yeon’s mouth ticked up. He’d known how this would end — SAVORY was the house his father had built with his own hands, and a man who builds a house knows exactly when it’s in good hands.

Father and son sat a while longer in the courtyard, sharing the wind and the peanuts. Then Song Ji-yeon went inside to find his mother.

Through the living room, straight to the kitchen. Kang Seol-ah stood at the stove in her apron, mid-battle; she heard the footsteps and turned, and the moment she registered her son, her whole face filled up with smile.

“You’re back.”

He nodded, came up beside her, and leaned over the big wok. A wall of meat-scent hit him square in the face.

“What is it, Mom?”

She turned back to the stove, and the smile went one degree deeper, into the territory of open smugness.

“Your favorite. Braised ribs.”

“Smells incredible.” He was already reaching for the lid.

“Ai-ai-ai — hands.” She slapped his away without turning around. “Not ready. Shoo. The fumes in here will pickle you. Go watch television; dinner’s a while off.”

He delivered an obedient oh, retired from the kitchen with his honor intact, and settled onto the living-room sofa, remote in hand.

The screen came up on a variety show. SBS’s Running Man — Episode 113, the Chuseok special, the ddakji tournament. He’d seen this one in his last life, and it had stuck: Yoo Jae-suk and Lee Kwang-soo waging full psychological warfare over the final paper tile, an hour of beautifully engineered stupidity. Idle anyway. A rerun with this cast was a fine way to spend it.

On screen, Haha was at it again — herding Kim Jong-kook and the guest Yeom Jung-ah into the same frame, determined to make love happen before the next commercial break. The man was, and had always been, Kim Jong-kook’s most devoted wingman. Song Ji-yeon laughed out loud.

The national variety show, red-hot for over a decade and counting. Say what you wanted — these MCs could detonate an episode.

Which was when the phone buzzed on the coffee table.

He picked it up. Ham Eun-jung. A KakaoTalk message.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Ham Eun-jung

Sajangnim scrapped the sub-unit plan.

The smile drained out of his face, slow, like color out of a photograph.

He hadn’t expected his interference to bend the track this far, this early.

On the original history, next year T-ara would debut two sub-units. T-ara N4 — Eun-jung, Hyo-min, Ji-yeon, plus Lee Ah-reum — pointed at the home market with a hip-hop concept, a Hail Mary at the domestic slide. And T-ara QBS — Bo-ram, Qri, So-yeon — aimed at Japan. Neither one had saved anything; the slide had kept sliding. But they had been the company’s flailing in the dark, and the flailing at least proved the body was alive.

Now both of them had simply… never happened. Quietly erased off the whiteboard.

So Kim Kwang-soo was done. Truly, finally done with T-ara — the man had looked at the cost of keeping them and chosen to spend nothing. Why fund sub-units for a group with no domestic future when you could sell the overseas name while it still carried a price, and recover the cash? The arithmetic of a man cutting a losses list.

Which suited Song Ji-yeon beautifully. The more urgently Kwang-soo wanted the weight gone, the smoother Kim Min-hyun’s glide path. His guess: the old fox would come calling on his own, soon, to talk about transferring T-ara’s management contracts. At which point all that remained was a signature — and T-ara was his.

He typed.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Song Ji-yeon

Good. Rest properly for a few months, all of you. Once I’ve bought you, I’ll put you back to work.

The reply landed before the phone was warm again.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Ham Eun-jung

Have you eaten? Want to get dinner?

He read it, glanced toward the kitchen — where something was sizzling with intent — and stood and walked over to the dining area.

The wide rosewood table was already three dishes deep. Steamed sea bass. Stir-fried shrimp with celery. Chilled jellyfish salad. And alongside, fresh from the pot, a tureen of corn-and-pork-hock soup, still steaming like it had somewhere to be.

All of it the food he’d grown up on — the table as it had looked his whole childhood, in one apartment after another, in one country and then another.

He took out his phone, photographed the whole spread, and sent it.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Song Ji-yeon

[Photo: a rosewood table under warm light — sea bass, shrimp, jellyfish salad, soup still steaming.] I’m at my parents’ today. Eating here.

✦ ✦ ✦

THE SAME EVENING, EARLY Apgujeong-dong, Seoul Ham Eun-jung’s place — the sofa

Eun-jung was home from the company, still in the plain tee and jeans she’d walked out in that morning, sunk so deep into the sofa that the sofa had opinions about it.

Mushroom lay against her leg, head pillowed on her arm, asleep at maximum depth. One of her hands moved through his fur on autopilot. The other held the phone.

When the photo opened, she swallowed. Audibly. Alone, in her own living room, to no witnesses.

It was a good photo. Warm light, and every dish sitting there in full color — she could all but hear the soup. She could picture his mother at that stove; she could feel the temperature of a kitchen like that from across the city. It was the exact flavor of home that a girl who had lived in dorms since fifteen could identify blind.

KakaoTalk · Song Ji-yeon

Ham Eun-jung

Wow… your mom’s cooking looks amazing. That all looks so good.

The reply came back fast — and she could hear the grin in it before she’d even finished reading.

KakaoTalk · Song Ji-yeon

Song Ji-yeon

If you want a taste, I’ll come pick you up.

Her cheeks went hot instantly, all the way to the ears.

Go… go to his house?

Meet the parents??

They had made it official yesterday. Meeting the parents today — was that not, by any calendar in the civilized world, catastrophically fast?!

The thoughts arrived in a stampede. What would she even say. What would she wear — this? Not this. Should she bring a gift? Fruit? A hanwoo gift set? Would his mother like her? Would they look at her — an idol, a girl currently passing through the worst press year of her life — and quietly think she wasn’t enough for their son?

Questions carousel-horsed through her head, round and round, each one worse saddled than the last. She scratched her hair, groaned, and rolled a full lap across the sofa — dislodging Mushroom, who lifted his head with the affronted innocence of the wrongly evicted.

“Woof?”

She looked into those clear, stupid, blameless eyes, and the dilemma did not resolve.

To go, or not to go. That was, in fact, the question.

Going felt reckless — she had prepared for exactly none of this. Not going felt like… a small ache. Because that table genuinely looked delicious, and somewhere under the hunger there was a worse hunger with no polite name.

She weighed it. Weighed it again. And in the end, reason outvoted appetite.

Not yet. We only just started — nothing’s settled. Meet the parents this soon, and if later… if there is no later… the table would be unbearably awkward. For everyone. Forever.

She took a breath and chose each word like it was going into a contract.

KakaoTalk · Song Ji-yeon

Ham Eun-jung

Better not… today’s too sudden, I haven’t prepared anything at all. Next time — next time I’ll come say hello to your mom and dad properly.

A graceful answer, she judged. It expressed willingness. It declined politely. Textbook.

She sent it, dropped the phone somewhere in the sofa’s upholstery, and went flat, exhaling at the ceiling like a woman who had just closed a hostile acquisition.

On the other end, Song Ji-yeon watched the refusal finally arrive, and the corner of his mouth curved up on its own authority.

From “want to get dinner?” to this — the visible deliberation, the long pause, the careful two-step wording. With his eyes closed he could have written her internal monologue himself: too fast. Not prepared. What if his parents don’t like me. What if there’s no later.

Women. Always living ten scenes ahead of the episode.

He looked at the polite little refusal, shook his head, and typed quickly — in English, because it was funnier in English.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Song Ji-yeon

What, the ugly bride is too scared to meet her in-laws?

Eun-jung had logged real hours on her English — the group had overseas schedules, and at overseas press events it was usually her and So-yeon doing the talking, so she’d put the work in. Fluency? No. But bride and in-laws, those two words she knew.

She was lying on the sofa, one hand halfway down Mushroom’s back, when the message landed — and she shot upright like a cat whose tail had been stood on.

Mushroom, evicted twice in one evening, bailed off the sofa entirely with a wounded woof.

“Aish!! I’ll have you know I am VERY pretty!! I am more than enough for you, Song Ji-yeon!!”

She typed it through gritted teeth, cheeks at full combustion — every scrap of this afternoon’s dilemma and shyness instantly converting to fuel-grade indignation.

Over in Mapo, Song Ji-yeon watched I am more than enough for you, Song Ji-yeon!! detonate across his screen, and laughed before he could stop it. Out loud. In his parents’ living room.

Because it was so completely her. No ceremony, no idol packaging, no cushioning — just the direct hit, like a small feral cat taking one honest swipe. And it struck him, holding the phone, how much more real she was now than the version he’d first met. The stage leader had always been courteous, composed, correct — a woman behind glass. This one blushed, talked tough, swore at him when provoked, whined when hungry, detonated over nothing.

Closer and closer to the girl in his memory.

That was what a relationship changed, apparently. He liked the new edition. Verdict: keep.

Smiling, he typed slowly.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Song Ji-yeon

Yes. More than enough for me.

Eun-jung read that line four times. Her face, already red, renegotiated upward. Something sweet and something mortifying arrived at the same time and staged a tug-of-war in her chest, and for a long moment she genuinely did not have a reply in stock.

She hugged the phone, rolled two complete laps of the sofa, and finally surrendered to the smile, and answered with full formality.

KakaoTalk · Song Ji-yeon

Ham Eun-jung

Hmph. Good that you know.

Another suppressed laugh, his side. They batted a few more rounds back and forth — pure nutrition-free nonsense, the exact conversational equivalent of holding hands — every line of it steeped in something neither of them was going to name first.

Until he finally lowered the phone, looked up — and discovered Kang Seol-ah standing behind the kitchen’s glass sliding door.

Apron on. Spatula in hand. Pressed to the glass with the wide, knowing grin of a neighborhood auntie at a wedding, and a light in her eyes that traveled straight down his spine and raised the blinds.

Song Ji-yeon’s entire body locked up mid-motion.

He touched his nose. There was no dignified way to put it: this was the exact posture of a high schooler caught mid-puppy-love by his parents — fully seen through, nowhere in the apartment to hide.

But where her son was quietly dying, Kang Seol-ah was quietly thriving.

A lifetime at the stove. A lifetime running the household. Years upon years of waiting for that boy to wake up and notice that life contained other humans. And now — now! — the family pig, raised for twenty-two years on a strict diet of work and money, snout buried in ledgers since childhood, had finally, finally learned to root up a cabbage.

In translation: her son had a girlfriend.

The gratification in her chest was total. Her mouth stretched practically ear to ear. She winked at her frozen son — one full, unhurried, Mom-knows-everything wink — and then, deeply satisfied, turned back to her stove.

Song Ji-yeon looked at his mother’s delighted back, and didn’t know whether to laugh or file a complaint.

This goofball of a mother. Honestly.

He shook his head, helpless — and underneath the helplessness, warmth rose in him without asking permission.

Dinner was the three of them at the table, nothing grander. Braised ribs, steamed sea bass, shrimp with celery, jellyfish salad, the pork-hock soup — the rosewood surface fully occupied. Kang Seol-ah loaded her son’s bowl at an industrial rate — and, for the first time in recorded history, raised no campaign platform about marriage whatsoever.

She had done the math already. A son who has learned to root up cabbages will, sooner or later, bring the cabbage home. She was a modern, enlightened woman; she would not interrogate the boy and become a nuisance. She would simply wait, patiently, for the cabbage to present itself at the front door.

What Kang Seol-ah could not possibly have modeled, waiting there by her stove: her son was not bringing home one cabbage. Her son was assembling a produce section.

Song Ji-yeon put a braised rib in his mouth — the glaze giving way, the meat sliding off the bone, exactly the flavor on file from childhood — and watched his mother across the table bickering with his father over something of no consequence whatsoever, and the last taut string somewhere in his chest quietly went slack.

After dinner the three of them sat in the living room a while, talking about nothing. Past nine, Song Ji-yeon took his phone and made to leave. Kang Seol-ah walked him to the gate, issued the standard safe-driving directives, and closed the door.

Engine on. Headlights came up in the dark, and the car pulled out of the quiet Mapo lane, pointing itself back across the river — toward Cheongdam-dong, and his own bed.
