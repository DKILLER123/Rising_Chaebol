Chapter One Hundred Eleven

Han So-hee, In for a Penny, In for a Pound

TUESDAY EVENING Cheongdam-dong — the Sajangnim’s apartment The kitchen doorway, and the two seconds after

Song Ji-yeon stood frozen for exactly two seconds before the picture finished loading.

And then it all came in at once — why she had gone cold on him from the very start.

From the moment at ONGI when he’d said I’ll sign you, this girl had been reading an entirely different document. It explained the contract signed without a single page turned. It explained the corridor bench at four-thirty — not a lost employee, a pickup. It explained the semi-basement, the “pack your things” taken without one question, the whole obedience of the afternoon.

And it explains the shower. Oh, it thoroughly explains the shower.

She hadn’t been seeing a patron of the arts. She’d been seeing a transaction — and she’d been bracing for the collections department since the second floor of her own restaurant.

Have I got this right? ‘Pack your things’ — what else was that ever going to read as? And after that — what did she think the evening was for? Unbelievable. She couldn’t have asked one more question?

He wanted to laugh and didn’t quite manage it. He was, by his own accounting, no saint — Eun-jung existed; Sulli and Ji-yeon orbited him in ambiguous poetry; the ledger was not clean. But there was a bright line between morally overdrawn and this, and he had never once in either life crossed it by force.

Besides — the arithmetic was fatal. He needed this actress healthy, thriving, and eventually bound to the Goddess Handbook. The Handbook’s binding condition was a genuine flutter of the heart — and nothing on this earth curdles a heart faster than duress. Touch her wrong tonight, and wasn’t the whole plan simply dead? What woman falls for the man who leveraged her? Unless her taste runs to precisely that — and nothing in this girl’s file suggests it.

Snow didn’t land on charcoal in this house. What he’d meant as rescue had been priced, in her ledger, as a debt — and collected at the door.

“Don’t move.”

He dragged his eyes off the ceiling, crossed in three strides, bent, and picked the towel up off the floor. Snapped it open. Wrapped it back around her himself.

Han So-hee flinched a half-step back — and his hand landed on her shoulder and ended the discussion.

“Stand still. No wriggling.”

“I can do it myse—”

“You can do what? You were plenty decisive a minute ago.”

He cinched the knot at her collarbone himself, tested it with two fingers, and only then let out the breath he’d been rationing.

Then he raised one hand and rapped her on the crown, none too gently.

“Ow!”

She clapped her palm over the spot, eyes gone perfectly round.

“What was that for?!”

“That’s my line.” He glared at her, exasperated straight through. “What exactly is stocked in that head of yours? Name three items.”

“Stocked with what? What did I say?”

Her mouth twisted sideways. What is this performance? We’re past pretending, aren’t we?

“What do you think?”

“Isn’t it exactly what you meant? Fine — go ahead.”

“What I meant—?”

Her hands went to the knot again, because she was done negotiating with the evening. It’s happening tonight either way — drag it out, why.

The apartment he’d driven her to. The bathroom he’d pointed at, permission implicit. The shower nobody had stopped. Every step of it read as preamble, and she had read it that way since the car — and he was still wearing that blank innocent face now, so who exactly was the show for?

Signed meant sold. Sold meant delivered without whimpering. The worst case had already been budgeted; the audit was over.

His hand closed over hers before the knot slipped twice.

“Question.” He held her wrist level, voice flat. “Do you think I signed you because I fancied you?”

No answer. None needed — the answer ran across her face in high resolution.

He sighed, caught between anger and the ridiculous, and let go of her wrist.

“…It really is? Unbelievable.”

“What else was I supposed to think? Why sign me at all — and then ‘pack your things and come home with me’?”

She smiled, and the smile had a draft running through it.

“I’d have loved to be wrong. But you followed me home. What company boss inspects an employee’s basement? And then — ‘pack your things.’ And then here. Every signpost on that road pointed one direction, and I followed the signs.”

He looked at her — really looked — stepped back half a pace, and put the width of the hallway between them like a notarized document.

“Han So-hee. What kind of man do you take me for?”

Her head dropped. Both hands held the towel’s edge at her collarbone, and she offered nothing.

“Did you decide I’m the kind who leans on the payroll to corner his own artist?”

He shook his head, half a laugh escaping at the sheer architecture of the misunderstanding.

“If that’s what I wanted, do you think I’d need a contract to get it? I have tools. The contract is not the tool.”

She kept her lips pressed together and stood her ground.

“What I want is your face and the actress inside it. Plainly, then. I’m a merchant. The agency exists to turn a profit. My read is that you become famous — very — and that fame compounds on my balance sheet. That’s why I signed you.”

“…Just to make money?” Quietly, suspicion still load-bearing.

“What else — charity?” He swept a hand at the apartment around them. “You’re here because that basement was unfit for anything with lungs. Damp, dark, two seasons of mold in the walls. You are now my company’s actress — I’m hardly going to leave my own actress living in that, am I?”

Her head lowered further.

“Tonight is one night — a spare room, a locked door, and tomorrow morning, a real apartment, on the company. That’s the entire plan. The bathroom — I pointed you to the bathroom because I assumed hands, face, ordinary plumbing. Who jumps from that straight into a full shower? Who scripts this, So-hee? Who reads a house tour and hears a countdown?”

The scald reached her ear-tips in one pulse — and then kept going, down her neck, into territory she couldn’t cover with both hands.

It finished arriving, all of it, at once. There was no arrangement. There had never been one. No transaction, no rules, no collector — nothing but her own imagination, running unsupervised since a corridor in ONGI Cheongdam, drafting clauses nobody wrote, and then presenting them, tonight, in person, in a towel.

I thought I was bowing to fate with dignity. In his account of the evening, I’m a lunatic who unpacked her own doom and handed it over gift-wrapped.

But there was no refund lane either. She couldn’t price the damage — whether he’d now file her under mad, or trouble, or move to void the contract entirely.

And the contract was the one door with anything behind it.

A grandmother in Ulsan still watched a blizzard of static every night, wishing one wish at it.

Miss this window, and the next one doesn’t publish a date.

So — in for a penny. He’s rich, he’s not hard to look at, and I’ve already paid the entry fee twice over. On the ledger as it stands… I’m not even losing.

Regret was for people with inventory left.

She closed the distance in one step, went up on her toes, and kissed him — mouth to mouth, hands fisted in his shirt, the whole nineteen-year-old hedgehog thrown at once.

One does not simply retreat from a misunderstanding of this magnitude. One doubles the position.

He was caught flat — and did not stop it. Because in the instant their lips met, the Goddess Handbook shivered once in his mind, like a struck bell.

Bound. Fifth goddess — bound. And I don’t even need the report to know why.

Gulp.

The kiss ended. Her face was a disaster of color, and she kept it up anyway, chin level, daring the room to comment.

“You’re insane.” He was almost admiring. “You understand what this step costs you?”

She said nothing at all — only nodded, once, shy in a way that had no legal relationship with anything she’d done in the last hour.

Hah—

He exhaled, long. He was not, whatever the office gossip would someday say, carved from willow wood. The lady had filed her position in triplicate. Remaining a saint past this point would be disrespectful to her paperwork.

He moved first—

— and the front door chimed.

Beep-beep-beep—beep.

The lock popped, crisp as a knuckle.

“Oppa! We’re at the company for practice tomorrow — are you coming to watch?!”

Park Ji-yeon’s voice reached the kitchen before Park Ji-yeon did.

She’d heard the kitchen noise from upstairs — identified the apartment as occupied and cooking — and descended on the spot, faithful to the sacred custom of the meal-buddy: where there is dinner, there is me.

OFF-DUTY · UPSTAIRS DROP-IN “THE SNACK HAUL”

The nation’s little dragon, off duty and off guard — arriving at the neighbor’s door like it’s her second pantry.

Set — a loose black athletic two-piece, zip jacket half-hearted about its zipping, track pants pooling at the ankle

Hair — one lazy low tie, strands defecting at the temples, zero diplomatic effort

Carry — a plastic convenience-store bag at full stretch: chip packets, soda bottles, the entire food pyramid of a nineteen-year-old

Footwear — house slippers, swapped at the door without breaking stride — the tell of a standing invitation

Face — bare, bright, and pre-flavored with the confidence of someone who already knows what’s cooking

The uniform of a woman who has raided this kitchen before, intends to raid it again, and recognizes no jurisdiction over the pantry

Door code: issued by the chef himself. An error of hospitality he would relive, at length, for the rest of the evening.

She came through the entry mid-sentence, slippers already on, bag swinging.

“I could smell it from upstairs — I knew you had to be cooking someth— …some… thing…”

The sentence died in installments.

On the strip of floor between the living room and the kitchen stood two people.

The man: aproned, backed up against the island counter, hands formally nowhere, wearing the expression of a host who has just remembered he left the stove on.

And across from him — a young woman, unmistakably fresh from a bath, hair still dripping dark onto her shoulders, the white towel doing towel’s-best, both hands strangling the fabric at her chest, face lit up like a festival.

The distance between them: a few steps. A few steps that were only that few because the man had clocked the door opening and manufactured them on the spot.

Park Ji-yeon’s whole body seized. The snack bag slid out of her grip.

Thwap.

It hit the floor and burst its seams politely; three bags of chips rolled out and orbited a slipper.

The air went glassy.

Song Ji-yeon pressed a hand to his forehead. The evening was already a fire. Now the fire department has arrived — by keycard. I gave the dragon my door code. There’s the mistake, right there, engraved in brass.

Han So-hee had jolted too — a full-system start. A woman. At his home. With her own door code. The girlfriend, then?

So: a rotten man after all. Girlfriend upstairs, mistresses downstairs, and the sermon he just delivered — ‘what kind of man do you take me for’ — delivered while the audience was on its way down. And notice: the kiss didn’t get pushed away, but the second the door beeps, he teleports three steps. Fastest feet in Cheongdam.

…And if that’s the girlfriend — doesn’t that make me the other woman? The role is cast. And occupied. By me. Currently in costume.

She scanned the living room for cover and found it had been designed by an optimist — all open plan and light. She stood very still and let the chips orbit.

Park Ji-yeon rebooted eventually, in stages, and recovered her voice before her face did.

“Oppa… who is this?”

She pointed at the towel with a finger full of pure confusion — no heat in it, none anywhere; a detective, not a wronged party, and visibly out of clues.

His scalp prickled. He got as far as drawing breath — and she was already orbiting the two of them, a full slow lap, eyes narrowed at the display like a customs officer at a suspiciously light suitcase.

“No, hang on. Oppa. Why is there another woman in your apartment — again? Last time it was Sulli, and now this one — and she’s — she’s fresh out of the shower, oppa? In a towel? In your kitchen?”

The confusion deepened into its own gravity well. She leaned in, dropped to a stage whisper that carried perfectly across the room, wearing the face of a woman trading state secrets.

“How many of you are there? How many beautiful women does this apartment hide?”

“What am I hiding? Nothing. This is the company’s new artist — Han So-hee. Your colleague, as of this afternoon.” He kneaded his brow. “So-hee — Park Ji-yeon, of T-ara. Your senior in the industry. Also my upstairs neighbor, when the mood takes her.”

A NOTE ON THE HOUSING · WHO GETS A COMPANY ROOF

Idols are livestock, institutionally speaking: trainees bunk three and four to a dorm, debut groups inherit the dorm, and the agency holds the lease, the utilities, and therefore the schedule. Home, for an idol, is a franchise location.

Actors are freelancers. Rookie actors doubly so — no dorm, no driver, no floor of the building with their name on it. The company that houses a brand-new actress at all is already an outlier; the question ‘why isn’t she in the company dorm’ is a fair one, and the honest answer is: there isn’t one, because until this afternoon the company’s entire acting roster was zero people.

“An artist?” The little dragon’s eyes narrowed to coins. Somewhere in her head, an abacus began moving beads on its own. “Why is an artist at your home? Showering? Shouldn’t she be at the dorm — or a company apartment — or, I don’t know, her own address?”

“Long story. Sit down. I’ll tell it once.”

So he told it — the short honest version. The Sinsa-dong semi-basement, damp and dark and unfit for anything with a career. The hour too late for realtors. One night, spare room, apartment hunting tomorrow. And one stretch of the truth worn loosely — out with me all day in the heat, wanted a shower and clean clothes — draped tactfully over the parts of the evening that were nobody’s business. The kiss got a full omertà. Some historical documents are sealed by treaty.

Park Ji-yeon listened all the way to the end. Then she walked a slow second lap around Han So-hee, itemizing.

Beautiful — undeniably; cold-featured and thin-lit, like a hanok in winter. Currently doing a flawless impression of a startled heron, ears incandescent, hands strangling terry cloth. Nobody’s idea of a black widow.

And yet the abacus kept clacking. But would a normal person shower at the Sajangnim’s house? I would not. Nobody I know would. The math on this girl doesn’t close.

“So. New actress. Nowhere to live. Your conscience did the charity and kept her one night?” She squinted, wanting to be wrong, checking twice.

“Every word true. The contract was signed at the office this afternoon — Seon-young witnessed it, Legal filed it, the ink has colleagues.”

The little dragon tipped her head and let the story settle.

Strictly speaking, this was none of her business, and she knew it. A neighbor — upstairs, downstairs, sharing a ceiling and a food chain — held no title, no vote, no standing to cross-examine a single thing he did with his evenings.

And still, somewhere under the sternum, an objection was being filed without her signature.

First Sulli. Now this one. And the whole gray territory with Eun-jung-unnie besides. Why is the airspace around that man a parade of gorgeous women.

Sulli, at least, was a known quantity — a peach with a meal ticket, harmless. But this one had walked in the door half-dressed and installed herself in the kitchen before the door closed.

This one gets managed.

Eun-jung-unnie was the best unnie she had. And unnie’s man — whatever the two of them thought they were or weren’t — fell, tonight, into the category of assets requiring protection. As for herself… well. Eun-jung-unnie had never once minded her and oppa being like this. The arrangement had always been generous to all parties. In her heart, the marriage of Eun-jung-unnie and oppa was already scheduled, catered, and assigned a dress code.

“Oppa — it’s not that I don’t believe you.” She put the objection into its formal dress. “It’s the two of you. One man, one woman, one apartment, one evening. If this leaks anywhere — the company’s name, the girl’s name — it’s a headline neither of you can afford.”

“So what’s your proposal?”

“Simple!” One clap, eyes alight with the joy of a well-built plan. “I live one floor up, and my apartment has more spare rooms than sense. She comes up with me — a woman’s place, a woman’s roommate, zero headlines. Wouldn’t that solve everything? You get your reputation back by morning.”

The little dragon’s philanthropy had a load-bearing ulterior motive, of course — she and Han So-hee had met under ninety seconds of circumstance and formed no bond whatsoever. The actual program was containment. One roof over the asset, her roof; one pair of eyes on the perimeter, hers. Two birds, one apartment.

He blinked — then nodded, once, slowly.

“That works.”

It genuinely works. I need the cooling-off period as much as anyone — that girl is a hazard to my composure, and the evening has already used up two of my nine lives.

He turned to her, voice gentling a register.

“So-hee. Ji-yeon’s right upstairs — best neighbor on the Peninsula, ask anyone I’ve fed. One night at hers, and tomorrow morning I take you apartment hunting myself. All right?”

Han So-hee was, at the molecular level, unwilling.

One kiss is not collateral. I need the whole document — full attachment, his signature, no early-exit clauses. This is half a position.

But she nodded anyway, because certain logistics are not conducted with an audience in the room.

And tomorrow he’s mine all afternoon — house hunting, alone, door to door. Rome was not annexed in one evening.
