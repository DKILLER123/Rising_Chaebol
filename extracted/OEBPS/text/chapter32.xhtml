Chapter Thirty-Two

We’re Really Just Friends

MONDAY Seoul T-ara’s dorm — the living room

“Unnie—”

“Was that serious? What you just said? Song Ji-yeon — hotpot guy — is really buying out our contracts? All of ours?”

Park Ji-yeon scrambled up off the carpet, abandoning an open bag of chips mid-handful, and attached herself to Eun-jung’s shoulder, eyes running at full luminosity.

“Yah! Volume!” Eun-jung’s ears were ringing from the shout; she slapped a hand over the maknae’s mouth and swept a guilty look around the rest of the living room.

When she’d come in last night, the others had already been asleep — and with the entire internet boycotting them, morning schedules weren’t exactly piling up. Waking them over this would have been a waste. Better to let them sleep and break it at breakfast.

She herself hadn’t slept properly. Naturally, that was Song Ji-yeon’s fault — his and the speech he’d made on her sofa.

She’d turned it over all night, and the thing still refused to come square. Why T-ara? She’d promised to be the first signature on the transfer agreement, and she’d meant it — but honest overnight arithmetic kept pulling her back the other way. The reason was blunt: T-ara, right now, printed nothing but losses. All that China talk — planting a flag in that market meant a fortune in upfront capital, and at the same budget the man had a hundred cleaner options than a walking controversy with a girl group attached. Why this one?

No answer came.

…Unless it’s me. Unless he’s actually — interested in me?

The thought landed, and her face went hot all by itself.

“Unnie. Earth to unnie. I asked you a question.” Park Ji-yeon watched the blush climb, grinned the grin of a cat that has located the cream, and jabbed one finger into the soft of Eun-jung’s waist. “Eun-jung-unnie isn’t thinking something naughty, is she? Look at that face. So~ shame~less~”

“Yah, Park Ji-yeon! I said he has the idea — that’s all I said.” She caught the offending finger mid-second-jab, caught red-handed and furious about it. “He asked me to come back and sound all of you out first. See where everyone stands.”

Park Ji-yeon wriggled free of the grip, eyes at full blaze, and bounced twice on the sofa cushions for emphasis.

“I’m in! Total, complete, one-hundred-percent in! As long as the six of us stay together, the logo on the company can be anybody’s. At CCM we get reamed out daily by that old man — at Song Ji-yeon’s place there’d be hotpot. Daily. On the house!”

Her grudge against CCM ran old and deep. Hwa-young — a rookie, air-dropped into a group that was already at altitude, handed outsized parts in every re-choreographed number, handed Eun-jung-unnie’s and Qri-unnie’s rap sections — and still couldn’t land them cleanly. Watching resources like those get incinerated had left a permanent mark.

Then came the bullying scandal, and the company’s silence in the weeks after — that was the part that made her blood genuinely boil. A door out of CCM? She’d vote yes with both hands, both feet, and the chair she was sitting on.

“Ji-yeon. That’s enough.” From the far side of the room, Park So-yeon’s brow creased one degree. She held the leader’s chair, which meant she carried arithmetic the maknae had never been asked to do. “Eun-jung. This — is Song Ji-yeon-ssi actually serious?”

She set down the script she’d been holding and leaned in, voice hedged. Twenty-five this year. She did not want to walk away from a stage she’d paid for in years and sweat — and even with the group’s name dragged through the mud, some stubborn part of her still believed they’d climb back out of it someday. Walking out of CCM now meant stepping off every map they had.

“CCM is an old-line agency under Mnet. Say whatever we want about how Kim Kwang-soo has handled the last month — the company’s channels and relationships, here and overseas, are real. They exist. Song Ji-yeon-ssi runs restaurants. If he signs us, what comes after the signature? Where we’re standing isn’t a problem one or two hotpot dinners can solve. Right now there’s a verbal promise, and no plan, no proposal, nothing on paper. To me this feels like far too much of a gamble.”

“So-yeon-unnie has a point.” Lee Qri, wall-leaning and nail-polishing, had stopped doing both.

On a face that ranked with the country’s best, very little moved. What did move was in the eyes — cool, level, openly doubtful. But days of watching Eun-jung orbit the dorm glowing, spacing out, checking her phone mid-sentence — a woman like Qri could read a chart off that. She asked, already half resigned:

“Eun-jung. He genuinely isn’t your boyfriend?”

“Qri-unnie — where does that even come from! He and I are really just friends.” The flush detonated; her hands and feet ran suddenly out of places to be.

Qri read the answer off everything Eun-jung wasn’t managing to hold still. Not a couple, no. But this was the season right before a couple — the ambiguous part, the part where everyone involved keeps saying the word “friends” like it’s a fence that will hold.

And then she took the thought one step further, and the step went somewhere uncomfortable.

A man who shows up alone at a black ocean holding your light-board, and now moves to buy six contracts — an actual girlfriend might not collect treatment at this level of thorough.

Her impression of Song Ji-yeon, up to now: perfectly pleasant. Probably — a colder voice noted — because none of the tripwires had been stepped on yet. Well. They were getting stepped on now. Idols in this country got burned badly in contract transfers; there were precedents, plural, and they all ended the same way.

“Eun-jung. How long have you actually known this man? A dozen-odd days — call it two weeks?” Qri tilted her head, and the coolness in her voice stopped pretending to be anything else. Not a boyfriend — then this was free fire. “A person you’ve known two weeks offers, out of nowhere, to spend billions of won acquiring six ‘hot potatoes.’ What’s the angle? Business is business — there is no business case for signing us right now. So which is it: something he wants from you? Or something he wants from all of us?”

“Song Ji-yeon is not that kind of person!” It was out before she’d chosen to say it. “At Jeju, he stood up alone — one person in that whole hall, under every cold stare in the building — and held our yellow light-board over his head. If he were after something, who does that? Who goes that far?”

“And if that’s the trick? If that’s exactly how trust gets built?” Qri didn’t give back an inch of ground, and the probe went in clean. “A man’s intentions are never as simple as the version you’re holding, Eun-jung.”

Her own arithmetic was short and pitiless: nobody — nobody — takes on six radioactive contracts for zero gain. This wasn’t charity; charity at least buys a reputation. Buy T-ara now, and the internet’s verdict writes itself: rich fool, money to burn.

“Qri-unnie, how can you say that!” The maknae was openly offended now, cheeks rounded into a pout. “Boss Song is a good guy! And he’s handsome! And he’s got guts — everyone else in this city is running away from us at full sprint, and he’s the one person on the peninsula trying to sign us. I think unnie’s nerves are inventing villains.”

“Ji-yeon. Quiet.” So-yeon silenced the maknae with one look, then turned back. “Eun-jung — Qri’s worry isn’t baseless. We can’t absorb another fall. If we move to a new company with no roots, and the resources dry up behind us… that may genuinely be the end of it. We’d never get back to where we were.”

The living room hit deadlock and sat there.

Eun-jung looked at So-yeon’s hesitation, at Qri’s crossed arms. Something blocked up in her chest, painful and unswallowable — and she understood both of them anyway. Every word either of them had said was for the group.

“Then—” A long breath in. Her fingers had strangled the hem of her shirt into a rope.

“…we invite him out. All of us. Whatever we want to ask him — we ask him face to face.”
