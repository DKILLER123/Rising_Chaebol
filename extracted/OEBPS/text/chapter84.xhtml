Chapter Eighty-Four

The Plan in Motion

MONDAY · 8:00 A.M. Cheongdam-dong → Mapo-gu, Seoul SAVORY F&B headquarters

The alarm went off at eight sharp, and Song Ji-yeon’s hand found the phone on the nightstand by muscle memory.

The curtains were still drawn; the room sat in half-dark. He yawned himself upright against the headboard and let the fog clear slowly out of his skull.

He’d run the company ledger through his head after going to bed last night — money in, money out, money promised out — and the arithmetic had chased him around the mattress well past midnight.

He pressed the spot between his brows and checked the light leaking around the curtains. Full daylight already; the morning air of early-autumn Seoul carried that first cool hint of damp through the glass.

Up. Into the bathroom. A handful of cold water later, most of the sleep was gone.

Face washed, teeth brushed, he spent a moment studying the absurdly well-cut face in the mirror, all sharp jaw and unfair symmetry, and allowed himself one honest observation.

“Honestly? With this face, who even needs a system. I’d have thrived on looks alone.”

Tragically, I am a man with ambitions.

He moved on to the closet and pulled one of the two suits. Both were bespoke — his mother, Kang Seol-ah, had marched him to the tailor herself — immaculately made, and worn exclusively for the office and formal occasions, because no well-adjusted human being strolls around in a full suit on a daily basis. In a decade’s time, a man dressed like this on a normal street would be mistaken for an insurance salesman.

Tie set, he scooped the car keys and left.

In the garage, the black Mercedes woke with a low hum and folded into the morning traffic.

Seoul wasn’t properly awake yet — light scattered traffic, storefronts mostly still shuttered, a handful of early commuters crossing streets with coffee welded to their hands.

He drove and, in parallel, reviewed the day.

Today’s weekly meeting was, frankly, a formality. SAVORY F&B had always been his father’s machine; the six department heads were the old man’s loyal veterans, and it wasn’t as if they needed re-introducing. His father had never actually let go of the company’s pulse, either — even from a distance he’d kept up with everything, down to the decimals.

There was nothing meaningful left to “hand over.”

What actually mattered were the two other items.

One: the registration of ShiningYouth Entertainment. If he wanted to take T-ara on board, the money and the legal qualifications both had to be sitting ready.

Two: the CCM negotiation — Kim Min-hyun’s side, facing down Kim Kwang-soo.

The sub-unit cancellation had reached him through Eun-jung. If Kwang-soo had shelved the T-ara N4 plan, then the man had already decided to sell the girls’ remaining contracts — the only open question was price, which was presumably why Min-hyun hadn’t summoned him to sign anything yet.

This week. Whatever it takes, the signing gets done this week.

Twenty-odd minutes later the car rolled into Mapo and stopped in front of SAVORY’s three-story building.

He cut the engine and straightened his collar in the mirror.

Early enough that the lobby held only a few staff and the receptionist, Lee Hye-jin, all having breakfast on the lounge sofas. At the sight of the door swinging open, they rose in a ragged chorus.

“Good morning, Sajangnim!”

“Morning.” He nodded back with an easy smile.

Being CEO hadn’t made him distant — on slow mornings he still parked himself in the break room with the staff and gossiped, which was how he knew half the company’s internal soap operas. Cheap entertainment, honestly.

“Hye-jin — Director Park and Director Kim in yet?”

If anyone knew who was where, it was the front desk; Lee Hye-jin was reliably among the first in the building.

“Director Kim is upstairs already. Director Park is still on his way — probably eight-fifty or so.”

Oh-ho. Old Park’s got the receptionist tracking his ETA now, does he. Someone’s been chatting up the front desk again.

Aloud, with a straight face: “Thanks. Finish your breakfast, everyone — I’ll head up.”

“Ne, Sajangnim!”

Since Park Seon-young wasn’t in yet, there was no point hunting down Kim Min-hyun now — he’d catch both men after the meeting. He climbed to his office, hung the suit jacket on the chair back, sat down at the desk, woke the computer, and logged into his brokerage account.

First thing on the screen: the Sewon Electronics candlestick chart.

Five straight trading days of climbing had planted the stock firmly above ₩19,500 — a gain pushing thirty-eight percent, matching the Stock Forecast Card’s projection to the decimal.

He checked the clock. Eight-forty. The weekly meeting was at nine; the market opened at nine.

The card said the stock had a little more room left in it — but he had no appetite for the last bite of any profit.

He set a scheduled sell order for nine o’clock sharp — the system would dump the entire Sewon position at open — then queued the same automated order on Sulli’s account, nine o’clock as well.

Done, he closed the trading screen, was halfway out of his chair for the meeting room — when the door swung open without a knock.

His father, Song Min-seok, stood in the frame in a dark suit, hair combed to precision. Seeing his son caught mid-rise behind the desk, the older man’s face eased into a smile.

“Dad? What are you doing up here?” Ji-yeon stood all the way, surprised. The company kept a chairman’s office for him — when Song Min-seok came in, that was where he planted himself.

“What, a father can’t come look at his son?”

“Of course he can.” Ji-yeon laughed, rounding the desk. “I just figured you’d go straight to the meeting room.”

“Time to spare. Came to see you first.” Song Min-seok settled onto the sofa and looked at his son with something quiet and satisfied in his eyes. “You’ve run the company well. This past stretch.”

He’d arrived early this morning and gone straight to the finance department before coming up, pulling the month-and-a-half’s actual revenue figures.

He’d known the hotpot conversion was a hit — in the abstract. He hadn’t asked for specifics. The abstract had not prepared him for the ledger: since the old restaurants had turned into ONGI hotpot houses, company revenue had more than doubled.

The pride in his chest had needed somewhere to go. So here he was.

“Dad, stop inflating me.” Ji-yeon sat down across from him, grinning. “The company runs because you built it. Without your seven stores and the veterans you left me, there’d be no ONGI to succeed with.”

Song Min-seok waved that off.

“Don’t be modest — everyone saw what you did with the place. The entertainment company you want to start…” A beat. “You want it, so do it. I won’t stand in the way. A young man should have plans of his own. From today, SAVORY comes back to me.”

Warmth moved through Ji-yeon’s chest, and he nodded.

“Thanks, Dad. I know.”

Song Min-seok set down his water and stood.

“Time. Let’s go — meeting room, together.”

✦ ✦ ✦

They walked in side by side. The six department heads were already seated; at the sight of chairman and president arriving as a pair, chairs scraped and backs straightened.

“Chairman-nim. Sajangnim.”

Ji-yeon nodded and pulled out the seat at the head of the table.

“Dad. Here.”

Song Min-seok glanced at his son, didn’t refuse, and took the head chair. Ji-yeon dropped into the first seat on his father’s right and cleared his throat.

“Two items today. The first, and the most important — as of today, my father resumes direct management of SAVORY F&B.”

A low ripple of murmurs ran the length of the table and settled.

Song Min-seok surveyed the room, smiling faintly.

“You’re all veterans of this company. Thank you for holding the line while I was away. I’ll be counting on you.”

The brief formality done, the meeting began in earnest — department heads reporting last week’s results and this week’s plans, one by one.

Ji-yeon listened quietly, interjecting a question here and there, but mostly ceded the floor to his father. It showed, plainly: even after his time away from the desk, the old man knew every bolt of this machine by name.

A little over an hour later, the meeting broke up.

As the room emptied, Ji-yeon caught two men by the sleeve — planning director Park Seon-young and legal director Kim Min-hyun.

“Director Park. The new company’s registration — where are we?”

Park Seon-young’s face assembled itself into maximum service-mode, body bowing into a comma.

“Sajangnim, rest assured — everything is on schedule. All documents are filed; we’re in the final approval stage. The business license should be in hand by tomorrow.”

“Slow is fine. Legal and compliant is not optional.”

“Understood!” Park slapped his own chest like a notarized seal. “Legal representative, capitalization, registered address — I’ve triple-checked every core field, and I’ve spoken with friends on the review side. By tomorrow afternoon at the latest, the license will be on your desk.”

ShiningYouth Entertainment was being registered as a subsidiary of SAVORY F&B — which meant that, capital aside, the machinery was simple.

“Good.” Ji-yeon’s attention moved to the man in the gold wire-rimmed glasses. “Director Kim. CCM. Where do we stand?”

Kim Min-hyun nudged his glasses up and spoke with courtroom calm.

“Sajangnim — as instructed, I contacted Kim Kwang-soo last week and sat down with him face-to-face.”

“And?”

“Kwang-soo has run out of patience with T-ara entirely.” The corner of Min-hyun’s mouth lifted a fraction. “The bullying-rumor storm shows no sign of passing. The group has zero bookings, and CCM is still paying their dorm and baseline expenses every month — bleeding out for the privilege. He said, in effect: whoever wants them, name a number, everything’s negotiable.”

“So what number did you land on?”

“I tabled a full buyout — the transfer fee for all six members’ remaining contracts, plus the associated termination-compensation clauses.” Min-hyun paused, adjusted his glasses again. “He didn’t refuse it on the spot. He asked for our number in writing. Yesterday afternoon he came back: the price is acceptable, but payment must clear in full at signing. No installments.”

“All at once.” Ji-yeon’s fingers stopped their idle drumming on the table. “His number?”

“After my counter-pressure — four billion won, all fees included.”

Four billion.

He ran the arithmetic in his head. The Sewon liquidation alone had just returned nearly four billion in profit; with the account’s existing balance, his deployable cash sat north of nine billion. This purchase barely made a dent.

And the number sat exactly inside his own limit.

“Acceptable.” He nodded once, decisive — then tilted his head. “Though if he’d already folded on price, you should have called me in to sign days ago. Why am I only hearing this now?”

Kim Min-hyun’s eyes flicked toward Park Seon-young; he chose his words carefully.

“Sajangnim, the CCM side is settled — but there is one structural problem we cannot walk around. The buyout of T-ara’s contracts must be executed by a corporate entity holding an entertainment-management license.”

“In plain terms: ShiningYouth has to be registered and licensed first. Only then can I put the buyout contract in front of Kim Kwang-soo under the new company’s name. Have SAVORY F&B sign it instead, and the agreement is legally defective from the first line.”

Understanding clicked into place.

So that’s it. Min-hyun wasn’t stalling me — he was waiting on Old Park’s license.

“Then the timelines line up. Park’s license lands tomorrow; Kim locks the signing date immediately after.”

“Ne, Sajangnim.” Kim Min-hyun inclined his head. “The moment the license is in my hands tomorrow, I call Kwang-soo the same day. We can be signing the day after, or the day after that.”

“Good.” Ji-yeon’s gaze swept the two of them. “I’ll wire capital into the new company’s account this afternoon so the funds are confirmed well before signing. Director Kim — give Kwang-soo a heads-up today. Our progress, our timeline. Let him prepare.”

“Understood, Sajangnim. I’ll fix the signing window with him this afternoon and pin the details once the license arrives.”

“Then that’s settled.” He stood and clapped each man on the shoulder. “T-ara doesn’t wait another week. The faster this closes, the better.”

The Handbook does not explain itself. It never has.

Samjin Group. The name rang a small bell — a Peninsula conglomerate, something in electronic components and display materials.

He didn’t think about it further. The system gave a result; his job was execution, not peer review.

He picked up his phone, opened his KakaoTalk thread with Eun-jung, and typed.

Song Ji-yeon

He read her reply and let it be. Phone down; back to the computer.

The account now held ₩9.1 billion. He moved ₩4.1 billion out to his bank, and within moments the text arrived from Seoul Bank: ₩4,100,000,000 received.

Four billion of it would buy T-ara — all six contracts, outright, in cash.

The last hundred million was for the Tenfold Rebate Cards. He’d been sitting on two of them for weeks; a card that turns one hundred million into one billion, and he’d never once had a spare hundred million liquid to feed it. Today, that hundred million was officially assigned to lighting the first one up — the rebate would seed the second.

A hundred million in, a billion back — twice over. There are worse errands.

Transfers done, he logged into Sulli’s account, then Goo Hara’s.

Sulli’s held ₩360 million. He mirrored the play without ceremony — buy order queued for Samjin Group.

Hara’s account held ₩1.5 billion.

Fifteen. Full fifteen billion won — far past anything he’d guessed. He’d assumed the little mermaid, entrusting her account to a near-stranger for the first time, would float a modest trial sum. Something careful. Something proportionate to how recently they’d met.

Instead: her entire treasury, handed over with both hands.

She trusts me like that. This much money — and she’s not the least bit afraid I’d simply take it and vanish to the other side of the world?

He leaned back in the chair, and the thought sat there, warm and slightly outrageous, while the morning traffic hummed on the streets of Mapo below.

License tomorrow. Signing the day after. Six girls, one crown, a company of his own — and a handbook full of goddesses still waiting to be collected.

The plan was in motion.
