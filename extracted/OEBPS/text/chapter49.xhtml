Chapter Forty-Nine

The Girl Left Behind

TWO DAYS LATER · MORNING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment

Two days went by in a blink.

Ham Eun-jung wrapped the Hong Kong concert, caught the late-night flight, and walked out into Seoul a little past eight in the morning. She didn’t go home first. She hailed a taxi straight to Cheongdam-dong.

The car pulled up beneath his building; she paid and hurried in. At his door she glanced once at his latest reply on her phone, and pressed the bell.

The door opened quickly.

Song Ji-yeon stood there in a gray lounging hoodie, hair still faintly chaotic — a man recently horizontal. He saw who it was and smiled.

“That was fast. Didn’t you text me from the runway, like, an hour ago?”

Woof!

The words were barely out of him when a white furball shot out from behind his legs, made a beeline for her ankles, and set its whole rear half wagging like a little motor.

“Mushroom!” Her face lit up all at once. She crouched, gathered the dog into her arms, and rubbed her nose into the fuzzy head. “Did you miss me or not?”

Mushroom whimpered in her arms and put his tongue to her cheek.

“I’d call that a yes.” He leaned against the doorframe, watching the reunion, the corners of his mouth climbing without permission.

She stood up with the dog in her arms — and only then noticed the faint shadows under his eyes.

“These two days… he didn’t make trouble for you, did he?”

“So much trouble.” He sighed on purpose and pointed at the sofa cushion with a corner gone missing. “Behold his work. The boy has energy to burn. I haven’t slept properly in two days.”

Another day begun with a Mushroom lick to the face.

She followed his finger — the cushion chewed open at one corner, white stuffing peeking out — and her ears went pink.

“Mianhae. He’s not usually like this. I think a strange place makes him nervous.”

“Relax, I’m teasing.” He waved it off and stepped aside. “It’s a cushion. Out with the old. Come in.”

She changed her shoes and carried Mushroom into the living room.

The instant his paws touched the floor he made two affectionate loops around her ankles — then, as if remembering an appointment, turned, picked up the chew bone Song Ji-yeon had bought him two days earlier, trotted it back to Song Ji-yeon’s feet, and started the tail motor again. The naked defection sat somewhere between exasperating and hilarious.

“Yah, Mushroom! Already forgot your unnie?” She crouched again and tried to lure the furball back with sweet talk.

Mushroom tilted his head. Looked at her. Looked at Song Ji-yeon. And chose to nudge the chew bone another centimeter against Song Ji-yeon’s slipper, throat rumbling out a wheedling mmph-mmph.

Inside, Song Ji-yeon was throwing a small parade. See? Even the dog knows who heads this household.

Outwardly he merely looked entitled, bending to pat the little head.

“Animals read people accurately. He knows who’s good to him.”

…

Fine. You win.

She straightened, declining to litigate the matter further with an ungrateful dog, and put her curiosity to the human instead.

“So what did you even do these two days — stay home dog-sitting? How did he get this attached to you?”

“More or less.” He headed for the kitchen, came back with a chilled bottle of sparkling water and a glass. “I signed a lease on a storefront two days ago, and after that there wasn’t much left for the company to do. So — babysitting your dog it was.” He twisted the cap, poured, and slid the glass across to her.

She took it and had a sip. Cold sparks burst on her tongue, and two days of schedules finally began draining out of her shoulders.

“A storefront? You’re opening a new place?”

“ONGI’s numbers are good. The company’s expanding.” He sat down across from her and, in passing, relocated the chew bone out of slipper range.

“So how was Hong Kong?”

“It was great. The crowd was amazing.” She sank into the sofa and closed her eyes — fatigue in the lines of her, but a live wire of excitement still running underneath it.

“Clearly the Hong Kong fandom is no joke.” He was glad for her — sincerely glad. What he genuinely feared was the six of them sinking for good into the Peninsula’s opinion-whirlpool, ending up sick with it, one and then another, the way they had in his previous life.

“Naturally—” She found a more comfortable slouch, sweetness spreading over her face. “Our group is quite famous across Southeast Asia too, you know.”

He believed her. 2012 was T-ara at their absolute peak — in the overseas market, not even SNSD, the nation’s girl group, could have sworn to a guaranteed win over them. The pity of it was that the peak had barely two years left to run: follow-up resources never kept pace, the bullying scandal torched the domestic market, and a generation’s chart-topping group quietly dissolved back into the crowd.

She reached for the sparkling water, sipped, set the glass on the coffee table, and burrowed deeper into the cushions. The lemon-yellow ocean in Hong Kong was still moving behind her eyes, and she found she wanted to hand all of it to him.

“You should have seen it — the second we started ‘Roly-Poly,’ the entire arena was on its feet.” Her eyes had gone bright; the words tumbled out with the urgency of someone holding a present behind her back.

He leaned on the sofa arm and watched the stage put color back in her. Something in his chest loosened. These were exactly the stories he wanted — anything that kept the pressure from settling into her bones.

“That must have been a sight. I’ve watched a clip or two of your overseas fan events — even through a phone screen, it lands.”

She folded her legs under herself, turned her whole body toward him, and her hands got into the act.

“And one fan held up this enormous board — ‘EUN-JUNG-UNNIE, SARANGHAE!’ — I nearly broke character onstage.” She burst out laughing before she’d even finished.

He watched her laugh with her guard all the way down, and moved in for the tease.

“Did you cry, though?”

Needling — plainly needling — about the last time she’d teared up. She knew it, too.

“I did not. I’m a professional.” Chin up, pride arranged.

He spread his hands with the infinite patience of a man humoring a child.

“Right, right. A professional.”

The shameless placating set her off again, and arguing didn’t seem worth the calories — she simply delivered a small, dignified hmph through her nose.

Then she settled back into the cushions, a cat with the bones removed, and let the trip pour out slow. Every taut nerve she’d carried through two airports had been unknotting, inch by inch, since the moment she walked through that door.

“But honestly — the night view in Hong Kong is gorgeous.” Her voice had gone lazy, half of it still inside the memory. “You stand at the hotel window and the whole of Victoria Harbour is lying there under your feet.”

He made a low sound of listening, and let her talk.

“We… went to Lan Kwai Fong too…” A thread of regret wound through it. “But manager-oppa banned alcohol. So we had… juice. Ji-yeon tried to sneak a cocktail order past him and got flattened by a single look.”

She laughed at her own story this time, eyes curving, dimples surfacing.

He laughed along with her.

“If it’s a drink you’re after, I have bottles right here. Next time, Ji-yeon can drink at my place.”

“Yah, Song Ji-yeon! She only just turned legal — don’t you dare corrupt her.” She puffed up — and underneath the scolding sat genuine bafflement. She was standing right here in his home, and when the man poured drinks, he invited… Ji-yeon? Not her? She could hold her liquor perfectly well, thank you very much.

He, of course, had no idea he’d trodden on anything — he was simply following the conversation where it went. And it was funny, in his head: Park Ji-yeon needed no corrupting from anyone. That little dragon was going to march into a tattoo shop all on her own one day, full personality, zero chaperones.

“Okay, okay. No corrupting the maknae.”

She curled deeper into the sofa and kept the Hong Kong stories coming for a long while — until he noticed the sentences arriving slower and slower. He checked the time. Almost ten.

“Sleepy?” he asked, quiet.

She nodded. “Last night was the red-eye. I only dozed a little on the plane.”

“Then sleep a bit here—” Worried she’d take it wrong, he patched it over immediately. “There’s a guest room. The bedding’s all new.”

She blinked — and the tips of her ears went faintly pink. Some part of her wanted very much to say yes. But the dorm had someone waiting in it.

“I can’t. Ji-yeon’s waiting for me. She wants to move out — I promised I’d go apartment-hunting with her.”

“Eh? Why’s that?” He was honestly puzzled. The company dorm had been perfectly comfortable — why the sudden move? He remembered it clearly, too: in his previous life Eun-jung, So-yeon, and Qri had drifted out of the Nonhyeon-dong company dorm one after another through 2012, but Bo-ram, Hyo-min, and Park Ji-yeon had stayed put. How had this life ended up different?

“Yah — aren’t you the one buying T-ara?” She shot him a look, exasperated. “We’re terminating with the company as it is. Squatting in the company dorm after that would just be awkward — so we decided to find our own places. So-yeon-unnie and Qri-unnie already had apartments in Seoul and they’ve moved out. Hyo-min bought a place. Bo-ram-unnie went home to her parents’. The dorm is down to Ji-yeon now.”

Ever heard of the girl left behind? Please direct your attention to Exhibit Park Ji-yeon.

He mustered an awkward little smile. This, he genuinely hadn’t gamed out — that one decision of his, by pure accident, had turned the maknae into the girl left behind.
