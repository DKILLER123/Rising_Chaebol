Chapter Twenty-Two

Together

SATURDAY, SEPTEMBER 8 · A LITTLE PAST NINE ICC Jeju — the east gate the tteokbokki stand by the exit

The east gate. Night wind came up off the ocean carrying the char of the squid grill tangled with the sweet burn of chili sauce, and set it all spinning around the exit. Song Ji-yeon stood at the stall with a paper cup in his hand. The tteokbokki had just come out of the pot — sauce lacquered on red and glossy, hot enough to numb the tongue clean through.

“Hss — okay. Hot.”

The ajumma behind the cart laughed and held out a bamboo skewer. He took it and began skewering the survivors at the bottom of the cup.

One breakfast since morning. Half a night of standing and shouting. Arms sore, stomach empty. This little expedition had not been cheap — flight, hotel, scalper’s ticket, custom light board. All told, several months of an ordinary person’s tteokbokki budget.

Worth it, though. Eun-jung had smiled before she left the stage.

He was two pieces in when a shape came flying out of the east gate. Black baseball cap pressed low, mask over most of the face. Eun-jung was running, hard, heels hammering the concrete.

He watched her rush the stall and slid his skewer back into the cup.

“Slow down.”

She caught herself on her knees, panting twice, and looked up at him. Her eyes were rimmed red; there was still an unblotted bright streak at the outer corner. She didn’t speak. She just fixed him with a long stare.

A few seconds of it. He lifted the paper cup slightly, grin starting.

“Eat?”

“You really are out here eating tteokbokki.”

Her voice was muffled behind the mask and thick through the nose. Somewhere in it was a note of injury — she had spent the whole walk out here unraveling, and this man had solved his evening with a paper cup.

He pushed the cup toward her, helplessly reasonable.

“What else was I going to do? Handstands at the exits?”

She blinked — and then she laughed. One short, quiet breath of a laugh. But a laugh.

“Just out of the pot. Fair warning — spicy.”

She shook her head and drew her neck back into her shoulders.

“…I want some anyway.”

“What happened to the figure management?”

He was surprised. Tteokbokki was one of the Peninsula’s most beloved street foods — he’d have bet money she knew every good stall in Seoul by name.

“It’s not that.”

The answer came out dull and low. Inside, she was sighing. Ever since the bullying scandal, that old variety-show clip — her feeding rice cake to Hwa-young — had been cut and recut into the core “evidence” of the case against her. Since then she hadn’t dared touch tteokbokki anywhere in public. Even the sight of it put a shadow across her chest.

“I thought you’d be a tteokbokki person.”

She didn’t answer that. Her fingers were strangling the hem of her leather jacket, the fabric creased into ridges. She didn’t know how to answer — the exhaustion of the stage braided together with the weight of ten thousand dark seats, and she couldn’t quite pull a full breath.

He noticed, then, that she was still in her stage costume: a thin black leather jacket up top, black hot shorts below, and the night wind moving over two pale legs that were trembling, very slightly, on their own.

He set the cup down on the counter. His brow folded.

“Why didn’t you at least change before running out? Aren’t you freezing?”

She forced her head into a shake.

“Not cold.”

The next gust came up off the sea, and her shoulders flinched anyway.

He laughed — and took off his jacket and held it out.

She startled back half a step, hands up and waving.

“No — no, I’m going straight back anyway—”

“You bolted out here in a stage costume. If the wind gets you sick, your manager will hang me off a streetlight.”

He left no room for debate; he dropped the jacket over her shoulders himself. And it wasn’t that he disbelieved her, exactly. Some refusals are pure protocol — a girl will wave off a jacket three times while meaning none of it. That was a lesson he had logged across two lifetimes and thirty-some years of them.

It got a laugh out of her after all. She held the collar in place and let the jacket settle. It still carried his warmth, and the warmth wrapped her all at once — even the ice at her fingertips eased. Something in her chest moved strangely. It had been a long time since anyone had stood a body between her and the weather.

Neither of them spoke for a moment.

A few fans drifted past the stall on their way out of the venue, and Eun-jung jammed the cap brim lower and went rigid.

He took one unhurried step forward, his shoulders sliding neatly into the line between her and the passersby, blocking most of her from view.

She looked up at the side of his face. Her ears went warm. Backstage she had had a stomach full of things to say, and now that he was standing in front of her, her throat was packed with cotton.

“Thank you” was too light. “Sorry” was too strange.

“Song Ji-yeon.”

“Hm?”

He tipped his head toward her, slightly.

“Why didn’t you leave? Just now.”

Her voice dropped. Her fingers were wringing the corner of the jacket.

“Didn’t I? I’m the one standing at a tteokbokki stand.”

“On stage.” She pulled a breath in and stared straight at him. “When the whole hall was like that. Why did you stand up?”

God knew what it had done to her, up there, when the only light left in the building came on.

He nudged a rice cake around the cup with his skewer, tone as light as the steam off the pot.

“I bought a T-ara light board.”

She froze.

“…That’s it?”

“What else? I’d already paid for it. Not switching it on would’ve been a loss.” He shrugged — the shrug of a man discussing margins.

Her mouth opened and hung there, unemployed. How was a person built like this. He had done the single warmest thing anyone had done for her in months, and he described it the way a grocer mentions the free scallions he throws in with your vegetables.

The gratitude jammed halfway up her chest, going neither up nor down, and her eyes started to heat all over again.

He saw the shine gathering on her lashes and went faintly to pieces. He coughed it back into order.

“There was one other reason, obviously.”

She sniffed and looked up at him.

“What?”

“You needed me.”

The wind passed between them. The flippancy was gone when he said it.

“It was that dark. Somebody had to be a light.”

The sentence went straight through her ribs. Her nose stung, and she turned her face away fast, developing a sudden, consuming fascination with the squid grill’s big iron plate.

“You are honestly—” Her voice had gone to gravel. “—a very strange man.”

“I heard that. That was a compliment.”

“It was NOT.”

“It was.”

“It wasn’t.”

“Then your delivery needs work.”

“Yah!”

Her fist came up and cut a small arc through the air — met his grinning eyes on the way, lost its nerve, and came down again.

He watched her fluster and fight the smile, and his own grin deepened.

“If you want to hit me, hit me. Don’t hold it in. You came out swinging the first time we ever met — one more round won’t move the average.”

“Pfft.”

That finally broke it — she clapped a hand over her mouth and laughed, and the laugh bent her forward, and as soon as it passed she ducked her head and swept her sleeve across her eyes, fast, as though it had never happened.

He saw none of it. Officially. He fished a fish cake out of the broth pot at the corner of the cart and held it out.

“This one’s not spicy.”

“I really can’t.”

She swallowed hard — reason making its last stand. She had barely eaten all day either.

“One bite.”

He advanced the skewer a few centimeters. Tempting her, with visible technique.

Her resistance folded at the knees. Her eyes locked onto the steam coming off the fish cake and stayed there.

He pressed the advantage.

“You danced two full numbers tonight. One bite is not going to move the scale.”

“You’re not a nutritionist.”

She was caving and covering it with grumbling.

“I own a hotpot restaurant. Round down — that’s half a dietician.”

She glared at him with everything she had, and took the skewer. She worked the mask down with two careful fingers and bit like a small cat, once, dainty. Hot broth flooded her mouth. Her empty stomach smoothed out on the spot, and the warmth of it ran all the way out to her hands.

She had the mask half-lifted back up when the phone in her pocket went off like an alarm. The screen flashed three syllables — Manager oppa.

The smile vanished off her face. She shot Song Ji-yeon one flustered glance and stepped away from the stall, quick, phone to her ear.

Overheard call · Manager oppa → Ham Eun-jung

“Oppa.”

“Where are you? Come back now — we’re leaving for the airport.”

The voice on the other end was ragged, fast, raw — and Song Ji-yeon, standing to one side, caught every word of it clean.

“Now? But it isn’t over — what about the closing photo?”

A strange half-second of silence. Then the manager’s voice came back down to a growl, defeat and fury in equal parts.

“What photo? The organizers aren’t letting you six anywhere near the closing photo. Get back here.”

The hand holding the fish cake stopped in midair.

Song Ji-yeon’s brows had knotted themselves into one steep crease.

Banned from the photo.

They’d been made to perform a full set into a Black Ocean — and then, offstage, denied even a place in the picture. They’d wrung the towel dry and wouldn’t let it hang on the line. Squeeze the fruit, then bill it for the mess. The disposal had been executed with a thoroughness you almost had to admire.

Eun-jung’s throat worked. Her eyes were full of things she had no room to say — and in the end, all of it collapsed into one obedient sentence.

“Understood.”

“Where are you now? Don’t wander.”

“Outside the east gate. At the tteokbokki stall.”

“Then stand there. Don’t move. The van’s coming.”

The call cut.

She stood where it had left her. The sea wind pushed her hair around her face, and it looked, he thought, unkindly lonely.

He walked over and stood beside her, saying nothing.

She turned around and manufactured a smile out of spare parts, voice at the bottom of its range.

“We’re going back to Seoul.”

“Now?”

“Mm. The organizers won’t have us in the closing photo. Manager says we’re going straight to the airport.”

Saying it out loud made her want to find a crack in the pavement and move in permanently. Everything that came with being an idol — every last scrap of dignity they had carried onto that stage — had been walked on tonight, in front of thousands of witnesses.

He set his paper cup on top of the trash bin by the stall and clapped his hands clean.

“Good.”

She stared at him. This man was unbelievable. She was standing here in ruins, and this was his idea of comfort?

“…Good?”

“Mm.”

Light as anything.

“No going back in there to smile at those people. Airport sooner, Seoul sooner, bed sooner. And you’re out of this cold wind.”

She looked at him for a long moment. There was no pity in his eyes. There was none of that sticky, humiliating sympathy either, and no helpless anger posing as strength. He had taken an eviction and filed it under going home early to rest.

The logic was crude, and it was gentle, and against all reason it unlocked something in her chest that had been bolted shut for an hour.

“What about you? What’s your plan now?”

“Seoul, same as you. Probably.”

He checked the time on his phone. 9:10 PM. If he booked now, he could make the late flight — home around one in the morning.

“Tonight?”

“Mm. Nothing left to see here anyway.”

She looked at him, and the idea arrived in her head already halfway out of her mouth.

“Then ride to the airport with us.”

He blinked.

“With you?”

She heard it as it left her — improper, more than improper — and her ears went pink; her hands came up, waving the sentence away.

“No — I mean, we have the van. We’re going to the airport anyway. It’s on your way. We could give you a lift.”

He watched her trip over the explanation, and started laughing.

“I haven’t said no to anything. Why are you negotiating with yourself?”

“So is it yes or no?”

She had her lip between her teeth, chin up, stubborn.

“It’s yes.”

She let a breath go and patted herself once on the sternum.

“Then don’t wander off. The van will be here any minute.”

“Do I look like a person who wanders?”

“Yes.”

Instant. No hesitation at all. A hint of relish, even.

“That assessment has personal history baked into it.”

He laughed again. They stood at the curb and waited.

Inside the venue the closing sets were still detonating at full volume. Out on the road, fans drifted by in twos and threes, laughing, comparing notes — ordinary people filing out of an ordinary Saturday-night concert.

For T-ara, it had been a nightmare with its own light show.

Eun-jung pulled the jacket closer around herself. The collar smelled faintly of soap, and she breathed it in without letting herself notice, and felt, absurdly, safe.

“Song Ji-yeon.”

“Hm?”

“I saw you. On stage. Thank you.”

She said it very quietly.

“I know. You nearly missed a half-beat.”

The tease arrived a second later. Her face went through a small crisis.

“I did not.”

“You did. Right at the top of the first number. Lucky you held it — otherwise this ticket would’ve been a write-off.” The merchant’s face, fully deployed.

She didn’t know whether to laugh or invoice him.

“A write-off. Again. Is there anything in your head except a ledger?”

“I’m in business. I keep books.”

“And tonight? Did tonight break even?”

She had turned her head toward him, eyes catching the streetlight, absurdly bright.

He looked back at her. In the dim orange light the rims of her eyes were still red, and she was lovelier than she had any intention of being.

The joking drained out of his face. He nodded, once, seriously.

“It broke even. With interest.”

She didn’t press for details. She looked down at her shoes, and the corners of her mouth climbed, and stayed up.

Headlights swung across them — a black van, big and boxy, pulling up smooth to the curb. The door rattled open along its track.

“Eun-jung-unnie! Get in, quick!”

Park Ji-yeon’s head appeared in the gap — and one second later her gaze locked onto Song Ji-yeon, standing at Eun-jung’s shoulder.

“Ohhh. So THIS is the person unnie was in such a hurry to see.”

The gossip kindling caught fire on the spot. Exactly as predicted — the someone Eun-jung-unnie ran off to meet was President Song himself.

Eun-jung heard the tease land. Her face went furnace-hot, and she marched over and pushed Ji-yeon’s head straight back into the van.

“Park Ji-yeon. Inside. Now.”

“I didn’t say anything—”

Ji-yeon dodged sideways out of reach, and while dodging found the time to waggle her eyebrows at Song Ji-yeon, grinning like a cat that had gotten into the cream.

He rubbed his nose, publicly embarrassed, and offered the van a polite bow of the head.

“Hello.”

“Hello, hello~”

Ji-yeon had escaped Eun-jung’s grip and returned the greeting with both hands’ worth of enthusiasm.

“Sajangnim! Your fame precedes you.”

“Preceded by whom, exactly?”

Eun-jung, at the end of her rope, climbed in bodily and used her whole torso to shove Ji-yeon deeper into the cabin. The van CCM had sent was a nine-seater Hyundai Starex, and Park Ji-yeon was not its only passenger — the other four were inside as well, and a man sat up front: T-ara’s manager, doubling as driver for the night.
