Chapter One Hundred One

The Qri and Bo-ram Show

FRIDAY EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Daewoo Royal County — Park Ji-yeon’s flat, second floor

The car slid into the Daewoo Royal County complex, and he rode the elevator up exactly one flight — the floor above his own Unit 101 — to a door he could have opened blind.

Passcode, from memory. A soft electronic beep. The lock gave.

He pushed the door open, took one step — and stopped. Then inhaled, sharply, the way a man does before lifting something with his back.

“Good grief…”

His brain threw a brief error screen.

The living room, previously famous for its floor space, no longer had any. Premium supermarket bags in five colors, unopened cartons of outdoor equipment, a cooler the size of a chest freezer — all of it stacked into a range of small mountains with hiking trails between them. And occupying the room’s dead center, in positions of honor, a row of still-packaged tents and folding tables and chairs, displayed the way other households display a piano.

Anyone walking in cold would have concluded that six women were preparing to pioneer Siberia, or to declare a sovereign nation somewhere in the wilderness.

The six women in question were clustered around the sofa in mid-conference. The door’s sound turned all twelve eyes at once.

“Oppa! You’re here!”

Ji-yeon came off the sofa like a released spring, trotted over, and took him by the sleeve, towing him in as cargo. She presented the mountain range with both arms, chin aimed somewhere at the ceiling.

“Look, look! One afternoon’s spoils! We even got the grill AND an outdoor projector!”

His mouth developed a small twitch, and then the roast simply had to happen.

“You call this camping? Take this pile into actual deep mountains and you’d comfortably outlive an ice age. What’s the concept here — debuting in the wilderness?”

The room collapsed into laughter, six-way.

Ji-yeon pooched her lower lip out to its full operational depth.

“Oppaaa, stop teasing us. We haven’t been out in forever — we want to actually play this time.”

And that, he had to concede, was unanswerable.

Being a top girl group meant your own time belonged to the calendar: pre-debut, the practice room had been the entire world; post-debut, the group had gone supernova almost immediately and the schedule had never once exhaled. Days that ran until the sleep budget hit zero don’t leave a line item for camping. And since the scandal — well. In the public eye they currently ranked somewhere between plague carriers and wanted posters; open venues were off the table entirely.

This trip was, by his count, their first real outing in months.

So yes — a first excursion deserved to be done in style. The style simply had a shipping manifest, that was all.

“But there’s extra, and then there’s— this.” He surveyed a supply line that could have opened a small supermarket. “Are we camping, or are we relocating the entire household?”

He pointed at the shin-high cooler holding down the far corner.

“What’s in that one?”

Ji-yeon’s tongue made a brief appearance at the corner of her mouth.

“Hanwoo! Bo-ram-unnie said we absolutely had to buy extra meat, or we’d starve to death out in the wild.”

Jeon Bo-ram, mid-excavation of a chip bag, heard her own name dropped and briefly froze.

Wasn’t that YOUR idea? Why is it suddenly my requirement? Why is it ALWAYS me?

She opened her mouth to file a correction — then remembered that she did, in fact, love meat, and elected to own the position with full authority instead.

“Camping without grilled meat is just sleeping somewhere worse!”

The sheer conviction of it short-circuited his rebuttal, and he laughed — while giving her a long, sidelong, deeply untrustworthy look.

Tsk. Tsk. So that’s where the capital came from. Every gram of hanwoo this woman had ever eaten, routed with a fund manager’s discipline to exactly the right districts. The sole disappointment of the portfolio: a complete refusal to add height.

“Hmm.” Bo-ram tilted her head back and stared up at him, eyes narrowed to slits. “I don’t know what it is, but I have the distinct feeling Song Ji-yeon-ssi is thinking something rude.”

“No, no. Nothing of the sort.” He waved it off, smiling. “Fine. Eat. All may be eaten. One question, though — who’s carrying this civilization downstairs?”

The six girls went silent in perfect unison, like a classroom the instant the teacher asks for volunteers.

Twelve eyes swung onto him, wide with innocence and trembling with hope.

They had only just finished hauling it all up. Now the same mountain was expected to go back down. There were labor laws about this, probably.

His mouth twitched once more; his palm met his forehead.

“Aigoo. I knew it. I came here today to be the free porter.”

Eun-jung stepped forward, unhurried, and pressed a bottle of ice-cold water into his hand.

“You’ve worked hard. Tonight I’ll grill you extra slices — a full stack, all for you. Deal?”

At that voice, at that temperature, roughly seventy percent of his grievance evaporated on the spot.

“This is why Eun-jung is, forever, the sensible one.”

He resigned himself to the contract, rolled up his sleeves, and began the load-out of the strategic materiel.

Thank heaven, at least, for German engineering — the Mercedes’ trunk swallowed an amount of equipment that should legally have required a truck. And to seat everyone, Lee Qri had driven her own car over as the second vessel. Two cars, four seats each; the party split accordingly.

So-yeon and Bo-ram boarded Qri’s car by unspoken treaty.

Which left Ji-yeon, Hyo-min, and Eun-jung assigned to his.

He reached over and opened the passenger door — and before a single word could leave him, Hyo-min had already whipped the rear door open and was waving the maknae in like a parking attendant.

“Here, here — back seat, back seat! So roomy back there!”

And in the same motion, smooth as dealing cards, she applied one discreet push to Eun-jung’s shoulder — aimed, with visible intent, at the passenger seat.

Eun-jung blinked. Pink arrived at her cheeks in a light, even coat. She didn’t resist — she let the vector carry her, sat down in the front, and buckled her seatbelt as quietly as a secret.

Hyo-min caught his eyes in the rearview mirror and winked, the entire scheme written on her face in headline type.

Because she was, by now, a fully invested shipper. Ever since the dragon’s intelligence had first paired Eun-jung-unnie with the Sajangnim, Hyo-min had considered the match self-evident — a match sewn in heaven, delivered to Seoul, waiting only for someone to pick it up.

And yet her instincts kept insisting that unnie was avoiding suspicion. Keeping a deliberate distance. Dancing around the man instead of toward him.

And this very morning — the dragon had reported a woman at oppa’s place. A rival, thank you very much.

Eun-jung-unnie. Listen. Good men get taken. Nothing begins by itself — somebody has to start the story! Aigoo. Fine. It always comes down to me, doesn’t it?

He pretended, generously, not to have seen any of it — though the corner of his mouth declined to stay level.

Park Hyo-min. Something else, this one. Matchmaking, was it? Openly? In his own car?

Which meant Eun-jung’s operational security was holding: months in, and her own members still hadn’t cracked the file. He filed the observation away, kept his mouth shut, and pressed the accelerator.

The car pulled out of the complex, level and calm, and folded into the evening traffic.

✦ ✦ ✦

They’d barely merged onto the main road before the rear seat detonated.

“We’re OFF! Finally! OUT! Do you know how long I’ve been imprisoned in that apartment?! One MONTH! One month of walls!”

Ji-yeon had gotten halfway out of her seatbelt in her fervor, torso in the front cabin’s airspace, volume set to stadium.

He laughed, eyes flicking to the mirror.

“What’s the matter — games not doing it for you anymore?”

“If you could go outside, would YOU stay in? Honestly.” A pout — and then her eyes did a full rotation, and the pout reconfigured itself into a scheme. “Oh — right. Oppa. Now that we’re signed to you — the overseas schedules. What happens to those? We still have the Japan ones, don’t we?”

The Okinawa showcase contract had been signed a month ago; CCM had already pushed the Japan release news and lined up the album promotions. Those dates were one of the few things their year still contained.

But the company had changed hands underneath the calendar. Whether the dates still existed, whether they still belonged anywhere — nobody had told them.

And the maknae had been home long enough that she could feel her limbs entering early retirement. Gaming, it turned out, did not scratch the specific itch of a stage under lights.

“You mean the K-Collection in Okinawa showcase and the Japan promotions for the new album?”

He’d actually meant to brief them all next week — no office, no staff, no point telling them sooner — but the question had been asked directly, and directly was fine.

“Relax. Everything you signed at CCM transferred over to ShiningYouth clean — the showcase, the campaign, all of it. So understand the terms: this week is the last leisure you get. Next week, once the office is signed, training resumes. Full program.” He let that land, then added, dry: “Though after a month of horizontal living, I do wonder if you can still dance.”

“Jinjja?!” The eyes in the mirror went floodlit; she nearly cleared her seatbelt a second time. “Oppa, do NOT underestimate me! I have every T-ara choreography since debut stored in my spine! No refresher needed — I could be pushed onto a stage right now and nail it!”

He smiled at the road. He did, in fact, believe her.

That was the thing about second-generation girl groups, the thing this decade’s fans didn’t yet know they’d miss: the reason those groups ran for years was that the fundamentals under them were load-bearing. T-ara had been a tier-one act. The craft wasn’t negotiable.

Besides — the Okinawa set and the Japan promotion rounds were all already-released singles. Material the six of them could perform asleep, in the dark, in the rain. One pass of rehearsal and they’d be stage-ready.

But this would be T-ara’s first time on any stage wearing ShiningYouth’s name, and he wanted the maiden voyage spotless.

One week of rehearsal. Granted.

“Oppa — so what about after that?” Hyo-min had leaned forward into the space between the seats. “The whole rest of the plan. Is it only the Japan dates?”

“Step by step. First the showcase, then the album. Get those two boxes ticked, and then — Chinese lessons.” His eyes swept them once via the mirror. “Next year, the China campaign begins for real.”

“Aigoo, Chinese lessons?! Chinese is SO hard… Oppa. Sweetheart. Boss. Can we skip that one part?”

“No. Want the China campaign? Then Chinese it is. No exceptions.”

The car went loud and stayed loud — Japan logistics stacked on top of China dreams, three voices deep, with running commentary. Even Eun-jung, in the passenger seat with the quiet smile of a woman whose scheme had no part in any of this, couldn’t keep her mouth from bending.

SIX P.M. Gwacheon, Gyeonggi-do Seoul Grand Park — the camping meadow

Their destination: the camping ground at Seoul Grand Park. From Cheongdam-dong out to Gwacheon, a solid hour of road.

A NOTE ON THE VENUE · SEOUL GRAND PARK

Korea’s largest comprehensive theme park — opened 1984 in Gwacheon, Gyeonggi-do: zoo, botanical garden, amusement park, and art museum in one sweep of grounds.

A first-rank holiday outing for Seoulites — an hour from the capital, and worth the drive on a good day.

The park keeps several camping zones: bring your own tent, book an RV slot, or take a cabin — your choice of wilderness intensity.

They rolled in at six on the dot. The sun hung low and red at the edge of things; a scatter of tents dotted the field; past them a wide lawn ran gently downhill toward a stand of small trees, and the air was all grass and turned earth.

Friday — the park was nearly theirs. He registered at the management office and, without a single complication, took a prime, generously sized independent site. The kind you pitch yourself; the kind that, on a holiday weekend, you book weeks in advance and fight over.

The six girls piled out of the cars, and every face in the fleet switched on.

“Wow — it’s gorgeous here!” Bo-ram spread her arms wide and breathed in like the meadow was personally hers. Her mood had gone from rented sedan to convertible.

He left them to their reverie and got to work — no ceremony, sleeves already up. The tents came out of the trunk in bundles; he carried them to the flattest patch of lawn he could find, crouched, and started feeding poles and driving stakes with the absent fluency of a man who had done this at every summer camp Koreatown’s churches had ever run.

Four tents, total. A genuine workload — and if he didn’t hustle, tonight’s bedroom was going to be starring him and the sky.

Eun-jung hesitated a moment, then came over after him.

“I’ll help.”

“You know how to pitch a tent?” He glanced up, honestly surprised.

“I can learn, can’t I?”

She crouched down beside him and watched his hands the way she studied choreography — total attention, no shortcuts — then reached in wherever the fabric needed holding. One teaching, one learning; the division of labor sorted itself out inside a minute.

The sun pressed warm on their crowns. A fine film of sweat came up along his hairline, and he raised his sleeve toward it —

— and she got there first. A tissue appeared out of her pocket and pressed, light and precise, against his temple.

“Don’t rub with that — your sleeve is all dust.”

Gentle, unhesitating, as if she’d done it a hundred times. Her fingertips passed over his temple, and his hand simply stopped where it was.

They were close. Close enough that the quiet underneath the wind was made of two people breathing.

For one full second, something in him moved without permission. He looked at her — looked, and kept looking — and somewhere under the looking, the same old sentence assembled itself: Ham Eun-jung, genuinely, is a good woman.

And he — by every sign and by his own signature — was not built to be a good man. The Goddess Handbook was bound to him, and that binding only ran one way, down a road with no exits.

Other roads, then. He’d simply have to make it up to her on every road he had left.

“Ahem.” Being looked at like that had exceeded her tolerances. Eun-jung’s ears had gone faintly hot; she broke the line of sight, bent her head, and busied herself with a tent rope that did not strictly need her. “That… this side’s done. Help me hold the other peg?”

“Mm.” Lips pressed around a smile, he crouched back down at her side.

✦ ✦ ✦

While those two built the tent line of the camp with their own private weather system overhead, the rest of the party had gone feral.

Ji-yeon and Hyo-min had seized Qri by the arms and sprinted her the length of the meadow for no stated reason, then peeled off to the edge of the trees to gather firewood for later skewers. So-yeon, cradling her freshly charged camera, had been lying in wait by the tents for twenty minutes taking extreme close-ups of wildflowers nobody else had noticed existed.

Bo-ram wanted none of it — not the sprinting, not the flora. She squatted alone at the lawn’s edge, pulling up grass in small thoughtful tufts.

She was mid-daydream when a rustling started up behind her — followed, unhurried, by a hand arriving over her shoulder, fingers delicately pinching an enormous black beetle of unknown provenance.

“Bo-ram-unnie—” The voice came out stretched, sing-song, and drenched in horror-movie. “Look what I caaaught…”

Bo-ram turned on pure reflex — and met, at zero range, a wall of black beetle: shell gleaming like patent leather, all legs bicycling in open air, radiating menace from every joint.

“KYAAA—!!”

Her soul very nearly filed for separation. One shriek, and she was up off the ground in a scramble of limbs, running flat-out across the meadow and screaming in continuous commentary.

“LEE QRI, HAVE YOU LOST YOUR MIND?! Get it AWAY! Far away! ANOTHER PROVINCE!”

She ran with her eyes doing all the steering, which was how the raised clump of turf got its chance — caught her toe, folded her forward into a two-step stagger that nearly ended in journalism, and then sent her careening toward the densest population center in reach.

Namely: the tent-pitching operation.

“Bo-ram-unnie, don’t run~!”

“Hahaha — why are you RUNNING?!”

Qri jogged after her at leisure, beetle held aloft like a torch, herding one shrieking idol around the campsite in wide circles.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi! SAVE ME!” The appeal arrived mid-sprint, at full volume. “Qri’s attacking me with a BUG!”

He stood up out of the tent frame, caught between laughter and arbitration — and before he could rule on the case, Bo-ram had plunged headlong into a finished tent, tunneled through it, and burst out the far side still at top speed, dragging half the interior behind her. The entire campsite had by now achieved a state of total, low-flying chaos.

The rest of them had folded double. Ji-yeon and Hyo-min were holding each other upright by the ribs. Even the unflappable So-yeon could not lower her camera — she feared insects to approximately the same degree as the victim, but Bo-ram-unnie’s catastrophe photos had proven the stronger calling, and she trailed the pursuit at a brave distance, firing off frame after frame of a senior member at her absolute worst.

Eun-jung watched the two of them orbit the campsite — the sprinting and the pursuing — and gave up entirely, laughing until her eyes bent into crescents.

The campaign ran long. Eventually Qri granted the armistice, and the beetle was deported into a far patch of grass with full honors.

Bo-ram collapsed flat on the lawn, breathless, face glowing red to the ears, muttering an address to the sky that was mostly “Yah, Lee Qri, you remember this. You remember this well.”

Qri, still bent double from laughing, straightened up just enough to deliver her closing argument with complete legitimacy.

“Who told you to be a scaredy-cat? One little beetle and you mobilized the entire county.”

“YOU’RE the scaredy-cat!”

And they were off — one line each, back and forth, neither yielding a single position, while everyone around them vibrated between pity and helplessness.

He watched, laughing along with the rest.

Qri, forever the Bo-ram menace — half a chance was all she ever needed.

✦ ✦ ✦

Another hour went by before the last of the four tents stood.

The light had gone long and low. His stomach, no longer willing to be managed, filed its complaint.

He turned toward the nearest tent and raised his voice just enough.

“Eun-jung. Hungry yet?”

“Are you hungry? If you are, go on ahead — I’ll lay out the sleeping bags and come.” Her voice came out of the tent muffled, all cotton.

He walked over and pulled the flap open: Eun-jung on her knees inside, surrounded by sleeping bags and ground mats, sorting them into order.

“Leave that. Eat first, finish after.”

“Oppa — it’ll be dark soon. After dark is exactly when it doesn’t get done.” She shook her head without pausing.

They’d brought flashlights, not lanterns — the streetlamps by the access road would do nothing for the inside of a tent, and a half-made camp after dark was its own species of misery.

“Then I’m helping.” He could not out-stubborn her and had stopped pretending to try; he crouched down and joined the sorting.

She smiled at the ground, sweet and unrestrained.

“Oppa, you’re the best!”

“Glad you know it. So — come find me tonight.”

He said it with the grin fully deployed.

Six girls. Four tents. The arithmetic was not subtle — three tents of members, and one empty room with his name on it, waiting.

“YAH, oppa! Ji-yeon and the others are right outside! What if they hear you?!” Her face went scarlet on the spot.

“Yes, yes, fine! Withdrawn! Nothing was said!”

Between the two of them, the four sleeping bags got arranged to standard, and only then did they go rejoin the others.

Their site sat beside a small stream, its sound running underneath everything. The division of the evening had organized itself hours ago: he and Eun-jung on the tents; Ji-yeon, Hyo-min, and the rest on the fire and the food.

And on that side, truthfully, everything was already done. Every ingredient they’d hauled out of the city had come pre-marinated — the work of one very thorough afternoon. Nothing left to prep. Light the fire, lay the meat, eat.

The only missing pieces were the two who had wandered off to a tent with a flashlight and a scheme — and so, at the fire, five girls sat waiting, glancing down the meadow, while the grill ticked and heated and the first hanwoo never had a chance.
