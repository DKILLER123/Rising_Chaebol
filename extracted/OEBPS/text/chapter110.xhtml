Chapter One Hundred Ten

The Test No Official Could Withstand

TUESDAY · EARLY AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, en route The Mercedes, and a passenger studying the window

The car slid through Cheongdam traffic toward ShiningYouth Entertainment, smooth as poured syrup.

Song Ji-yeon glanced sideways at Han So-hee, buckled into the passenger seat and turned fully toward the window — and there she had stayed, the entire ride, not one extra word spent on him.

He didn’t get it. Signed out of nowhere by a company boss — shouldn’t she be happy? By now the girl had been shown the door by half the agencies in this city; even if gratitude was too much to ask for, a polite smile at the man holding her contract wasn’t.

Apparently not. Apparently the default setting ran colder than the air conditioning.

He turned it over once, twice, then decided the silence had lasted long enough.

“By the way — you’re going to be an artist now. Have you thought about a stage name?”

“Can’t I use my real one?”

A NOTE ON THE NAME · TWO CIRCLES, TWO RULES

The film world and the idol world do not share a naming philosophy. Idols live or die by overseas fandoms — and Korean, bless it, is a language the world stumbles over: few who try, fewer who survive the pronunciation. So idols armor themselves in English. Song Qian became Victoria; Bae Joo-hyun became Irene. International, memorable, safe on a foreign tongue.

Actors have no such problem. Screen work is domestic work — and nobody on earth picks a movie for the syllables in the credits. The story is the product, the performance is the product; the name is a label on the tin.

That holds strictly for rookies, of course. A legacy actor’s name is a different instrument entirely — three syllables that open budgets and guarantee openings. But that is a currency earned over decades, and no one hands it out at the door.

“You can — if nobody’s already using it.”

A NOTE ON THE SURNAME · THE HOUSE OF YI

Lee is to the Peninsula what water is to a riverbed. The old kingdom spent five centuries as the Yi dynasty, and dynasties multiply — princes, cadet lines, and a steady drip of the surname granted to meritorious officials like medals. Half the phone book descends from that arithmetic.

Which means collisions. In an entertainment industry with a decade’s head start on Lee So-hee, the odds that the name sat unclaimed were the odds of finding a parking spot in Gangnam at noon.

Sure enough — in his last life, the name on her records had never once been Lee So-hee. Someone had gotten there first, or the company had simply waved her past the question. Either way, the file in his memory read otherwise.

“Ne!”

She nodded along — then, after a beat, actually asked.

“If there is a same-named artist… can I still use my real name?”

“Most likely not. The company drafts a few options for you — but the final call is mine.” He paused, and went ahead with the question he’d been carrying since the car left ONGI. “What do you think of ‘Han So-hee’?”

It sat better in the mouth, he’d always felt. Easier on the ear, easier to remember — and the road it traveled in his memory was paved in gold.

Han So-hee’s brow folded. Is this not a conversation for show? You’ve already said it out loud — you clearly want me to use it. What am I supposed to do, decline?

Though — it wasn’t bad. Same given name, surname swapped to Han. It read well. It sounded, if she was honest, rather nice.

“It’s very nice.”

“Then it’s decided. From today, your stage name is Han So-hee.”

Black lines crawled down her forehead.

Hmph. He had it decided all along. If the asking was ceremonial, the notification would have done the same job — why dress it up as my choice? A domineering man, through and through.

She turned her head back to the window and stopped spending words on him.

Song Ji-yeon watched the girl pivot away yet again and felt distinctly out of his depth. How did one person grow spikes on every square centimeter?

Ten seconds ago the name was ‘very nice.’ Now I’ve been demoted back to scenery. Which is it?

The truth — that in his memory, this particular girl had rather liked the name that made her famous — stayed safely in his head, where it did neither of them any good. As far as he could tell, she was simply the quiet type, and quiet types were entitled to their weather.

He let the silence have the rest of the ride.

TUESDAY · ONE O’CLOCK Cheongdam-dong — ShiningYouth Entertainment Four floors, two SY monogram signs, and lunch straggling home

The car pulled up below the company’s building before long.

Two days of settling-in had transformed the place past recognition. The four-story block now wore its identity in broad daylight — two large SY art-logos hanging off the facade — and the hiring was nearly done besides. Past one in the afternoon, the lunch crowd was trickling back in ones and twos, employees who had gone out foraging returning with cups and convenience-store bags.

Song Ji-yeon walked Han So-hee in through the lobby, and greetings hailed him the whole way. He had, after all, practically lived in the building yesterday — a full day of it — and the young Sajangnim’s face had duly circulated.

He returned each one, rode the elevator to the fourth floor, and found Park Seon-young already planted at the office door, where he lit up the moment he spotted the pair.

“Sajangnim!”

“This is the company’s new artist — Han So-hee. Seon-young, the management contract I asked for. Ready?”

Park Seon-young’s eyes did one lap of Han So-hee and just about applauded. The Sajangnim’s eye for talent, truly — where does he find them this fresh. He nodded, folder out and extended.

“Ready since this morning, Sajangnim — only waiting on your review.”

Song Ji-yeon took the folder without opening it, unlocked the office, and waved them both in.

“Sit anywhere. Let me look it over first.” He dropped into the broad executive chair and aimed a finger at the sofa across the room.

Han So-hee nodded, asked nothing, and sat on the sofa to wait for whatever came next.

Inside, she had gone still as a pond.

I’m already here. Is there a refund counter somewhere? Then acceptance is simply the cheaper option.

Meanwhile, at the desk, Song Ji-yeon leafed through the management agreement Park Seon-young had drafted. Utterly ordinary. Soaked through with pressure and exploitation, top to bottom — and none of it his problem to fix, because it was the industry’s resting state, not his invention.

EXCLUSIVE MANAGEMENT AGREEMENT — ACTOR TRACK · STANDARD FORM

Article 1 — Term

Exclusive representation for seven (7) years from the date of debut. Trainee and pre-debut periods included.

Article 2 — Company Rights

All performances, appearances, endorsements, and portrait rights managed solely by the Company. Stage name, image, and all derivative rights vest in the Company for the full term.

Article 3 — Settlement

Net receipts divided after full cost recovery; schedule guarantees at the Company’s sole discretion. No minimum annual settlement is promised.

Article 4 — Termination

Unilateral termination by the Artist constitutes breach; liquidated damages not less than five hundred million won, plus full recovery of all training and promotional outlays.

① ShiningYouth Entertainment Co.   ② Lee So-hee

Oppressive? Thoroughly — and precisely at the industry median. The template predated him, would outlive him, and had been signed by six girls he loved without anyone starving. Capitalism’s boilerplate has no feelings either way.

He had signed Han So-hee intending exactly one thing: to intercept a future star and monetize her. Pressure was the business model.

And if she never earns a cent under it — what of it? Say the Handbook someday binds her. Is a bound goddess ever going to want for money? One stray stock tip and she’s free for life.

He shelved the philosophy, decided the contract passed, rose with it, crossed to the sofa, and held it out.

“So-hee. Your artist contract. Read it — if there’s nothing wrong, sign.”

Han So-hee took the folder, skipped the reading entirely, flipped straight to the last page, and wrote her name on the line.

What the pages say has never mattered. This is sponsorship with new stationery. What matters is the timestamp — from this second on, I’m sold.

He stared, genuinely thrown. Decisive didn’t begin to cover this girl — she had personality the way a knife had an edge.

Not one question. Not a single ‘what’s clause three.’ She isn’t afraid I’ll fleece her at all, is she? …I like her.

And he laughed, because a money tree had just been planted in ShiningYouth’s front yard, and who on earth wouldn’t be pleased.

He picked up the phone and rang Lee Hye-jin directly. First ring, answered.

Phone · Song Ji-yeon → Lee Hye-jin

“Hye-jin. I’ve just signed an artist. Come up and walk her through the company — and line her up with a manager.”

“Ne, Sajangnim — on my way up this minute.”

At the new company she had been appointed manager-team leader — six or seven under her now, Jeong Soo-ah among them. The front desk’s loss was the talent division’s gain.

A NOTE ON THE JOB · WHAT “MANAGER” MEANS HERE

Do not picture Hollywood. The Peninsula’s manager is not the strategist of an artist’s empire — career planning, resource hunting, deal-making. That role barely exists here. The manager of 2012 is a life-support system with a driver’s license: meals, schedules, wardrobe bags, wake-up calls.

The status is correspondingly humble — the bottom rung of the company ladder, polished by being trodden on. A single headlining artist travels with three or four of them at once: one on the wheel, one on the crowd, one on the bags, all on the phone.

Lee Hye-jin arrived at the office door shortly, collected Han So-hee, and walked her out for the grand tour.

“Sajangnim — signing an artist this early? And an acting contract at that?”

Only with the girl out of earshot did Park Seon-young step in, diplomacy first, worry underneath.

SY Entertainment was days old. The idol business — the simplest business it had — wasn’t even running yet; the company was surviving on T-ara’s pre-booked overseas schedule while he personally hustled the follow-ons, hairline receding by the day. And now the Sajangnim had added an actress.

Film and drama resources were the scarcest currency in the entire industry. Wasn’t this just more pressure, hand-delivered?

Sure, the girl was beautiful. Since when did the industry have a beauty shortage? How many of those ever surfaced?

Song Ji-yeon smiled through it.

“No rush. I didn’t sign her to invoice her this quarter — I’m betting on the woman she becomes. No screen work yet? Then go find her modeling jobs. A face like that sitting idle is a crime against assets.”

“Oh — and find her an acting coach. Or a crash course, one of those express academies. Let her actually learn the craft.”

He genuinely hadn’t bought her for the present tense. The endorsements in that future of his were top-luxury — Boucheron’s fee alone would amortize this whole quarter’s costs, and that was one contract of many. Who sweats the tuition when the graduation gift is a fortune?

“Right — the practice rooms I asked about. Done?”

T-ara’s six had just crossed his mind: tomorrow they were in this building for their first rehearsal.

October eighteenth was next Thursday. The runway was short.

“Finished, Sajangnim — Artist Management notified them yesterday. Tomorrow, first thing, they rehearse here.”

“Good work. Go be busy.”

“Ne, Sajangnim — see you.”

Alone, with nothing urgent left, Song Ji-yeon opened his computer and pulled up the week’s market.

Monday, as always, he had run Eun-jung’s Goddess Ability — the weekly stock forecast — and this week it had pointed one direction: biotech, across the board, on the Obama administration’s stem-cell research policy. The named stock was a pharma outfit called Bukwang Pharm: seventy-five percent up this week, three straight sessions at the limit.

He did what he always did with a forecast that clean — went all in.

Worth noting: the T-ara six, facing a rehearsal week with no time to babysit brokerage apps, had simply wired him their money to manage. And the comedy department reported that Sulli and Hara had reached the same conclusion independently — after the last dinner, Hara had decided that withdrawing from her stock account to her card every time just to pay him a consulting fee was, quote, a hassle. So she had asked for his bank number on the spot and pushed the account’s entire contents across.

Sulli, constitutionally unable to let an unnie win anything alone, had matched the move — six hundred million, one transfer.

Seoul Bank SMS · Account ending in 8888 — ₩7,000,000,000 received over two sessions (T-ara six · Goo Hara · Sulli ₩600M). The management business, it turns out, runs both directions.

BUKWANG PHARM · KOSDAQ — WEEKLY FORECAST, POSITION OPENED

Capital base₩12.0B unlevered — Monday morning, before leverage

Leverage2× — ₩24.0B deployed into biotech, whole-hog

ThesisObama stem-cell policy tailwind lifting the entire biopharma complex

Forecast sourceEun-jung · Goddess Ability (weekly Stock Forecast) — 75% upside, three consecutive limit-ups

Projected exitFriday close — ₩18.0B profit on the position

Friday’s exit would land eighteen billion won of profit on a Monday decision. He closed the portfolio screen feeling like the day had gone rather well.

TUESDAY · FOUR-THIRTY ShiningYouth Entertainment — fourth floor The corridor bench outside the Sajangnim’s office

Past four he finally emerged from the office — and stopped.

Han So-hee was sitting on the corridor bench. Waiting for him.

“You’re still here?”

He’d assumed the girl had found her own way out hours ago.

She looked up, mildly furious. Why do you think I’m still here? Do I have to say it out loud?

She was, if nothing else, a woman of contract. Sold meant sold; obligations meant honored.

But the way it’s written is humiliating! Had I known, I’d have simply left. …Fine. I was afraid of being shelved. That’s all. That’s the whole reason I didn’t walk.

Song Ji-yeon took in the puffed-cheek silhouette and genuinely could not decode it. What is this one after? …Don’t tell me she’s lost. She can’t be — the office is in Cheongdam, spitting distance from ONGI; she worked the neighborhood for weeks. She knows these streets better than my accountant.

Unsolvable. But she was here, and he wasn’t made of stone.

“Up you get. I’ll drive you home.”

She blinked. Home. Mine. …So this is how the rich play it — skip his own mansion, tour the client’s basement flat first?

He watched her sit there processing something opaque, gave up on parsing it, reached down, and pulled her up by the hand.

TUESDAY · LATE AFTERNOON Sinsa-dong, Gangnam-gu Behind Dosan Park — the old alleys

Out front, into the car, engine on.

“Where’s home?”

“Sinsa-dong, that way…”

Under her direction the car crossed into Gangnam, threaded Sinsa, and finally folded itself into a tangle of old lanes behind Dosan Park, where it stopped and the scenery stopped being scenery.

Song Ji-yeon looked out at the decay and slowed down inside.

“You live here?”

“Mm.” She unclipped the belt and pushed the door open.

He followed — and watched her walk toward a narrow staircase going down instead of up. The mouth of it was black and damp, breathing mildew.

He kept after her, and when his eyes finished the arithmetic — that is a semi-basement door — the whole man went quiet.

A NOTE ON THE CITY · THE BANJIHA

Every city keeps its poverty somewhere; Seoul’s is half-underground. The banjiha — the semi-basement flat — sits below the street with one half-height window for a ceiling’s worth of sky: light at a discount, damp at no charge, everything you own smelling faintly of the season before.

The families in them are not the city’s tragedies, exactly — they are its floor. Working people, priced one rung at a time into rooms the sun declines to visit. A future director will one day make the whole world learn the word; in the autumn of 2012, it is simply where someone lives.

He knew the pre-fame version of her had been hard. He had not budgeted for this — and something in the region of his chest filed an involuntary complaint.

From the top of the stairs he could see inside: dim, one small window rationing the light, possessions stacked against walls the room never agreed to.

“This is where you live?” The shock got into his voice before he noticed it was there.

“Yes. Is there a problem with that?” She turned around, and the tone wore a lace of self-mockery over a dare.

Go on — whatever your taste runs to, the disappointment should be obvious by now. The canary you picked lives in a mouse hole. Does that dent the collection? Or has the mood already left with the lighting?

He looked at the armor she was wearing — the practiced stance of it, fitted perfectly over something much softer — and did not enjoy the view at all.

She’s my employee now, technically. My future revenue line. Decent treatment is just… maintenance.

“Pack your things. You’re coming with me.”

He said it flat, no cushioning.

Her body stiffened. One beat.

Here it comes.

A cold little laugh, somewhere behind her ribs.

Fine. No more circling. If it can’t be dodged, then let it be dodged past. I’ve already decided to fall — I refuse to perform reluctance on the way down. Think of it as a dog bite. One bite, and it’s over.

“Okay.”

One syllable. No questions, no hesitation, no asking where to — she turned and walked down into the dark.

Song Ji-yeon watched her go and found the compliance almost unsettling in its speed. Not even a destination requested.

Under two minutes later she was back up.

Her entire life, it turned out, was one half-worn backpack — straining at the zippers, worn shiny at the straps, carried on one shoulder like it weighed nothing, because mostly it did.

“Done.”

She stood in front of him and looked at him, level.

He looked at the backpack for a long moment, felt the complaint in his chest refile itself somewhere more permanent, and said exactly none of it.

“Let’s go.”

Nineteen years old. One bag. The city had taken everything else — and had been holding the receipt.

TUESDAY EVENING Cheongdam-dong — the Sajangnim’s building Five-thirty, no realtors on duty, one improvised plan

The drive back to Cheongdam ran quieter than the drive out — the air in the cabin had set somehow, like cooling wax.

The Mercedes crossed the glittering blocks and slid at last into his own apartment’s underground garage. Five-thirty-something: too late to start an apartment hunt, too early to call it a night. So the plan was simple — tomorrow, a proper small apartment; tonight, any of the dozen spare rooms one floor up. His home ran a surplus of both.

He led her into the elevator.

It rose. The sealed box held nothing but two sets of shallow breathing.

Han So-hee stood against one corner, eyes down, studying the canvas toes of her sneakers as if they’d begun to glow.

Minutes now, and I walk through a man’s front door. One man, one woman, night already outside. What happens next, I can recite from memory.

Her fist tightened on the backpack strap — knuckles going pale by degrees.

Am I afraid? …A little. But there’s no road back. And I don’t get to vote on it anyway.

Ding.

The doors parted. He walked her to his front door, thumb on the reader.

Beep.

The lock gave a small electronic chirp and withdrew. He pushed the door open and stood aside — her first.

No hesitation at all. One full breath, and she stepped through.

The entry sensor caught her and the hall lit itself — and past it the living room opened up wide and bright, a different planet from the box she’d just decommissioned.

Click-clack.

Behind her, the door shut, and the lock’s falling bolt sounded once, very clearly, in the quiet hall.

Han So-hee stood exactly where the entryway ended. Not one step further.

Her back stayed to him. When she spoke, the voice was flat enough to level a table.

“The bathroom… where is it?”

You’ve walked this far. The rest is just walking.

Song Ji-yeon blinked — a beat of pure blank — then filed it under ordinary biology and pointed at the common bathroom off the living room.

She went in. He went to the kitchen.

Dinner, he had decided, was the diplomatic option. The two of them had been running on friction since ONGI, and he couldn’t for the life of him locate the source — so feed the problem. The saying promised that the way to a man’s heart ran through his stomach; he saw no reason the anatomy differed in women.

So the Sajangnim of SAVORY F&B stood in his own kitchen, knife working, improvising a détente.

Water ran behind the bathroom door the whole while. He noticed it, filed it under long shower, and left it alone. The bathroom habits of women were a jurisdiction he had voluntarily ceded.

Some of them re-scrub the entire toilet before touching anything. If she wants to run the tap an hour, that’s her rent.

✦ ✦ ✦

Click.

The bathroom door opened.

Han So-hee came out wrapped in a bathrobe.

He heard it first, looked up second — the kitchen faced the bathroom square-on, and there she was, framed in the doorway: hair dripping dark down her back, cheeks flushed scarlet from the heat, the towel-cloth covering what it covered and nothing more — collarbones out in the open, long legs out in the open, the whole arrangement a dare aimed at physics.

Song Ji-yeon inhaled through his teeth.

What is the meaning of this. Bathing the moment she arrives — and then out in that. Is this for general viewing? Is it for my viewing?

Clatter!

The kitchen knife hit the tile — a bright, ringing crack of steel — and he had no hands free to care about it.

Because Han So-hee’s fingers had gone to the knot and pulled, and the last covering she had went the way of the towel before it — and the girl who had stepped into his entryway like a signed contract stood in his kitchen doorway wearing nothing but her hands and her blush.

She had turned away her face; the red ran from her ears down her throat; her eyes, when they found his, carried shame, fury, and one thin wire of something like blame — all three at once, none blinking.

Uh. This stimulating? Then the question is — who, exactly, is being tested here.

Anti-corruption boards of the Peninsula, take note: this is the trial. No official in recorded history has ever passed one like it.
