Chapter Fourteen

Sulli’s Dinner Invite

SEPTEMBER 2012 Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment — early evening

Meanwhile, Song Ji-yeon was still gnawing on the problem: how, exactly, was one man supposed to defuse the troll incident for Ham Eun-jung?

The phone screen lit up. An unknown number, ringing loud in the quiet apartment.

He glanced at it, thumb already drifting toward the red circle — these days every third call was a subscription pitch or a scam. But the thumb hovered, and for reasons he would never be able to explain, he pressed answer.

Incoming call · Choi Sulli → Song Ji-yeon

“Annyeonghaseyo~ Song Ji-yeon-ssi! It’s me — Choi Sulli!”

The voice came out of the receiver sparkling — a little breathless, joy pressed down at the corners and leaking out anyway. A fox kit that had gotten into the candy jar and knew it.

“Sulli-ssi? Where did you get my number?”

“I, um. Looked at your business card last time. And memorized it.”

The sparkle collapsed into something mumbly, her tempo slowing by half. She was embarrassed. Audibly. He could picture her without any effort at all — one hand wringing the hem of her shirt, lower lip bitten down over a grin.

The shyness survived approximately three seconds.

“That Samsung thing you told me about — I bought it! It jumped this afternoon! Like, a LOT! Oppa, how did you KNOW?”

“I’d done my homework. It’s a habit. …How much did you make?”

“Oh — not much! Not much at all. I only bought a little. It comes out to… one round of barbecue? Maybe one and a half?”

A pause; her breathing came closer to the microphone, like she was gathering something up. Behind her, muffled staff voices — she was hiding in some break room, making the call in secret.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. I want to buy you dinner. To say thank you. Are you free tonight?”

“I’m free. What are we eating?”

“Then… Korean BBQ? You know Umi, right? The one in Gangnam?”

Her ending went up like a kite on the last word. She was extremely pleased with her own suggestion.

“I know it. Where are you now — want me to pick you up?”

“Ne! Thank you, oppa.”

The mischief dropped straight out of her voice and left something soft and obedient behind.

Of course he knew.

Setting aside the 2012 Samsung chart that had arrived stapled to Eun-jung’s activation reward — in his old life, Samsung and Apple had spent years at each other’s throats over patents, and it had been international headline news the whole way down. The suit crawled through the courts forever; Samsung took the war in the end; the stock climbed the entire climb. None of which he had any intention of explaining to this particular trusting creature.

He’d kept it light, bland, nothing to see — homework, just a habit — because the alternative was telling silly Peach he was a returner who’d brought the future home in his luggage, and that speech ended with men in white coats.

He had laughed out loud, though. Trust her to convert her profits into barbecue units. Most people upgraded to bags, watches, cars; she upgraded to seconds. In an industry that ran on appetite suppression, a girl whose winnings were denominated in grilled meat was rarer than she knew.

And he was, in point of fact, dying of boredom. The apartment held nothing but the hum of the air conditioner and the last hard glare of a summer evening. He wanted to see her again — fine, honestly — and not only because a Goddess Ability reward was still sitting unclaimed under her name. Whatever the Handbook meant by contact, he hadn’t cracked it yet. Besides, silly Peach had asked first. Refusing would have been worse than rude.

He’d offered to pick her up because Sulli had only just turned eighteen and probably hadn’t tested for a license. On the Peninsula, trainees and idols her age moved in company vans, door to door, schedule to schedule. The ones who drove themselves were a rare species.

He hung up and assembled himself: a black casual blazer, slung over one shoulder; car keys and wallet off the coffee table. In the entryway mirror he caught his own reflection — hair a lost cause — dragged his fingers through it twice, squared the collar, switched shoes, and was gone.

SAME DAY · 6:00 PM SM Entertainment Cheongdam-dong, Seoul

At six sharp, the black Mercedes rolled to a stop outside the SM building.

His first time here.

It wasn’t what he’d pictured. No glitz, no billboard-sized logo — a respectable, buttoned-down office building that could just as easily have housed an insurance company.

Nobody at the doors, either. Fangirls knew the economics: idols lived in airports and schedule vans, and when they were actually in Seoul they were home, horizontal, unconscious. Camping the lobby was for amateurs.

Empty doorstep notwithstanding, SM Entertainment remained the star factory, full stop. Even in his last life — someone who had never properly chased the Peninsula’s scene — he could have reeled off the roster: Kim Tae-yeon, Jung Soo-yeon, Lim Yoon-ah, Victoria, Sulli… and, a few years down the line, Yoo Ji-min.

The men barely needed mentioning. The four Chinese idols who would one day leave this company for home were going to become the mainland’s first generation of untouchable megastars, each commanding a fandom in the tens of millions.

He lived five minutes away — also Cheongdam-dong, less than a kilometer as the crow flies. By the math, half the industry had to live in this neighborhood, and he had yet to collide with a single one of them at the convenience store.

He’d just gotten his phone out to call her when the front doors opened and she came out at a trot.

The sunset was at its gold. It poured over her and lit her short hair up like pale chestnut.

An off-white knit sweater, loose over jeans. A peach-shaped clip in her hair, catching the light and trembling with every step — some restless butterfly of an accessory. Her cheeks had gone faintly pink from the run, and her breathing hadn’t caught up with her feet.

She pulled the door open and let a wave of summer-evening heat into the car, tangled with a faint peach sweetness. Shampoo, probably. Or body wash. She bent at the waist and grinned at him over it, a neat row of little white teeth on display.

“Song Ji-yeon oppa! Did you wait long?”

“Just got here.” The engine turned over; the vents stirred and began pushing cool air. “Seatbelt.”

“Ne~”

She reached for the belt with both hands and buckled it — click — solemn as a schoolkid on a field trip.

He watched out of the corner of his eye and failed to keep the corner of his mouth down. Funny, thinking what a famous rebel this little peach is going to become one day.

The car slid out of the SM forecourt and merged into the evening traffic.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Umi barbecue house sat down an alley easy to miss — without the old-fashioned sign, dignified and quiet, a person could walk past it for years.

The facade was hanok: dark-brown timber pillars under pale-gray roof tile, conspicuously unmodern between the glass towers around it and entirely unbothered by the fact. The paper lanterns were already lit, their warm yellow pooling on the stone path — a soft circle of light in the dusk.
