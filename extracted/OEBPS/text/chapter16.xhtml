Chapter Sixteen

Pinky Promise

SEPTEMBER 2012 Gangnam-gu, Seoul Umi barbecue house — still the same evening

Aish. System, you absolute menace. What is “contact,” exactly? I patted her on the HEAD. The head pat — the finishing move of finishing moves — and that doesn’t count? Hair touched hand! Hand touched hair! In any court in the land, that is contact!

He gave her hair one more ruffle, purely for form’s sake — palm full of something soft and smooth, heart full of failure — then retreated, drank half a glass of ice water, and forcibly steered the conversation out of the ditch.

“Anyway. Who’s in this drama of yours?”

“Choi Min-ho-oppa. Lee Hyun-woo-oppa. And Ji-won-unnie.” She counted them off on her fingers, one-two-three. The pink hadn’t entirely left her ears. She picked the tongs back up and applied herself to the charcoal with tremendous concentration — and noticeably less dexterity than before; a slice of beef made a break for it, and her chopsticks barely recaptured it.

They talked a while longer. Outside the sliding door, the sky let itself down by degrees; inside, the charcoal glow moved over Sulli’s face and kept every one of her expressions warm.

Set stories put her in full animation. Imitating the director in a temper, she’d scrunch her nose and drop her voice two registers; recounting the scolding she’d gotten afterward, she’d shrink into her shoulders and stick out her tongue. Her whole face was a sticker set — you wanted to save every one of them.

He listened, mostly, dropping in a line here and there, and it was easy. Easier than he’d expected, being liked this simply.

The rooms around them turned their tables; laughter and glass-clinking drifted through the wall, came, went. Somewhere in there the plates emptied, and the banchan were refilled twice.

By the time they stepped out, Gangnam had gone fully over to night.

Seoul by day and Seoul by night were two different cities wearing one name. The commuting suits vanished; in their place came young people in twos and threes, neon, and queues outside every club.

He drove her home. The f(x) dorm was in Cheongdam-dong too, five minutes from the SM building, in a complex with real green space — the trees and trimmed hedges stood out even in the dark.

It wasn’t far from his own building, either. A kilometer as the crow flies, maybe less.

The Peninsula was, frankly, not big. Two people both living in Cheongdam-dong were neighbors by definition.

The seatbelt released — and in the quiet car, the click sounded like a verdict.

Sulli turned and smiled at him, moonlight coming in through the glass and softening everything it touched.

“Song Ji-yeon oppa, this is me. Today was really, really fun.”

“Me too. Next time you’re hungry, you have my number.”

“Then — next time I want to try oppa’s hotpot place. Do I have to pay?” She straightened up, grinning, a sly little light moving behind her eyes.

He gave her a flat look — while his mouth, traitorously, curved. “Eat all you want. You’d need a bigger stomach than that to eat me out of business.”

“Then it’s a deal! Next time, oppa’s hotpot.” She raised one pinky finger and held it up in front of him, expectation and seriousness written all over her face. “Pinky swear.”

Who taught her that? Pinky promises weren’t a Peninsula thing. Around here you sealed a deal verbally or with a joking high-five; a raised pinky was a first in his entire Korean lifetime.

For him, though, it was childhood — LA playgrounds and summer camp, deals struck over trading cards and notarized with linked pinkies.

He smiled despite himself — where had she even learned it? — and hooked his right pinky through hers.

And since she was waiting, chin up, he said it first, a thread of indulgence winding through his voice — the old chant, Mandarin rolling out syllable-perfect:

“Lā gōu shàng diào — yī bǎi nián bù xǔ biàn.” Pinky swear, sealed and stamped. No take-backs for a hundred years.

“Gulp!” Her eyes went perfectly round, mouth falling open a full centimeter. That was textbook Mandarin — better than textbook, not a hair of accent on it. “How does oppa know that?! I thought oppa was a local!”

She’d assumed he was local, obviously. His Korean was flawless, his manner fully assimilated, he even complained about Seoul parking like a native. But that Mandarin — Vic-unnie’s Mandarin, with the accent filed off — was suddenly confusing all the evidence.

“Korean-American,” he said, lifting their still-hooked pinkies as if presenting them to a court. “Born in LA. But I grew up one freeway away from the San Gabriel Valley, and half my friends were Chinese-American kids. Pinky-swear law is international.” A pause. “Fair warning — that chant is about ninety percent of my Mandarin. The other ten percent is ordering dumplings.”

In his memory, at least, that was the honest shape of it: born and raised in LA, Korean at home because his mother had made it house law. English was for school. Korean was for dinner tables.

“Ah! That explains it!” Sulli nodded, hard, twice — pinky still hooked to his and refusing to be surrendered.

“Who taught you the chant?”

“Vic-unnie, of course! She’s from China.” The fondness in her voice was undisguised. f(x)’s Chinese member — to Sulli she was older sister and half a mother, both at once.

“Yah!” Suddenly she was staring at him, eyes tracking from his brows to his nose to his chin like she was running some kind of internal facial recognition — and she burst out, one finger raised. “Oppa, your surname is Song. Vic-unnie’s surname is Song. Are you two FAMILY?!”

He blinked.

Well. His family’s registry went back to some county in Chungcheong that his grandfather used to drone on about, if you believed half of Grandfather’s stories. Surely that didn’t count. Surely.

But then — the world had a lot of Songs in it, and yet here were two of them, both on this peninsula, both in Seoul, both inside the same tiny entertainment bubble. Sometimes coincidence stacked so neatly it looked engineered.

His father had never once mentioned relatives in China, though. Or relatives anywhere, honestly, beyond a second cousin in Fort Lee, New Jersey.

“Probably not. My parents never mentioned any.” He came back to himself and touched her forehead lightly — fingertips to skin, fine and warm. The gesture arrived completely naturally, the way you’d steady an over-excited puppy, or head off an imagination currently sprinting in the wrong direction. “Okay. It’s late. Go up — you have filming tomorrow.”

“Ne! Good night, oppa!” Obedient nod, smile still on duty. She climbed out, then folded herself at the window — half a face, one hand waving. A kitten at a windowsill. The night wind moved her bangs off a smooth forehead.

“Night, Sulli. Sleep. When I get a free day, I’ll come find you in Yeouido.”

“Oppa better keep his word!”

He waved once and started the engine.

He waited until she had gone in through the lobby doors and the glass slid shut behind her before pulling away.

✦ ✦ ✦

LATE NIGHT Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment

By the time he was back in Cheongdam-dong, the night had gotten properly deep.

He keyed in the passcode; the entryway light — motion-sensored — woke on its own and laid the wide living room open in front of him.

Two hundred and eighty square meters. For one person, it echoed.

He hung the blazer on the rack and kneaded a shoulder that had no business being sore.

It had, admittedly, been a full day. A lap of the Star Road. A few billion won of Samsung Electronics, bought and paid for by mid-afternoon. An evening of Sulli, a stomach full of barbecue, a chauffeur run to the dorm — and one pinky promise he still couldn’t entirely believe he had participated in.

And the system’s “contact” — that riddle was exactly as far from solved as it had been at breakfast.

Head pats: rejected. Forehead: rejected. What was next on the ladder — a kiss?

He caught that thought at the door and showed it out. He had known Sulli for a week. The second meeting in a lifetime of two meetings. Kissing a girl on meeting number two wasn’t “contact.” That was a criminal offense.

Whatever. The boat would find the bridge when it got there. He would crack the system’s little riddle eventually.
