Chapter Ninety-Four

Victoria’s Apology

LATE MORNING SAVORY F&B HQ, Mapo the CEO’s office — second coffee in hand

Song Ji-yeon carried the second Americano of the morning back to his office and had barely settled into the wide leather chair when the phone in his jacket began to buzz.

He fished it out and read the screen. Park Min-suk.

Curious. Min-suk noona almost never called. She ran the dining floor at ONGI Apgujeong — the flagship’s day-to-day engine, the one person Lim Woo-hyun trusted with everything below the ceiling — and she reported the way she’d been trained to report: tidy weekly emails, floor numbers, VIP receipts, staffing notes, all filed and cross-referenced. A live phone call from her meant exactly one thing. Something at the store had grown teeth, and the boss’s mouth was required.

Phone · Park Min-suk → Song Ji-yeon

“Sajangnim! Are you free right now? There’s— there’s someone here at the store. Someone big. Asking for you by name!”

“Someone big.” One eyebrow climbed. “How big are we talking? Blue House?”

“Aigoo, Sajangnim, this is no time for jokes! It’s f(x)’s leader — Victoria! She’s sitting in the store right now, and she says— she says she’s a friend of yours. She wants to see you.”

The coffee cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

Victoria.

The image surfaced instantly: the private room, the pressed-white composure, the woman delivering the word scam like a coroner’s verdict.

How had she found ONGI? And since when were they friends? Their last encounter — the Sulli business, and an endorsement pitch he’d rushed out before the soju even existed — had ended ugly enough to be a genre. He’d assumed that was the entire relationship: one dinner, zero contact, forever. And now she’d surfaced at his own restaurant, unannounced, wearing his friendship like a borrowed name tag.

“Sajangnim? Are you still there?”

“Still here.” He surfaced from his own head, a frown assembling. “Did she come alone?”

“Yes — cap and sunglasses, but I’d know that face with the lights off. It’s absolutely her. Sajangnim, do you want me to— should I find some kind of excuse—”

Because Park Min-suk had questions of her own. Since when did the Sajangnim’s social circle include the leader of a top girl group? And why did this visit smell like a subpoena rather than a surprise? She was nobody’s fool. Friends did not track you down at your workplace — everyone in this country had a phone; people who wanted to meet texted first, picked a café, arrived on time. A woman following the trail all the way to the restaurant itself meant one thing, and Min-suk had seen far too much this quarter to rule anything out. Last time: two women, one table, one war — and the boss had walked out of it and gone straight up to the PIEN PLUS building with a bag of snacks for a third woman, then had the nerve to make Min-suk personally run hotpot delivery up to that apartment. And now an idol had tracked him to the store. Honestly. The Sajangnim’s luck with women was becoming a workplace safety issue.

“No need for excuses.” He cut the sentence off cleanly. “Walk her to a quiet room and treat her like visiting royalty — best tea service we have. I’m leaving now.”

“Ne, Sajangnim. Understood.”

He ended the call and set the coffee down with a clunk louder than the mug deserved.

What was this woman playing at?

A reckoning? He’d checked his conscience; it came back clean. Sulli’s brokerage account was out there compounding by the day — money didn’t lie, it just accumulated. What was left to be upset about? Or was this on Sulli’s behalf — a formal warning to keep his distance from their innocent little peach?

He nearly laughed. Fine — he’d admit the spokesperson hunt had been rushed. Sloppy, even. You didn’t shop for a celebrity face before the product existed; Victoria had been right about that much, and he’d conceded it to her face at the time. But his interest in Sulli herself had never been crooked. Well. Harvesting rebate cards off her didn’t count as crooked. …All right, it counted as slightly crooked. The point stood: the help was real. A kid that alive shouldn’t get the ending the last life had scheduled for her. And underneath all of it, the system had bound her — under the Handbook’s house rules, a host who wanted rewards from a goddess could not afford to cheat her. The opposite, in fact. The system demanded he go out of his way for her, and he’d made his peace with that arrangement months ago.

This woman, meanwhile, had skipped every preliminary question and gone straight to the crown: con man. In his private ranking of rude things, that sat somewhere near the summit. And now she’d turned up at his restaurant, publicly his friend, and he genuinely could not read the play.

But if she’d come all this way, refusing the audience would only look like a confession.

Whatever she was staging — he’d watch it from the front row.

Keys off the hook. Door. Gone.

✦ ✦ ✦

JUST PAST ELEVEN Apgujeong-dong, Seoul ONGI Apgujeong — the Bamboo room

A hotpot house only truly wakes at five in the evening and runs hot until ten. At eleven in the morning, ONGI sat in a hush — chairs down, broth perfuming the air like a rumor of dinner.

Song Ji-yeon pushed through the door and Park Min-suk materialized at his elbow, eyes flicking a full message toward the innermost room.

“Sajangnim. She’s inside.”

“Thanks, Min-suk noona. Back to the floor — I’ll shout if I need anything.”

He walked the corridor. Stopped.

…The Bamboo room. The Bamboo room — the one he’d booked the evening he’d tried to host Sulli and her leader, site of the shortest dinner in ONGI history. A dearly departed memory rose out of its grave and ambushed him one more time.

“Huh.” He smiled at the door, rueful. “A reunion venue. Somebody’s got a sense of occasion.”

He pushed it open.

Victoria sat bolt upright at the table, sunglasses off, the famous cool-milk face fully deployed — porcelain, precise, faintly armed. At the sound she lifted her head, and her eyes came over like searchlights.

He shut the door behind him. Skipped the pleasantries. Settled directly into the chair across from her, leaned back, folded his arms — the internationally recognized posture for talk fast, I’m busy. They had no relationship to warm up. Their one previous meeting had ended with her sweeping out and him drinking an entire pot of Da Hong Pao alone in the wreckage.

She was an idol; so what. He didn’t do fan-glasses. People were people — she’d brought the cold shoulder, and he wasn’t in the business of offering warm faces to cold ones.

“Victoria-ssi. Long time no see.” A beat, pleasant as a drawn blade. “So — what does this con man owe the honor?”

Right out of the gate, no seatbelt, straight for the wound she’d left him with. In this very room.

Victoria’s face jammed for a frame. She hadn’t expected him to open with her own verdict, served back at room temperature.

The truth was that she’d been circling this conversation since her schedules let up — she’d come home from the studios with the question still lodged under her ribs: was this man working an angle on Sulli? From Sulli herself she had extracted exactly one fact about him: he was a CEO. The size of the company never came up. But a CEO this young, who could drop a hundred-million-won watch on a girl like it was a vending machine… on the Peninsula, that arithmetic printed one answer and one answer only: chaebol second generation. And chaebol second generations had a smell on the Peninsula — a bad one. Especially the subspecies that spent family money orbiting female idols; in idol circles those names circulated like weather warnings. It was exactly why — even after the dorm-room afternoon, after the trading screen, after maybe I had him wrong — she still could not bring herself to green-light Sulli’s little friendship.

But Sulli would not hear it. And Victoria couldn’t ask Sulli for his number, because Sulli guarded her phone these days like a border crossing — request a contact, and the girl would tail her to the meeting like a duckling, guaranteed. Which left one channel: come herself. And the only address she had was this one, because Sulli had once mentioned whose restaurant it was.

She drew a long breath through her nose, wrestling the whole conversation down to a flat line.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. I didn’t come here to fight with you.”

“No?” The eyebrow went up again. “Then this is what, exactly? It can’t be urgent. I haven’t done anything lately.” And he genuinely didn’t know. One dinner, months ago. Zero contact since. There was nothing between them to even have a fight about.

“You—!” Her chest rose and fell once, hard. She balled her fists in her lap and forcibly refrigerated herself. “I have a few questions. I ask them, and I go.”

What he hasn’t done—! That watch. That WATCH. What is a watch like that even for, if not courting? The girl stares at it like a second moon! Caught, more than once, wrist under the practice-room table, smiling down at the Cartier with her cheeks in another climate entirely—

“Ask.” He settled in with the patience of a man about to enjoy himself. He was, in honesty, curious what she could build out of nothing.

She fixed him with her full attention and dropped the question one syllable at a time.

“What are you actually trying to do… to Sulli?”

He nearly laughed in her face. You drove across the city for this? Ma’am, the hotline exists. This is a matter for a text message. Maybe two.

He ran the real answer past his teeth first. In the beginning: the Goddess Handbook — pure system arithmetic, no romance in it. And underneath that, something less printable: he had watched, from very far away, what this industry did to a girl exactly this bright, and he had precisely zero intention of sitting through the rerun. Neither version was tellable. So he went with the third one.

“What can I do to her? I trade her account. I make her money. Is putting a few more Sejongs — the ten-thousand-won kind — in her pocket against the law now? Or, in Victoria-ssi’s courtroom, is making a friend rich simply the original sin?”

“That’s not—” Her volume climbed; she caught it and wrestled it down. “The con man thing. That was wrong of me. I retract it, and I’m sorry.” A breath. “I’m talking about the watch. That Cartier. Sulli is… she’s a child about people. She trusts first and checks never. I will not stand by and watch someone whose name nobody can even place play with her feelings.”

“Play with her feelings.”

Now the shape of the visit finally resolved. Not money. Never had been. The heart.

And the laugh nearly escaped again. You’re policing this hard now? Where’s this vigilance a few years down the road, madam?

Because he had thought about this more than he let on. What happened to Sulli in the last life — the family had laid the foundation for it, yes. But the structure got built afterward, plank by plank. First, the company’s porcelain-pure packaging: a personality poured into a mold and sealed, years of can’t-do and can’t-say, a bright girl serving a fiction of herself. Second, the girl growing up inside it — old enough to want out, hungry for air, for freedom, for a world that answered back. And into that pressure cooker, Victoria’s love had entered as one more lock on the door. The tighter the grip, the harder the eventual spring. Keep squeezing, and the old road comes back exactly on schedule — the clubs, the wrong people, the whole ending.

It was only 2012. From everything he’d seen, the peach hadn’t touched a club night yet. What she had done — quietly, without noticing — was make him her pressure valve. Cut that off, and he could picture with unpleasant clarity where a girl like that would go looking for air.

He let a beat of silence stand. Then he eased his voice down and let the smirk go somewhere else — for once, he wanted the words to land clean.

“Victoria-ssi. I know you’re good for her. Genuinely — I know it. But has it occurred to you that she’s an adult now? Her job is to go touch the world with her own hands. Tell good from bad on her own clock. Own her own choices — including the bad ones. You cannot keep her under your wing forever. Wings aren’t permanent housing.”

“I’m not trying to keep her under my wing forever—”

“But what you’re doing right now is building her a cage.” He cut across her, level and unhurried. “She’s not a child anymore. She has her own ideas about earning money. Her own judgment. And — this is the part everyone forgets — the right to hit a wall and pick herself back up. The tighter you fence her in, the easier she falls for the first smooth talker who leans over the fence, because she’s never once been allowed to fall down and see what the ground looks like. Let her go bump into the world a little. A few bruises beats a greenhouse and no idea what weather is.”

That was the polite version. The impolite version stayed where he kept it: the peach leaned on people — on her leader, on her unnie, now on him. She needed one square meter of her own footing, or the ending would come back around to collect.

Victoria’s mouth opened. Nothing came out. Her face went red in one patch and white in the next, like a signal flag in a crosswind.

His words were blunt enough to bruise — and every one of them had landed where she lived. Did she not want Sulli free? She wanted nothing on this earth more. But the industry’s water was the industry’s water — sink past your ankles in it and it owned you. And Sulli’s heart was a wet paper lantern. One wrong gust, and—. That was the entire reason she’d wrapped the girl in bubble wrap in the first place.

“Nothing to say?” He leaned back into the chair, hands laced over his stomach, entirely comfortable. “Then let me simplify. You don’t trust me. Fine — don’t. But if you don’t, why burn a trip? Close the account from your side. Tell Sulli to stop seeing me. One sentence. You’re her leader.”

Her lips pressed into a single thin line.

Because — and here was the thing she could not say out loud — that sentence had already been issued. Many times. Filed by the recipient under: noise. If she could actually control Choi Sulli, she would not be sitting in this room. She would not have marched into a stranger’s restaurant like an aggrieved ahjumma demanding to see the manager. It was precisely because the silly girl had decided — on faith, on vibes, on the strength of one wristwatch — that this man was one of the good ones, that the leader of f(x) had been reduced to fieldwork. And saying so out loud was impossible. It would amount to confessing that her leadership had a hole in the bottom. That in the heart of the girl she’d raised like a younger sister, her word now ranked somewhere below a hotpot CEO’s.

The room went quiet. He didn’t rush her. His fingers ticked a small idle rhythm against the tabletop — tap, tap — the only sound.

Finally, something in her finished deciding. She raised her head, and the aggression had drained out of her eyes, replaced by something rawer: confusion, and the effort of holding it together.

“You’re right. I don’t trust you.” Her voice had gone husky. “I can’t work out what’s in it for you. That’s the whole thing. That’s the question.” Her gaze fastened on his face and stayed there, sweeping for cracks. “A chaebol second-generation like you is never short of women. So why her? Why Sulli? She’s naive. She’s easy to fool. She’d follow a kind voice into a van. What exactly are you harvesting from her?”

He blinked.

Chaebol second-generation. — What kind of man am I, exactly? Handsome? Rich? Young and promising? Pick one.

And then the keywords caught up with the laughter, one by one. Chaebol second generation. Never short of women. Fixated… on Sulli. A terrible, magnificent hypothesis assembled itself in his head — ridiculous, and yet load-bearing.

He looked up at her face — the I-have-seen-through-everything face, the detective delivering her summation — and the last gear clicked into place.

This entire time. From the very first dinner. She had not been dealing with him. She had been dealing with a stock character: one of those Peninsula chaebol heirs who’ve never done a day of work, who treat women as a seasonal menu, who are handed an allowance and a title and a leash made of family name. In her eyes he’d been born with a gold spoon in his mouth, and Sulli was the innocent little rabbit the wolf had happened to single out.

He didn’t know whether to laugh or apply for a new identity. And the part that genuinely broke him — Sulli. Sulli. They’d known each other how long now? And in all that time, the girl had never once told her beloved, paranoid, hyper-vigilant unnie the single most disqualifying fact about him: that he wasn’t a native son of this Peninsula at all. That he’d told her about, in plain words, more than once. The misunderstanding had now officially outgrown the weekday melodrama. It had gone into nature documentary.

“Pfft.”

It escaped before he could civic-mindedly stop it.

She had been waiting — gravely, spine straight — for the answer. The laugh hit her like a thrown drink.

“What’s so funny? You find me funny?” Her dignity was being ground into the carpet in front of her, and the man across the table was doing the grinding.

“No. I’m not laughing at you—” He waved a hand, fighting his own face. He lost. The harder he pushed the corner of his mouth down, the higher it went; from where she sat, it could only be read one way. He shifted forward, elbows finding the table, and gave up on pretending. Whatever else this was about to be, it was going to be fun to say.

He cleared his throat.

And then he opened his mouth, and out came — not Korean.

“Tell me something, Victoria. Is there any chance you’ve got the wrong man?”

The sentence arrived in English. Not the English of Seoul cram schools — all consonants and mortification — but the real, native article, vowels worn round and easy by two decades of Southern California, delivered at the unhurried pace of a man ordering lunch in his own hometown.

Victoria’s whole body stalled.

Her ears ran the sentence back twice. English. Native English. She had come out of China at twenty, and five years inside this industry had taught her precisely one thing about listening: vocabulary can be memorized; an accent cannot be faked. No heir raised on this Peninsula spoke English that had clearly been alive somewhere.

Before the first shock finished landing, the second one came in over the top — same easy voice, same lazy rhythm, every syllable crisp:

“I’m not a chaebol, Victoria. I’m a gyopo. Born and raised in Los Angeles. Everything I have on this Peninsula, I’m building myself.”

Gyopo. American.

Her mind went white, and stayed white.

She stared at him. Eyes round. Mouth slightly open. As if the man across the table had been swapped for a stranger while she was blinking. Every structure she’d built — the chaebol heir, the playboy, the heart-hunter — collapsed in on itself at once, and when the dust settled there wasn’t a brick left standing.

A small, involuntary gulp.

Several full seconds went by before she located her voice. And then — carefully, in rusty, high-school-vintage English, one word at a time — she managed:

“You… American?”

Watching her take it — the face of a woman seeing a ghost in broad daylight and checking whether the ghost was also on the schedule — his last shred of irritation left the building. What remained was, honestly, mostly delight. He nodded, sunny and unrepentant, the grin of a man whose prank had detonated exactly on cue.

“Born and raised. Would I lie about the hometown advantage?”

Certified, stamped, genuine.

With that, the floor fell out of every deduction she had ever made about him. All of it — every cold look, every guard, the whole campaign — had stood on one foundation stone: he is a Peninsula chaebol second generation. The stone had now been removed by hand.

A gyopo who crossed an ocean to build a company from zero, and a chaebol heir leaning on the family name to do whatever he pleased — those were not two versions of one man. Those were two different species. One of them was an inspirational profile. The other was a crime blotter.

She had spent an entire season prosecuting the wrong species.

Her face went red at a speed visible to the naked eye. Not temper this time — temper would have been a mercy. This was mortification, wall to wall. She could see herself now with terrible new clarity: a woman who had marched onto a stage she didn’t understand, to perform a one-woman opera of righteous accusation, for an audience of one — who had turned out to be the one person on this Peninsula constitutionally incapable of being the villain in her script.

She wanted the floor to open. She’d have settled for a fire alarm.

And she finally understood the laugh. If their positions had been reversed — if she had delivered an entire sermon at a man and then discovered she’d gotten his country wrong — she’d have laughed too.

This had stopped being a misunderstanding three exits ago. This was performance art.

Her cheeks burning, she looked away from his eyes — from the laughter living in them — and sat there with her spine suddenly at a loss. Leaving was cowardice; staying was surgery. In the space of one sentence, the room had gone from two countries on the brink of war to a shared waiting room, excruciating in every direction.

He watched her sit there radiating the desire to dissolve, and — mercifully — declined to press the advantage. He settled back into the chair and stayed in the English, which had abruptly become neutral ground between two embassies.

“Now. Would you like the real story about Sulli and me? Properly, this time?”

“Ne!” Korean, mortified, out before she could audit it. She nodded into the general direction of her own lap.

“The short version. A little over a month ago, I was at the exchange in Yeouido opening a brokerage account. Walked out of the building, and my blood sugar simply resigned — lights out, on the sidewalk. The girl who hauled me to the hospital was Sulli. I owed her, so before we parted I pointed her at Samsung Electronics — no fee, no strings. We never exchanged numbers; I didn’t think about it twice. But — and here’s the part you’ll appreciate — while she was playing nurse, she went through the card case on my nightstand and quietly extracted a card. So when Samsung jumped, my phone rang. A thank-you dinner, she insisted. Her treat. One dinner became two. Two became the present. The rest — the account, the trades, the watch — you’ve already seen with your own eyes.”

The Goddess Handbook went unmentioned, naturally. Some tellings are for the table; that one wasn’t.

He finished, leaned back, and watched her over his crossed arms — openly entertained, a man waiting on a kettle that owed him an apology.

She held out for two more seconds. Then it all came out at once.

“I’m sorry.” Low, and it shook a little. “Truly. I’m beyond sorry.”
