Chapter Ninety-One

Bo-ram’s Flying Embrace

EVENING · 6:00 P.M. Mapo → Cheongdam-dong, Seoul rush-hour crawl

The clock turned six just as Song Ji-yeon closed out of the last thread of the day and stood up from the desk.

He scooped the briefcase off the leather blotter and dropped it on the passenger seat of the Mercedes — inside, six printed consent forms, each one tucked into its own clear sleeve, each one signed in blank at the bottom margin where the notary seal would later sit.

Tonight.

He pulled out of the SAVORY garage and straight into the evening rush, where a drive that should have been ten minutes quietly stretched itself into thirty. By the time the Mercedes nosed into the underground lot of the Cheongdam Daewoo apartment tower it was already six-thirty, the sky above the Han fully dark, the streetlights of Apgujeong-ro coming on in long wet rows.

He parked, killed the headlights, lifted the briefcase by its handle, and walked to the elevator.

Pressed the floor for Park Ji-yeon’s apartment.

The doors opened on a hallway that smelled like sesame oil and grilled pork and the soft warm starch of fresh rice. Behind the door at the end of the corridor — laughter, the clatter of a ladle against a clay pot, and rising above it all, that high-decibel dragon-shriek that could only belong to one person on this Peninsula.

Song Ji-yeon stopped at the door and let himself smile at it for half a second before he knocked.

Inside, the smell of dinner was already deep into the air. Ham Eun-jung was at the kitchen stove beside Park Hyo-min, both of them in aprons, sleeves pushed past their elbows. Eun-jung had just glanced up to check on a pot of doenjang jjigae when her eye caught something moving at the edge of the dining table — Park Ji-yeon, slinking along the wall with a pair of chopsticks that had materialized from nowhere, the tips already aimed at the plate of spicy stir-fried pork.

“Yah, Park Ji-yeon — oppa’s not here yet, don’t sneak bites, that’s rude!”

Caught red-handed, the little dragon made zero effort to look innocent. She plucked a slice of pork off the plate with the chopsticks, shoved it into her mouth, chewed loudly, and then turned her head toward the sofa where Jeon Bo-ram was sitting.

“Hehe, unnie, relax — Hyo-min-unnie made a whole mountain of food, oppa’s not gonna notice one missing slice. Bo-ram-unnie, come! Try the spicy pork! It’s amazing!”

From the sofa, Bo-ram swallowed. Her mouth was, in fact, watering. Why does the food smell this good — is this entrapment? Is this a setup? She wanted to eat. She badly wanted to eat. But the kitchen pair had already spotted her looking, and as the oldest unnie on this team she had a certain image to uphold, a certain dignity to preserve, a certain standard that did not include tiptoeing toward the pork plate before the guest of honor had even arrived.

She licked her lips once — visibly — and stood up with the slow, painful reluctance of a woman walking against her own will. She crossed her arms. She cleared her throat.

“Ji-yeon. Eun-jung-unnie’s right. The guest isn’t here yet — you can’t just steal bites like that.”

Park Ji-yeon paid exactly no attention to this lecture. She picked up another slice of pork with the chopsticks, crossed the living room in three strides, and pushed it gently, irresistibly, right up against Bo-ram’s mouth.

Bo-ram wanted to refuse. Bo-ram meant to refuse. But meat had arrived at her lips, and meat at the lips is not a thing a mortal woman refuses. She ate it.

The instant the pork touched her tongue, Park Ji-yeon burst out laughing and pointed at her like a prosecutor introducing Exhibit A.

“Unnie! Bo-ram-unnie stole bites too! You can’t just blame me — that’s unfair!”

Bo-ram, still chewing, panicked.

“Yah! Park Ji-yeon — you SHOVED it in my mouth, that’s not stealing, that’s… that’s…”

“You could’ve spat it out! I saw you chew — you loved it!”

“This… this…”

“You wanted to eat. You just didn’t have the guts.”

“No!”

Bo-ram, failing to assemble a single coherent defense, did the only thing left to her: she lunged to clap a hand over Park Ji-yeon’s mouth. The little dragon sidestepped like water.

“Nyah nyah nyah — can’t catch me! You wanted to steal bites too!”

Park Ji-yeon stuck out her tongue, broke left around the coffee table, and the chase was on. Bo-ram, shorter-legged, furious, lunging after her in tightening circles around a living room too cramped for a chase of any honest speed.

The other four members watched the circuit with the tired, fond resignation of people who had seen this exact show a hundred times before. The little dragon only ever bullied Bo-ram-unnie. And Bo-ram-unnie, in turn, had zero unnie authority to speak of.

None. Not a shred. Not after three years of this.

And then — a knock at the door.

Park Ji-yeon’s eyes lit up like somebody had flipped a switch. She forgot Bo-ram entirely and bolted for the entryway.

Bo-ram, who had not heard the knock at all, who was still in full furious chase, saw the little dragon break for the door and followed on pure instinct. Her short legs in the cramped living room were never going to catch the long-legged dragon under fair conditions — but if Ji-yeon was going to stop to open a door, that was her one opening, and she was going to take it.

Park Ji-yeon got to the door first. Threw it open. Saw who was on the other side — and ducked behind him like a child hiding behind her father’s leg.

“Yah! Where do you think you’re running!”

Song Ji-yeon had about half a second to register that the door had opened, that a small dragon-shaped figure had materialized at his back, and that the small dragon-shaped figure was shouting at somebody coming up behind him — before he turned his head to ask what in the world was going on.

He never got the question out.

A small, soft body came barreling around the doorframe at full tilt and collided straight into his chest.

He threw an arm out — instinct — and caught her at the waist.

For one whole beat nobody moved.

From Bo-ram’s side of the impact: she had timed her lunge to catch Park Ji-yeon in the exact split second the door was swinging open. When the door opened she’d been a clean thirty centimeters from the little dragon — she’d accelerated, lunged, grabbed —

— and the body she grabbed was not the soft, fragrant little dragon at all.

Whoever this was had a chest like a wall. Her nose hurt from the collision. The waist under her hands was too solid, too straight, too male. She squeezed once more — twelve different kinds of wrong — and looked up.

Song Ji-yeon looked down at her.

Her hands were on his waist. Her hands had squeezed. Twice. She had just squeezed the new owner of her agency’s waist, twice, with both hands, in front of three other members of her group, in the doorway of an apartment that smelled like dinner.

Her brain BSOD’d for three full seconds.

When it came back online, it came back online screaming.

“Ahhh!”

Bo-ram sprang off him like she’d touched a hot stove. She stumbled two steps backward, caught her heel on the shoe cabinet’s edge, and almost went down — caught herself on the wall with one flailing hand. Her face flooded red in a single rising tide, from her cheeks up through her ears, the color of a steamer lid just lifted off a fresh batch of rice. She was, head to toe, emitting heat.

“I — I didn’t mean it — I was — I was chasing Park Ji-yeon —”

She was talking in fragments, hands wringing the hem of her top, head bent so far forward her chin was nearly against her collarbone. She could not look at him. She physically could not look at him.

Song Ji-yeon, for his part, was unbothered. He straightened his jacket where her hands had crumpled it, looked down at the small, bright-red, steaming creature in front of him, and let himself smile.

“You okay? Did you fall?”

“Daebak!”

The instigator broke the silence first. Park Ji-yeon, still half-hidden behind Song Ji-yeon, was doubled over, slapping her thigh, crying with laughter.

“Bo-ram-unnie — you are so enthusiastic! We just said oppa was coming and you go straight in for the big hug! This is the legendary flying embrace I’ve heard so much about!”

Bo-ram, mortified past the point of blushing, found a new gear of fury.

“Yah! Park Ji-yeon! This is YOUR fault! Why’d you suddenly run to open the door!”

“I opened the door because oppa came.” Park Ji-yeon stuck out her tongue. “And it was YOU who didn’t brake, unnie. How is that my fault?”

“I — I —”

Bo-ram choked on the rest. Her face had graduated from red to the deep, liver-purple of a woman pushed past every limit her eldest-unnie dignity could bear.

Lee Qri and Park So-yeon, who had risen from the sofa the moment they heard the knock and were coming over to greet Song Ji-yeon properly, reached the entryway just in time to see Bo-ram spring off his chest like a rubber ball and start screaming at the little dragon.

Lee Qri took one look at Bo-ram’s I-want-to-evaporate face, glanced at Song Ji-yeon’s innocent one, and bit the inside of her cheek to keep from laughing. Then, with the perfectly straight face of a person enjoying herself enormously, she asked:

“What? What? Bo-ram-unnie, why’d you scream so loud?”

Bo-ram turned her head. Caught Lee Qri’s expression — the deliberately provoking one, the one that said who, me? with both hands raised.

There are TRAITORS on this team. Qri and Ji-yeon — they only ever bully me!!! Even with an outsider here, they won’t give their unnie one shred of face!!! I’m not living anymore!!!

Every eye in the entryway was on her. The gazes landed on her skin with weight, with heat — she could feel them, pressing on her like hands. Her eldest-unnie authority had, today, in front of Song Ji-yeon, shattered into dust. She wanted the floor of the entryway to open up and swallow her whole, apartment and all.

“Yah! Lee Qri — don’t you start!”

She stomped one foot. Did not look at Song Ji-yeon.

Park So-yeon, ever the diplomat, stepped in and laid a palm between Bo-ram’s shoulder blades, patting gently the way one calms a cat that has just been forced into a bath.

“OK, OK — Ji-yeon and Qri just like to joke, Song Ji-yeon-ssi, please don’t mind them.” She turned to him with the polite, slightly exhausted smile of a woman who had been herding these cats for years. “Please come in. Eun-jung and Hyo-min are done cooking — we’ve been waiting for you.”

Eun-jung and Park Hyo-min emerged from the kitchen, Eun-jung untying her apron strings. She took in the entryway — Bo-ram purple, Ji-yeon giggling, Qri innocent, So-yeon herding — and correctly diagnosed the entire situation in under a second.

“Oppa, you’re here, come sit.” She shot a look at the little dragon. “Ji-yeon just goofs off, ignore her.”

Park So-yeon, in the meantime, had reached over and thrown an arm around the still-giggling Park Ji-yeon’s neck — the little dragon was hiding behind Song Ji-yeon’s back, peeking out like a meerkat — and pretzeled her into a gentle, fake-threatening headlock.

“Yah, you little dragon — bullying Bo-ram-unnie again, just wait —”

“Ah! So-yeon-unnie, I was wrong! I won’t do it again!”

Song Ji-yeon shook his head, smiling, and let the tide of them carry him into the apartment.

“It’s fine, no need to be so formal.”

He toed off his shoes, slid into the offered slippers, and let his eyes wander over the apartment — the warm yellow light, the table already laid out with side dishes and stacked bowls, the smell of sesame and gochujang and freshly steamed rice braided through the air.

“Wow — all this. Thank you for the trouble.”

“Hyo-min did most of it,” Eun-jung said. “I just assisted.”

She’d wanted to cook this dinner alone. But she and Ji-yeon had spent the whole afternoon at the securities center opening Ji-yeon’s brokerage account, and then gone grocery shopping, and gotten back to the apartment close to five — only to find the other four members already waiting for them. Hyo-min, the team’s designated chef, had taken one look at the groceries, taken one look at the clock, rolled up her sleeves and taken over.

Hyo-min’s head poked out of the kitchen, a dish towel over one shoulder.

“When the Sajangnim’s coming, we go all-out! Sit, sit — one more soup, almost ready.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Song Ji-yeon was ushered to the head of the table. The five girls arranged themselves around him in a loose half-circle, chairs scraping amiably against the floor. Jeon Bo-ram, with the careful tactical precision of a woman executing a retreat, chose the seat farthest from the head of the table, sat down, and lowered her head until she was ostensibly deep in the study of her own rice bowl. The tips of her ears were still bright red.

Too humiliating. Why me. Why, of all people, me.

Jeon Bo-ram, at this moment in the timeline of her life, was technically alive, spiritually deceased.

The last of the food came out. The table was full — spicy stir-fried pork, a round golden seafood pancake cut into wedges, three kinds of kimchi, a clay pot of doenjang jjigae still bubbling at the rim, and a separate small bowl of seasoned spinach that nobody had ordered but Hyo-min had made anyway because she was, as advertised, the team’s designated chef.

Song Ji-yeon surveyed the spread. In his head, Korean food had always been a two-item category — kimchi and ramyeon. That was the entire folder. It was, frankly, an embarrassing opinion for a man who ran a restaurant group, but a man who had grown up in a Koreatown family in LA could be forgiven for thinking he knew what Korean food was from the inside out — and therefore, paradoxically, for never having properly tasted it.

Eun-jung ladled him a bowl of the doenjang jjigae herself, and reached across the table to pick up the kimchi from the farthest spot, settling both in front of him.

“Oppa, try Hyo-min’s cooking — she’s our team chef.”

“Mm.” He picked up the spoon, took a slow spoonful of the jjigae, and set it back down. Nodded once.

It was, in fact, good. Not politely good. Actually good. The broth had that depth that comes from a paste that has been simmered with dried anchovies and dried shiitake and a daft amount of patience. The preconceptions he’d carried into this room were, file by file, being deleted.

“Delicious. You could open a restaurant with this.”

He meant it. And in the back of his head — from his previous life, from the long ago of late-night YouTube rabbit holes and allkpop archive binges — he remembered, faintly, that Park Hyo-min had been the best cook in T-ara. That she’d held an actual culinary certificate. That the fandom had a small, devoted faction whose entire content was Hyo-min cooking things.

No exaggeration, apparently. None.

Hyo-min, at the other end of the table, beamed so wide her eyes folded into moons.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi, glad you like it — eat up, eat up!”

Slowly, the table loosened. The initial stiffness of a dinner where the host was about to become the boss melted off, plate by plate, sip by sip. The girls were eating and talking over each other in the easy, sliding register of members who had shared a dorm kitchen for four years. Bo-ram, despite herself, took a second helping of the pork. Then a third.

And Park Ji-yeon — the team’s appointed mood-engine, the atmosphere-maker, the dragon whose entire job in any room was to break silences for a living — could not, in the end, hold herself back.

She poked a chopstick into her rice, eyes shining up at Song Ji-yeon.

“Oppa — you really bought us? We’re really your company’s artists now?”

Song Ji-yeon understood exactly what was happening. The little dragon knew the answer already — she’d spent the afternoon at the securities center with Eun-jung, she’d been on the inside of this deal since the first day — but she was asking anyway, in front of the others, because she was teeing him up. Opening the topic for him. The kid was doing him a favor and pretending she wasn’t.

He smiled at her, grateful.

“Haven’t signed yet, strictly speaking. But soon.” He set his chopsticks down, took the conversational baton she was handing him. “The company’s called ShiningYouth. New company, just registered. For now, T-ara is the only group on the roster.”

A small ripple moved around the table at the word new. The faces shifted — Bo-ram’s chopsticks paused, mid-air, over a slice of pork. Hyo-min tilted her head. Park So-yeon’s eyes, calm and watchful, narrowed a fraction.

Lee Qri, the most direct of the six, didn’t make anyone wait for the question.

“Sajangnim, forgive me for asking — a new company… won’t resources and channels be difficult?”

It was a fair question. The Korean entertainment industry was, in 2012, one of the most developed on the planet, with an infrastructure that ate small agencies for breakfast. A new company with no history, no network, no in-house producer and no broadcast ties, trying to break a group — much less a group currently in the eye of a bullying-rumor storm — would, by every reasonable industry model, fail to clear the first hurdle.

Park So-yeon, leader’s instinct, took the next sentence up.

“Qri-unnie means — we’re grateful you’d sign us, Sajangnim. But we’re also worried about future development. Especially the China market. We don’t know it at all. Does the company have plans there? Connections?”

She’d been the leader long enough to ask it carefully. But the worry under the question was plain — they had agreed, weeks ago, when Song Ji-yeon had first floated the China plan, almost as a joke. They’d said yes easily because at the time saying yes cost them nothing. Now the papers were on the table. Now saying yes meant signing.

Song Ji-yeon nodded. He’d been waiting for this question since he sat down.

“Your concerns — I understand them. All of them.”

“First, resources. The new company is thin at the base, that’s true. But money is not the problem. I’ve injected four billion won as starting capital — and there’s more where that came from. In Korea, in the initial phase, we stay low-profile. Stable. We don’t move loudly.”

“Second — and this is the most important — the China market. I founded this company to sign you for one explicit goal: China. I have connections there. I have resources there. I can’t promise you’ll skyrocket overnight — no honest person would promise that. But I can pave the early road for you. I can find the right platforms, the right promo channels, the right first foothold. That part, I will handle.”

He paused. Looked around the table, one face at a time.

“Your job is this: get your heads right, study Mandarin, and prep new music. Leave the rest to me.”

I’m not joking. I know exactly what I’m promising them. I’m holding TikTok’s underlying source code in my head — every line of it, where it’s going, what it will become. The moment T-ara is settled and the contracts are dry, I make a trip back to LA, and I sell that nuclear weapon for the price it deserves. Then the resources I’m promising tonight won’t be empty talk.

None of them fully followed. They didn’t need to. They’d heard enough. He says money isn’t the problem. He says he has the China connections. He says he’ll pave the road. If even half of it was true, they had a road out — and the Peninsula market was no longer a road at all. The domestic scene had frozen over after the bullying scandal. Schedules were empty. Variety appearances had dried up. The overseas goodwill that T-ara had spent five years building in Japan was cratering by the week as the rumor mill kept turning. Without a way to break out of the Peninsula, the only end-state left to them was disbandment.

So if Song Ji-yeon could guarantee development and stages after the transfer, they would try. They had no other road. None of them had any other road.

Song Ji-yeon judged the moment ripe. He set his chopsticks down, pushed his chair back, stood up, and walked to the coffee table where he’d set the briefcase. He popped the clasps. From inside, six identical documents — each in its own clear sleeve — and laid one in front of each member of T-ara.

“These are the contract-transfer consent forms.”

His voice wasn’t loud. It didn’t need to be. The table went silent on its own — chopsticks set down, glasses set down, every pair of eyes landing on the clean white paper in front of each of them.

TALENT MANAGEMENT CONTRACT TRANSFER CONSENT FORM

Declaration of Transfer

The undersigned artist voluntarily transfers all rights and obligations of her exclusive talent-management contract from CCM Entertainment Co., Ltd. to ShiningYouth Entertainment, a wholly-owned subsidiary of SAVORY F&B.

Signatories

① Ham Eun-jung   ② Park Ji-yeon   ③ Jeon Bo-ram

④ Park Hyo-min   ⑤ Lee Qri   ⑥ Park So-yeon

All transfer fees settled in full at signing · executed this date at the Cheongdam Daewoo apartment, Cheongdam-dong, Seoul.

He set a fountain pen and a small red ink pad in the center of the table.

“Have a read. If it’s fine, sign. As of today — welcome to the ShiningYouth family.”

Ham Eun-jung, sitting at his right hand, picked hers up first. She read it carefully, line by line, the way she did everything — fully, slowly, without cutting corners. The clause was simple. A standard legal instrument. A declaration that she, of her own free will, transferred her exclusive representation contract from CCM Entertainment to ShiningYouth.

That was all. Sign here. Press here. Walk out of the grey past.

She finished reading. Looked up at Song Ji-yeon. Her eyes were steady, the smallest warm flicker at the edge of them. She picked up the pen, set the nib to the signature line, and wrote her name in careful, even strokes — stroke by stroke, the way a woman signs when she has decided something and means it. Then she dipped her thumb in the red ink and pressed it, firm and clean, into the thumbprint box.

“Once decided, I don’t hesitate. I’ll sign first.”

She’d already decided long ago. Long before tonight, long before the securities center, long before this apartment. She’d already given him the rest of her, the whole rest of her — what was a contract? She wasn’t afraid he’d cheat her. He wouldn’t cheat her. She knew that down in the marrow.

Park Ji-yeon signed second. She barely read the form — she didn’t need to. She knew oppa wouldn’t cheat her. She picked up the pen and scratched her name down in bold, wide strokes, the kind of handwriting that belongs to a woman who has never doubted a person in her life. The thumbprint followed, smudged slightly at one edge because she pressed too hard. She grinned at Song Ji-yeon across the table, the dragon-grin, all teeth.

Jeon Bo-ram and Park Hyo-min signed next, in tandem. They had reservations — of course they had reservations, both of them; Bo-ram’s ears were still pink from the entryway. But they were a team. The other two had already signed. The maknae had signed. Eun-jung-unnie had signed. If they weren’t afraid, what was there to be afraid of. And no matter what came next, it could not be worse than now — and a new company, even a thin one, was at least a chance. A chance to break out. They picked up the pen, wrote their names, pressed the pad. Done.

Lee Qri and Park So-yeon signed last. They were the most cautious of the six — would remain the most cautious of the six for as long as the group existed — and the promises of resources were still, in their heads, promises and not facts. But they reasoned it out cleanly, the way two practical women reason it out: Song Ji-yeon had just spent four billion won to acquire them. Nobody sinks four billion into a flower vase. If he’d paid that price, he would move heaven and earth to find them work, to earn the money back. And if, in the end, the resources did not materialize — well, a contract was a contract, and a court was a court, and an artist who had been lied to could always sue for termination.

They signed. They pressed.

Six forms. Six signatures. Six red thumbprints.

The dust settled.
