Chapter Forty-Four

A Goddess Chased by a Dog

SIX THAT EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the evening walk

Song Ji-yeon had assumed dog ownership was a two-skill job: feed it, walk it. Half a day of hands-on experience had thoroughly revised the estimate. Mushroom’s energy was beyond anything he’d imagined — the animal spent the day ricocheting around the apartment like a white fuzzy billiard ball — and then there were the incidents. Plural. Unsanctioned, on the floor, in the corner behind the sofa. His blood pressure had left the building.

But he was the one who’d said sure, bring him over. He had volunteered for this particular little tyrant, and so there was nothing to be done except what grown-ups do: grab the paper towels, keep his face neutral, and clean it up himself.

At six o’clock sharp, Mushroom trotted up to him with the leash in his mouth and set it down, ceremonially, on top of his slipper.

Then he sat, and looked up.

Man and dog locked eyes in silence.

Song Ji-yeon understood the message perfectly. He also did not wish to move. He therefore elected to perceive nothing — no leash, no dog, no six o’clock — and went back to his phone, lying there like a man with no appointments and no shame.

“Woof.”

No response from the human. “Woof. Woof. Woof.” Reminder after reminder, escalating in urgency, aimed at the lazy staff.

Song Ji-yeon cracked first. He rolled over with a sigh.

“I owe you something, clearly. Fine. Walk.”

He locked the phone and pocketed it, changed into a black zip-up hoodie, and pulled the hood up over his head. Mushroom was carried to the entryway, where Song Ji-yeon crouched to clip on the leash — a procedure Mushroom interpreted as wrestling practice. Front paws up on his knees, head straining toward the door with the force of a departing train.

“What’s the rush.” He pressed the squirming head still and clicked the buckle shut. “Your owner gave me strict instructions — the leash stays on. If I lose you, she’ll latch onto me for life, and then neither of us will ever know peace.”

Mushroom understood not one word, and wagged at all of them.

The evening air in Cheongdam-dong had a cool edge to it. The sidewalks outside the complex were laid in pale-gray brick, the streetlamps had just come on, and the shadows of the trees lay across the pavement, swaying with the wind. Mushroom hit the street at maximum excitement — nose down, tail up, cataloging the entire block one lamppost at a time, every roadside patch of grass deserving a full scholarly inspection. Song Ji-yeon followed at the end of the leash, inert with the special exhaustion of a father walking a toddler who has just discovered legs.

“Mushroom.”

“Slow down.”

He looped them once around the neighborhood — and somewhere in the loop, his feet made an executive decision, and he looked up to find himself standing in SM Entertainment’s block.

It wasn’t his first time here. He’d come by once before, to pick up Sulli, and had lingered roughly ninety seconds before the car doors swallowed him again. This was the first time he’d actually stopped and looked at one of the Peninsula’s Big Three.

Eight stories of deep-blue reflective glass, curtain-walled from corner to corner, set off by gray cylindrical columns along the flank. Say what you liked about SM — the building had presence. Considerably more presence, he couldn’t help noting, than a certain four-story SAVORY F&B office in Mapo.

When the money comes in, I’m putting up a tower of my own. Taller than this one. Bigger, too. The ambition rose in him, uncomplicated and enormous. If Lee Soo-man could do it, there was no clause in the universe saying Song Ji-yeon couldn’t.

Mushroom, for his part, did not care about his human’s five-year plan. He cared that the walk had stopped, and he lodged a formal complaint about it — planting all four feet and hauling forward with everything his little body had.

What neither of them had accounted for was that Song Ji-yeon hadn’t actually wrapped the leash. One good surge, and the line snaked clean out of his loose grip — and Mushroom was FREE, rocketing up the sidewalk at top speed.

The dog, if he could have filed a grievance on the spot: Excuse me? Did your owner not specifically instruct you to hold the leash?

Song Ji-yeon’s hand closed on air. He froze for half a second — then tracked the trajectory.

By the entrance of the convenience store up ahead stood a girl in a gray hoodie, a plastic bag of purchases hanging from one hand, cap pulled low over most of her face. Low as it was, he recognized her in one glance.

Bae Joo-hyun.

“Block him!” he called out to her.

She had spotted him too — he saw the small start of recognition cross what was visible of her face. Then his shout registered, and her eyes went past him to the white missile incoming at street level, and every system in her body shut down at once.

Mushroom had also acquired the target. He let out a thrilled “WOOF!” and made his final approach, dead straight at Bae Joo-hyun.

The convenience-store bag nearly hit the pavement. Her shoulders jumped up around her ears; she stumbled back a step.

“Yah!”

This only encouraged him. Mushroom came on harder, leash clattering and skipping along the ground behind him, short legs a blur of joy. She gave up two more steps, and her voice lost every trace of its usual cool — it came out thin and bright with panic.

“Don’t come over here!”

But every step she retreated read, in dog, as an invitation to play. The tail went to propeller speed. Bae Joo-hyun turned and ran.

Song Ji-yeon stood where he was, watching the whole thing unfold with his mouth slightly open.

Wait. Hold on. She’s afraid of dogs?

Here was the honest accounting of the moment: when the leash had slipped, he could have caught Mushroom easily — two strides, done. But he’d seen Bae Joo-hyun standing right there in the dog’s path, and the thought had arrived fully formed and extremely lazy: let her block him. He hadn’t wanted to move.

Who could possibly have foreseen — Bae Joo-hyun. Afraid of dogs!!!

This was a crime against nature. He was complicit in a crime against nature.

She wasn’t even running well — arms in, bag flailing, choosing directions at random. Mushroom pursued at a happy yapping gallop, leash whipping along the ground behind him, and the two of them achieved a grim equilibrium: girl fleeing in genuine terror, dog maintaining a considerate two-meter gap because — whatever it looked like — he wasn’t trying to catch anyone. This was a game. The game was going beautifully.

Song Ji-yeon’s legs finally rejoined the operation. He broke into a run.

“Mushroom!”

Mushroom did not acknowledge the summons. Bae Joo-hyun glanced back over her shoulder, registered that the dog had gained, and her eyes filled edge to edge with a silent SOS.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi! Get him away from me!”

She remembered him, then — the young CEO from the café, the one meeting, the fifty cups of drinks.

Mid-sentence, Mushroom arrived at her heels. She yelped and leapt in place — and began sprinting laps around the bench at the curb. Mushroom, delighted by the new rules, chased the laps with the leash streaming out behind him. Song Ji-yeon covered the distance in three long strides, tracked the wildly whipping leash across the pavement, and snatched the handle clean off the ground.

Freeze that frame, and behold it: one girl terrified of dogs, sprinting laps around a bench; one small white Maltese, certain he is at the park with his new best friend; and one Song Ji-yeon, finally in possession of the leash.

Two young men passing by had stopped to watch, their eyes pinging between all parties like spectators at a match. One of them had his phone halfway out of his pocket.

Song Ji-yeon’s face went a shade darker. Today’s dignity losses: significant. Potentially viral.

He wrapped the leash twice around his fist and hauled back, hard. Mushroom was anchored at last — four paws still scrabbling toward Bae Joo-hyun, piteous complaints pouring out of him. Bae Joo-hyun had her back against the bench now, one hand pressed flat to her chest, her breathing in visible disarray. She stared at the dog. The dog stared back at her.

Song Ji-yeon took in the tableau — and the laugh arrived before he could put a stop to it.

“What’s so funny?”

The glare was instantaneous. She had been genuinely frightened — badly. She’d been scared of anything with fur since she was a child: dogs, yes; cats, too; the entire furry kingdom, no exceptions.

“Nothing.” He waved a hand, his face a portrait of professionalism.

“You laughed. I saw it.”

“I was only thinking — Mushroom seems to like you.”

“I don’t need him to like me.” Back in under a heartbeat.

The dog barked at her again. Her shoulders flinched — and before she could think about it, she had ducked behind Song Ji-yeon, one hand fisted in the cuff of his hoodie. He glanced down. Half her face was visible past his shoulder, eyes locked warily on the dog, her whole body drawn tight as a startled cat.

And this is the ice-cold Bae Joo-hyun?

“He doesn’t bite.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you—”

“Knowing something and being afraid of it are two different things.” Her voice came out iron. The hand on his sleeve did not loosen by a single millimeter.

He let his eyes drop to that hand. It was fair, the fingers slender — and gripping hard enough that the knuckles had gone faintly white. Bae Joo-hyun followed his gaze down to her own fist, understood at once what he was looking at, and released him as if the sleeve had been red-hot. She cleared her throat, straightened her spine, and reassembled, item by item, the composure she was known for.

“That wasn’t — I didn’t do that on purpose.”

“I didn’t say anything.” He was smiling as he said it. Getting grabbed and dragged around by the industry’s future god-tier visual — he found, on reflection, that his mood was excellent.

“Your face said it.” She pursed her lips, genuinely aggrieved now. His dog had terrified her, and somehow she was the one standing here apologizing for a sleeve. What kind of person was this?

Song Ji-yeon raised an eyebrow. He had detected no fault whatsoever in his own expression, and he was curious to hear the charge.

“What did my face say?”

“It gloated.”

He had no answer to that. I mean — yes, I am, at heart, a man who enjoys watching the world’s drama unfold from a good seat. But you can’t just SAY that to my face.

“You’re seeing things.”

“I saw exactly what I saw. You stood there gloating while a dog chased me.” She said it with the total certainty of a witness reading into the record.

He was gearing up a further defense when Mushroom, tired of being the only one working, spun two impatient circles and tried once more to sidle toward her. The color left her face on the spot; she gave up half a step. This time Song Ji-yeon had the leash pre-tightened in his fist, and the dog gained no further ground.

“Mushroom. Sit.”

The dog did not sit.

Song Ji-yeon looked down at him. Mushroom looked up at Song Ji-yeon.

Several seconds went by.

He gave up and waved a hand over the whole affair.

“Aish. He doesn’t listen to me either. I’m out of moves.”
