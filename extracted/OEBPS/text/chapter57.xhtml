Chapter Fifty-Seven

Stock Predictions

MONDAY MORNING Mapo-gu, Seoul SAVORY F&B — third-floor conference room

Kang Jin-ho had barely reclaimed his chair when the next item came down the table.

“Next — sales. Last week’s numbers.”

There was only one set of numbers that mattered, and everyone in the room knew it. SAVORY F&B was, for now, a one-brand company — ONGI was the whole enterprise — which made ONGI’s trading the one report Song Ji-yeon genuinely listened to.

The head of the sales division stood: Lim Min-ju, a composed woman in her thirties holding a printed data sheet she had clearly checked three times over.

“Sajangnim. The seven directly operated stores.” One small breath. “Apgujeong first — daily average of ₩4.8 million last week, with both weekend days breaking ₩6 million.”

Ji-yeon gave a small nod and let her run.

“Hongdae — daily average ₩4.2 million, single-day weekend peak of ₩5.5 million. With the universities back in session, student traffic is climbing visibly, but the average spend is running under the district norm. Our proposal is a lunch set aimed at the student crowd, priced around ₩50,000 — it pulls them in the door without denting the overall gross margin.”

“That thinking works.”

He approved it on the spot. In his old life, Haidilao — the service-first hotpot empire he’d studied half to death — had run a university campaign: flash a student ID and thirty-some percent fell off the bill. It had marched students through the doors in battalions. The playbook was sound; there was no reason it wouldn’t rhyme in Seoul.

“But watch the set-menu costing — I’m not running a charity. At ₩50,000, gross margin holds at sixty percent minimum. Anything under that, the menu doesn’t ship.”

“Ne, Sajangnim.”

Lim Min-ju ran through the remaining five stores — results, problem spots, the odd hiccup. The numbers rhymed: daily averages holding between ₩4 and ₩5 million a store, weekend peaks shoving past ₩6 million.

Taken together, the seven stores were clearing ₩30 million a day.

He ran the arithmetic in his head at reading speed. On this pace the seven stores would book nine hundred million to a billion won a month — and once food costs, payroll, rent, and utilities took their bite, something like ₩200 to ₩300 million of that would survive as profit.

By the standards of the restaurant industry, that was a quietly spectacular number. By the standards of Song Ji-yeon, it was a crawl.

ONGI’s operating profit was the engine of every expansion plan he had — and ₩300 million a month didn’t cover the opening costs of a single new store. Every additional door meant topping the company up out of his own pocket.

It made his teeth ache.

On paper, there was an easy answer: outside money. At ONGI’s scale and trajectory, investors would form a line around the block. But outside money arrived holding hands, and the chaebols of this country were not people a company his size survived offending. Open the financing door even a crack, and inside a few years little SAVORY F&B would be one line in the CJ food empire’s brochure — acquired, assimilated, renamed, gone.

So: no financing. Which left two doors. A capital injection — and the family war chest was already scraped to the boards. Or bank lending — asset audits, credit checks, guarantors, one stamp chase after another; the money would land somewhere around the next ice age.

Aigoo. Nobody warns you how expensive the chaebol dream actually is.

Fine. Slow expansion it was — one door at a time, on ONGI’s own steam.

He shelved the whole meditation and lifted his eyes to the table.

“Last week’s numbers were good. Genuinely. But this is only the beginning — the brand is a seedling, and nobody relaxes around a seedling. Three requirements before you leave this room.”

Around the table, spines straightened on cue.

“One — quality control does not slip. Broth bases and core ingredients follow company standard, to the gram. There will be unannounced inspections. Anyone caught skimping, the store manager clears his desk that same day.”

“Two — service keeps climbing. We are a premium service house, not a street tent. From the moment a guest is greeted to the moment they’re bowed back out, every touchpoint says you are respected here. Logistics and training — the full service-flow standards get documented this week and distributed to every store.”

Kwon Hyeok-eun, head of logistics and training, nodded and started writing at speed.

“Three —” a pause, and the table leaned in half an inch — “no silos. If one store invents a hit dish, every store knows it by Friday. A service innovation, a cost-control trick — it belongs to all seven. I don’t want seven separate restaurants. I want one company wearing seven doors.”

The meeting broke a little past ten-thirty. The department heads filed out; the conference room drained down to three — Ji-yeon, Park Seon-young, Kim Min-hyun.

“Sajangnim — one more item.” Park Seon-young closed his notebook and chose his words with visible care. “Several malls have approached us about putting an ONGI inside. COEX Mall’s package is genuinely attractive — first year rent-free, plus a fit-out subsidy. Do we want a look?”

Ji-yeon didn’t answer right away. He let the two sides of it argue in his head.

A mall store came with its foot traffic baked in — no anxious months waiting for a neighborhood to learn your name. But the same deal shipped with handcuffs: hours set by the landlord, rents with no give in them, and a long lease the mall could hold over the brand’s head the moment momentum dipped. Get tangled in that before the model was proven, and you didn’t run a brand — the mall ran you.

“Not yet. Make the Cheongdam flagship bulletproof first — run the premium single-store model end to end until it hums. When that one’s steady, we talk about the next ten doors.”

“Understood.”

Park Seon-young gathered his things and went. Only then did Kim Min-hyun speak.

“Sajangnim — Kim Kwang-soo still won’t budge. He’s holding firm at ₩4 billion for T-ara’s contracts. The ₩4 billion does include full copyright to every song from their three years since debut, and the secondary-distribution rights on all the MVs—”

The glasses got their familiar, troubled push up the bridge of his nose.

“But the finance department has run its assessment, and frankly — acquiring T-ara doesn’t align with this company’s development needs. Sajangnim. Reconsider.”

“Buying T-ara is a personal matter. Nothing to do with the company — and I won’t move one won of company money to do it.”

He reached for the water bottle on the table, cracked the cap with more force than the plastic deserved, and swallowed his irritation down along with two mouthfuls.

“But fine — four billion is four billion. Close out Kim Kwang-soo before this week is out, Min-hyun-ah. I want good news waiting for me next week.”

“Ne, Sajangnim. If there’s nothing else, I’ll get back to it.”

A bow, and the door clicked shut behind him.

STILL MONDAY MORNING Mapo, Seoul SAVORY F&B — the president’s office

Back in his own office, Song Ji-yeon shut the door on the world.

He dropped into the executive chair and didn’t reach for the laptop. Eyes closed. Thirty quiet seconds, the day’s chessboard arranging itself behind his eyelids.

Then his eyes opened, his fingers flexed once, and the laptop came up. Brokerage. Account. Password. The page turned over, and there it was — the Samsung Electronics line, the day’s quotes ticking gently upward, alive.

He’d gone in at the top of the month with four billion of his own and doubled it on margin; the rally had climbed the exact staircase in his memory, and three weeks in the saddle had compounded into close to ₩1.2 billion in paper profit — paper that was overdue for conversion into the real thing.

The order window blinked up its confirmation, patient and official: Sell entire position — Samsung Electronics 2× leveraged ETF · 40,000 units. Confirm?

He hit Confirm without a hair of hesitation.

The system chewed on the order for a dozen seconds. Then the trade-complete notice blinked in, and he refreshed the balance: the number settled at ₩5,230,000,000.

Twenty-odd days. Four billion in, five point two three billion out — the margin repaid, the profit his own, real and spendable. He looked at the figure a moment, and the corner of his mouth went up on its own authority.

₩5.23 billion — and with the company’s liquid funds stacked on top, the capital he could actually mobilize was brushing ten billion won. In ordinary-person arithmetic, that was two very nice Gangnam apartments and a lifetime of comfortable idling.

For Song Ji-yeon, it was the starting line.

He closed the trade window, fished his phone out of the desk drawer, and called up the Goddess Handbook.

Ham Eun-jung’s golden card floated serenely at the center of the page. Below it, in the card wallet, two dark-gold Stock Forecast Cards lay side by side, a faint sheen circling their edges like slow syrup.

Two cards. Two weeks of ammunition.

He extended one index finger and tapped a card — lightly, the way you wake something sleeping.

The card dissolved into a streak of light, shot straight up out of the screen, and bloomed in front of his eyes into a panel of translucent glass. Rows of data raced down it, faster than reading, faster than sense — and locked.

A blue ticker. Thirty-seven point eight percent. Five sessions.

Song Ji-yeon sat back slowly, and the smile that had been a corner-of-the-mouth affair graduated into the real thing.
