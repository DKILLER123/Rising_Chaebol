Chapter Eighty-Three

Almost Something

SUNDAY · AFTERNOON INTO EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Park Ji-yeon’s study → first-floor kitchen → the sofa

“Oppa. THIS is your ‘carrying me’?”

“Lag! That round didn’t count—”

“We’ve lost FIVE in a row. You haven’t carried once. I’m done. This isn’t fun anymore.”

Park Ji-yeon glowered at the screen, where the word DEFEAT glowed an insolent red for the fifth consecutive time. She dropped her headset on the desk, drew her knees up to her chest, wrapped her arms around her shins, buried her chin on her knees, and shrank into the gaming chair — a dragon in a nest, pouting at the monitor.

She was genuinely wounded.

Today was supposed to be her showcase. Flex on oppa, demonstrate the hidden depths of Park Ji-yeon’s mechanics. Instead, five games in, she had nearly become the savior of every match — while oppa got ganked, got solo-killed, and contributed team fights roughly on the level of a particularly ambitious ward.

“You picked Yasuo and E-queried your way directly under their turret. Who survives that? WHO?” She twisted the knife further, warming to her grievance. “And that Lee Sin game — you flash-kicked their tank INTO our carry. I just…”

Ji-yeon rubbed his nose, sheepish.

He couldn’t tell whether he was rusty or Korean solo queue was simply built different. Either way: five losses.

In fairness his own stats were decent — solid, unspectacular, neither dragging her down nor lifting her up. A couple of team fights had gone sideways, and the rest was luck. She roasted him and he didn’t mind. Games were games; he didn’t play often enough to have an ego invested.

If anything, I’ve done the dragon’s digital health a service. Five rounds of watching me feed — she may yet quit gaming on her own.

His mouth curved at the thought — and she caught it, snapping upright.

“You’re SMILING? We lost five straight and you’re smiling?”

“Win some, lose some. Perspective.” He spread his hands, magnificently unbothered.

“Perspective! Sure! Easy for YOU — you’re the one who fed, not me!” She turned her face away with a huff, though her peripheral vision stayed fully deployed on him.

She was too cute when she sulked, so he let her have it and checked the clock in the corner of the screen. Past five.

Games really did eat time alive — five matches, three-plus hours, gone.

The light outside had gone honeyed. Sunset pushed through the curtain gap and laid a long gold stripe across the floor.

“Alright, that’s enough.” He stood and patted her head on the way up. “It’s late. I owe a kitchen an apology — dinner time.”

Lunch had been after eleven; five-something was squarely dinner-prep territory.

She was mid-sulk, but at the word dinner her ears rotated independently toward him like satellite dishes. Her mouth stayed on duty, though.

“Hmph. You’re just bad and hiding behind cooking.”

“Yes, yes. I’m bad.” He surrendered unconditionally, then deployed the real weapon. “Weren’t we partnering on meals now? Come eat. I bought fresh beef and salmon this morning.”

Because he had opened his big mouth at the start of the session — let me show you what a real climb looks like — and then delivered a five-game landslide. The least he could do was bribe the dragon back into a good mood with dinner, or she’d stay puffed up until Tuesday.

At beef and salmon, Park Ji-yeon’s stomach pronounced its verdict — a long, unmistakable growl that filled the quiet room like a foghorn.

She froze. Her face went from zero to crimson in one frame.

…

Floor. Open up. Now.

She hesitated, then rose from the chair with what dignity remained, muttering all the way.

“…Obviously I’m eating. Games aren’t food. And after throwing five games on my behalf, oppa is legally required to compensate me with a feast.”

“Yes, yes. Compensation it is. Let’s go.” He laughed — the dragon’s mood-swings had landing-gear-speed turnaround. Five minutes ago: justice for the lost games. Now: dinner logistics.

They shut down the rigs and went down, one after the other, Park Ji-yeon in front. Mention food and the dragon develops sudden athletic ability.

Down one flight, she keyed in the code herself and opened his door like a regular.

She’d lost count of how many times she’d been in oppa’s apartment now, but each time the same feeling waited for her inside — something unnameably safe. A living room clean and squared away. A half-drunk glass of water on the coffee table. Laundry drying on the balcony, the air faintly sweet with detergent.

A different world from hers. Hers being, in her own honest assessment: snack bags, delivery boxes, clothes in free-range distribution. A sty. A stylish sty.

Not thinking about it. Lalala. Not thinking about it.

✦ ✦ ✦

In the kitchen, Ji-yeon unloaded the morning’s haul. Prime beef tenderloin, that sunset-orange slab of salmon — both onto the cutting board.

“Why are you tailing me into the kitchen? You can’t cook.”

“Me?” Park Ji-yeon craned her neck over his shoulder, then grinned. “I’m here to HELP, obviously.”

Some shred of shame did survive in her. He’d fed her, was feeding her still, and charged her nothing — sitting idle waiting to be served crossed some line her conscience drew. So: labor.

“Fine. What can you do?”

She counted on her fingers with great solemnity.

“I can boil water and cook instant noodles! I can microwave leftovers! And if there are already-cooked leftovers, I can even reheat them on the stove…”

“Stop. Please stop.” He cut her off before the laughter got out. What had he expected from a girl whose food pyramid was snacks stacked on delivery — that she’d produce a confit? She deserved applause for remembering where the kitchen was.

He handed her a bag of greens.

“Wash these, then.”

She took the bag and set to work under the tap, copying his technique, scrubbing the leaves one by one with intense care, narrating under her breath.

“Cooking’s actually pretty easy? I always thought it’d be so hard. Look how clean this is. I could totally learn this. I’m basically halfway to being a chef.”

He heard. His mouth twitched. He said nothing.

The day you learn to cook, the sun will file a formal complaint about its orbital path.

He worked in parallel: tenderloin carved into even, thin slices — every piece the same width, the same size — into a soy-and-cooking-wine marinade, hands quick and economical. Then the salmon: boned, pin-bones tweezed, portioned for a simple pan-sear.

A small distance apart, Park Ji-yeon sneaked a look up at him.

Head bent, wholly focused on the knife. The line of his profile under the kitchen light was frankly unreasonable; his lashes threw a small shadow down his cheek.

…If every day could be a little bit like this. That wouldn’t be so bad, would it.

The thought completed itself before she caught it — and when she caught it she nearly dropped a fistful of spinach.

PARK. JI. YEON. What is going on in there. Focus. VEGETABLES.

✦ ✦ ✦

Pan-seared salmon, black-pepper beef stir-fry, and a plate of garlicky greens — three dishes landing on the table in short order, their aromas annexing the entire living room.

Park Ji-yeon sat down, surveyed the spread, and audibly swallowed.

First bite of the beef — and her eyes lit like signage.

Tender, juicy, black pepper cracking open across her tongue, the sauce rich by exactly the right degree, every mouthful a small sigh of contentment waiting to happen.

“Mmf!! Oppa, your cooking is just — it’s not fair. Delivery can’t even compete! It’s always the same five things — fried chicken, jajangmyeon, kimchi stew — I was this close to growing feathers.”

He laughed and put a piece of salmon in her bowl.

“Then eat properly. Look at you — there’s nothing on you.”

“Who’s thin! I’ll have you know my figure is EXCELLENT.”

She said it, and instantly the morning replayed itself in ultra HD — standing in the living room, oppa in the doorway, all of her extremely figure-visible.

…He did technically see everything this morning, didn’t he.

…Wonder if oppa liked— PARK JI-YEON. FINISH. YOUR. FISH.

“Sure, sure. Excellent figure. Eat.”

They talked in lazy loops over dinner — the cursed games, then the new skins coming out, then skin prices, then which delivery place was least disappointing. He half-listened, gaming a running commentary of “mm,” “right,” “no way” — until something snagged in his memory.

Wait. The Good Luck Card. Used it this morning. And all day the luckiest thing that’s happened is… seeing the little dragon indecent. If that’s what the system counts as good fortune, the system and I need to have a conversation about standards.

…Unless that IS the good fortune. In which case the system is a menace and I want a refund.

Dinner done, Park Ji-yeon rose with suspicious initiative and began stacking dishes.

“I’ll wash.” She gathered the plates against her body and marched them to the sink. A deal was a deal; the dishes were her jurisdiction.

Ji-yeon sank into the sofa and watched her go with a smile. She’d found an apron somewhere — where, he had no idea — and had it on backwards-tied like a veteran. Her hands at the sink were noticeably more competent than they’d been with the spinach.

Water running, the occasional bright clink of porcelain. He left her to it. She wanted to do it; far be it from him to interrupt someone else’s work ethic.

He turned on the TV. A drama was on — he watched three minutes, felt his brain begin to dissolve, and settled into channel-surfing, legs up on the coffee table, remote aloft, completely at peace.

In the kitchen, Park Ji-yeon glanced back at the living room.

The sight that greeted her: oppa, horizontal, remote in hand, radiating the aura of a feudal lord reviewing his estate.

Her brow furrowed. Something displeased began circling in her chest.

Washing dishes wasn’t hard. She wasn’t being precious about it.

But — why should she be in here scrubbing while oppa lolled out there being entertained? When he cooked, she’d helped! She’d washed vegetables with her own two hands!

The least he could do was come dry dishes. Or say “good work.” Or acknowledge her existence.

The more she thought, the hotter it burned, and her scrubbing intensified until suds were staging escapes across the counter.

Splash. Last plate rinsed, racked. She dried her hands, inhaled like a diver, and marched out.

He was exactly where she’d left him. Exactly.

Aish. I’m in here elbow-deep in dishwater and you’re out there living your best life. Okay. OKAY.

She advanced on the sofa and planted herself over him, hands on hips, a small storm cloud with a ponytail.

“Yah, oppa! You’re really just… fine with this? Lying there?”

His eyes stayed on the TV, where a weekend variety show had a comedian performing an elaborate physical gag to roars of laughter.

“That was the deal. I cook, you wash. Division of labor. Objections?”

“Objection! Hugely!” Volume rising. “I washed a mountain of dishes, my fingers are turning into raisins, and you can’t even come help — or, I don’t know, show one molecule of concern?”

“Concern? I’ve been supporting you spiritually this whole time.” He finally dragged his gaze off the screen, gave her a leisurely once-over, and deployed the most punchable smile in his arsenal.

It was four plates. The dragon excavated them like archaeological finds.

He genuinely could not parse the crisis. What was happening here.

“Spiritually?!” She barked out a laugh at the sheer nerve. “I require ACTION.”

Her eyes dropped to the remote in his hand — and an idea detonated behind them.

Oh, you like TV? Your favorite thing? Watch this.

She lunged. No warning, total commitment, both hands for the remote.

“YAH— what are you—!” He jolted and shot the remote-hand straight up like a periscope.

She was already airborne onto the sofa, half her body draped across his, arm stretched to its absolute limit chasing the remote. They were pressed together from shoulder to hip — she could feel his warmth through the hoodie — but Park Ji-yeon had exactly zero bandwidth for that observation. Her world was the remote.

“Give it!” An enraged kitten, claws everywhere.

“Nope.” He dodged with insulting ease — and his free arm, entirely on reflex, wrapped around her waist to keep her from sliding off the sofa.

The arm was solid. It settled around her middle like a seatbelt and removed her mobility options.

“Let GO of me!”

“Get down first.”

“Remote first!”

The wrestling match was on. In a living room the size of this one, on a sofa the width of this one, two people fought a total war for one channel changer. Park Ji-yeon scrambled up his ribs like scaffolding, all knees and determination; Ji-yeon, with his unfair wingspan, kept the remote in a different time zone from her fingertips every single time.

The variety show’s laugh track dueled with their shouting.

“YAH! Song Ji-yeon! Hands off!”

“You first.”

“Remote first!”

Then she overcommitted one lunge too many — her knee skidded off the cushion — and gravity filed its motion. She squealed and toppled, and the remote squirted free of his hand as his arms snapped around her, and they went down together onto the sofa cushions in one tangled heap.

Patter. The remote landed on the carpet, wheels-up, defeated.

Quiet.

Park Ji-yeon lay full-length on his chest. Her face hung above his — less than a fist’s distance. Through his sternum, clear as a drumbeat through a wall, came his heartbeat.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

It landed somewhere behind her own ribs, and hers picked up the tempo to match, faster and faster.

Their breath mingled in the few centimeters between them, warm, uneven, and suddenly very loud.

Every muscle in Park Ji-yeon locked.

…What am I doing. This is the move I use on the unnies when I steal their snacks. This is not a move that has EVER been used on— the POSITION. Look at the POSITION we’re in.

She could smell him — clean soap, faint laundry-warmth — and her face was heating up in real time, heartbeat thundering up into her throat, doing percussion from the inside.

The remote was gone. The war was over. The battlefield was silent, and her head was entirely, deafeningly empty except for his face, far too close, filling the whole screen.

Ji-yeon had gone still too.

The person in his arms was light, and soft, and smelled faintly of that green-apple shampoo. Looking down, he could see her lashes trembling, and the blush climbing her cheeks like a slow tide.

A long, long moment.

Then Park Ji-yeon’s system rebooted all at once.

Heat detonated from the soles of her feet to the crown of her head, and her face achieved a red that could be bottled and sold as nail polish.

“AHHH!!!”

She shrieked like she’d touched a stove, scrambled off him in pure panic — knees everywhere — cracked her shin on the coffee table corner hard enough to make her hiss.

No time for shins. No time for anything.

“I— I-I-I—”

Language had left the building. Not daring to look at him again, she grabbed the nearest coat off the sofa as a shield.

“I’m going home!!”

She didn’t even stop for shoes. Barefoot, coat flapping, she made for the door like a rabbit with a tiger on retainer, wrenched it open, and vanished.

SLAM.

The living room returned to its previous programming: one man, horizontal, and a variety show laughing at its own jokes.

Ji-yeon stayed exactly where he’d landed, staring at the ceiling, and then — at the sound of the upstairs door a moment later — started laughing.

Terrible at the game, absolutely cannot quit the game. That’s our dragon.

✦ ✦ ✦

Meanwhile, second floor.

Park Ji-yeon burst into her own apartment, slammed the door, and collapsed against it, chest heaving.

Her heart was still sprinting. Her face could poach an egg.

“AISH!!!”

She slid down the door into a crouch and buried her face in her knees, releasing one long, muffled howl of pure mortification.

It’s over. It’s over, it’s over, it’s over.

Whatever retrograde planet was steering her today, it had been at the controls since dawn. Walked in on while changing. Five straight losses. And now — now that, an accidental full-body collision on a sofa, of all the sofas in Cheongdam-dong.

Every possible embarrassment available to a human being, she had collected. All in one day. Complete the set.

How am I supposed to look at oppa’s face after this?? HOW.

✦ ✦ ✦

One floor down, a certain someone was completely unburdened. It was just a hug; he’d felt nothing beyond mild impact physics.

He watched another hour of variety shows, showered, traded a few goodnight messages with Eun-jung and Sulli, and went to bed. Tomorrow’s weekly meeting started at nine, and sleep was a schedule item too.

His father would be taking SAVORY F&B back under direct management this week, but Ji-yeon still needed to sit tomorrow’s meeting — for the handover, and more importantly to press Park Seon-young on the new entertainment company’s registration, and to find out from Kim Min-hyun exactly where things stood with Kim Kwang-soo.

He already knew from Eun-jung that CCM had scrapped the sub-unit plan. If Kwang-soo had cancelled T-ara N4, then the old fox had decided to sell — so why hadn’t Min-hyun called it in yet?

Guess I’ll ask him myself. Tomorrow.
