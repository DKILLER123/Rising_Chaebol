Chapter Thirty-Five

Thanks for Twenty-Two Years of Waiting Tables

DUSK Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Tteurak Hanwoo House

Cheongdam-dong at dusk, the western sky a dark-red burn. Ji-yeon parked the car and followed that smear of red straight up to the Tteurak signboard.

The place hummed money lives here from its stone front to its doorman’s gloves.

Standing outside, Ji-yeon held a brief internal council with himself. This was not dinner. This was walking into a spider’s parlor — one fly, six spiders, and not a single fly’s vote on the menu.

He shook it off before it could grow teeth, went in, and gave his name to the hostess. She walked him down the corridor and stopped at a door marked 3.

“This way, please — Mr. Song.”

He pushed the door open, and heat and aroma hit him full in the face.

Naturally, though, the first thing his eye found was the ring of six girls around the table.

They were all in civilian clothes, and it made no difference at all — that girl-group polish refused to be hidden, shining straight through the hoodies and cardigans. Looking at six distinctly, individually beautiful girls in one private room, Ji-yeon had to hand it to the man: Kim Kwang-soo might have had the strategic vision of a garden slug, but nobody had ever out-picked him on faces.

Kim Kwang-soo, wherever he was: “Excuse me — where are your manners, I ask?”

“Oppa! You finally came!!” Park Ji-yeon was up like a spring — the little dragon had never once in her life considered treating him as an outsider — and she reached the door in two strides, big eyes (a surviving fossil layer of stage smoky-eye not fully scrubbed off) crinkled with mischief. “Sit, sit, sit. We’ve been waiting so long the flowers started dropping petals.”

She had his sleeve and was towing him in before he could answer. Little dragon, are we close? This is our second meeting ever, and you’ve already graduated to arm-grabbing?

Ham Eun-jung, seated near the middle, kept her head bowed at an angle of profound embarrassment. Help. What do I do with a maknae whose social battery never runs out?

“Ji-yeon-ah. Decorum, please.” The leader’s voice, measured as ever. Park So-yeon rose and offered a small, precise bow. “Song Ji-yeon-ssi — we meet again.”

The rest of them rose in a soft rustle and bowed along with her.

“Please — enough with that.” Ji-yeon waved both hands. “We’re all friends here. Keep bowing and I won’t manage a single bite without scalding my conscience.”

He dropped into the seat beside Eun-jung — for one simple, logistical reason: it was the only empty chair at the table. He could hardly make one of them trade seats for his comfort.

Eun-jung shrank half a size and lowered her head even further. On paper, the two of them were spotless — clean, uncomplicated friends. What the other party actually thought, well. Eun-jung’s own thoughts had stopped being spotless some time ago. Somewhere between the ridiculous misunderstanding at their first meeting, the flutter in her chest during that lightless concert hall, and the warmth of cooking side by side in a kitchen, she had apparently started to like this man. She just — liked being around him. That was the whole finding, and she had no idea where to file it.

While Eun-jung’s inner weather system rolled through its fronts, Ji-yeon was perfectly at peace, because his reasoning ran exactly one sentence long: that chair was the empty one.

“Let’s at least order.” Lee Qri, seated at the far end, opened her menu with unhurried elegance and slid it across to him. “Since we’re the ones treating Song Ji-yeon-ssi, then Song Ji-yeon-ssi should do the ordering… and no being polite about it. Whatever you want.”

He declined immediately. He wasn’t a fool. He was the guest here; the six of them were the hosts; the guest follows the host’s lead — a rule carved into the bones of every restaurant child alive, and he had twenty-two years of family training under his belt. They weren’t nearly close enough for him to go pointing down a menu on their tab, and he knew precisely how close they were.

“Qri-ssi’s order is fine. I eat everything.”

“Yah, how could we— it’s our first time buying you dinner and we don’t even know what you can’t eat. You. Order.”

“Yah! If you two are going to keep passing it back and forth, I’ll do it!” Park Ji-yeon, out of all patience, snatched the menu out from between them and opened fire at point-blank range.

Marbled hanwoo. Beef tongue. Tenderloin. Anything on the page that had once gone moo — one of each, two of the good ones.

Ji-yeon watched the recitation roll out like a stock ticker and finally understood that the little dragon title hadn’t been earned by the fearlessness alone. The appetite was pure dragon cub too.

Before long the staff were in and out with charcoal braziers and the first waves of meat, the coals burning bright red and crackling.

Once the dishes started landing, Ji-yeon picked up the tongs on his own initiative. Nothing for it — he was the one man in the room, and he was not built to sit and watch six girls work a grill.

What about the room staff? Please. Six idols in one private room — and not just any six: T-ara, the single most flammable name in the country right now. One leaked photograph from one waiter, and the next round of online hate would already be waving hello to them. Grilling duty defaulted to him, and he took it without ceremony.

The aroma of premium marbled fat unspooled through the private room, thick and golden.

What Ji-yeon didn’t know was that the tongs were quietly doing reputation work on his behalf. Around a Korean dinner table, the grill belongs to whoever sits lowest in the pecking order — every one of them had grown up under that rule, watched it enforced, lived it. A man who simply reached for the tongs and stayed there, unasked, put a different kind of warmth into a room.

Park So-yeon felt it most of all. Her thoughts drifted, uninvited, to her boyfriend in the army — how every visit, every restaurant, it was her at the tongs while Oh Jong-hyuk sat enthroned like visiting royalty, waited on hand and foot, a great man graciously allowing her to serve. Without meaning to in the slightest, she set the two men side by side, and something in her chest tilted.

Suffice it to say: the reputation of the Korean boyfriend had been honestly earned.

Song Ji-yeon, turning the meat with a peaceful heart: Thank you — truly — for twenty-two years of waiting tables.

Oblivious to the shifts he’d caused in two separate hearts, he grilled on — scissors to the finished cuts, tongs laying the pieces one by one into the six plates in front of him.

Park Ji-yeon saw meat land in her dish and lit up like a slot machine paying out. Chopsticks straight in, no fear of the heat — one bite, then two more in quick succession, then a third, and only when her plate stood empty did she surface, cheeks full, to deliver her verdict: “Sho good. Oppa, your grilling is legit.”

She raised a thumb at him without pausing her chewing.

Watching her eat that joyfully pulled the whole table’s appetite awake, and the chopsticks came out in a wave.

“Omo!” Jeon Bo-ram, genuinely startled. “Song Ji-yeon-ssi, I had no idea you grilled this well.”

“Daebak,” Park Hyo-min chimed in from the next seat. “The meat’s perfect. Best Korean barbecue I’ve ever eaten, hands down.”

Ji-yeon smiled and privately marveled. The idol industry really was the world’s greatest charm school — listen to this language arts. Every word calibrated to land like a tiny gift. A delight. An absolute delight!!!

He turned the meat over, and his glance crossed the table to Park So-yeon. The leader was eating, yes — but her eyes kept drifting over to him, again and again, the way eyes do when their owner is carrying an undelivered speech.

He watched her hesitate through two pieces of beef and then took pity on all of them.

“So-yeon-ssi — whatever it is, just say it. Hold it in much longer and you’ll ruin your digestion.”

So-yeon startled, chopsticks frozen mid-air. She’d been building her opening line for the last five minutes, and he’d just kicked the door in ahead of her.

While she stood there recalibrating, Ji-yeon laid a slice of grilled beef tongue into Eun-jung’s dish and went back to his meat, paying the leader no further mind.

Eun-jung lifted her plate to receive it and said, very quietly, “Thank you.”

So-yeon gathered herself. Chopsticks down. Mouth dabbed. When she spoke again, the temperature of the whole room dropped to business.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. About the matter Eun-jung put on the table yesterday.”

“Are you serious?”

The words landed, and the chopsticks around the table slowed, one set after another. The private room went quiet enough that the meat still had the only voice in it — fat whispering, spitting softly, on the red charcoal.
