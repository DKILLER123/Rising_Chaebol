Chapter Ten

A Cat with Its Fur Standing Up

SEPTEMBER 2012 Apgujeong-dong, Seoul ONGI HOTPOT · PRIVATE ROOM 3, THE POT AT FULL BOIL

The broths had come up to a full rolling boil, the mala pot’s red waves rolling hardest of all, a few slices of tripe surfing the surface. Ji-yeon lifted the strainer, scooped out everything that had finished cooking with one practiced flick, and dropped half of it into Eun-jung’s bowl.

“Beef aorta. Ten seconds in the broth — that’s the entire trick. All crunch.”

Eun-jung looked at the small mountain range forming in her bowl and laughed out loud.

“You — one ‘noona’ and then nothing? That was the whole ceremony?”

“First names feel friendlier.” He glanced up, entirely unbothered. “Unless we’re not friends.”

She opened her mouth, discovered she genuinely had no rebuttal to that, and ate instead. The aorta was, in fact, absurdly crunchy — with the garlic-sesame dip, one bite and her whole mouth was perfume.

“So good.” Sincere to the bone, and she raised her right thumb to underline it.

“Then have more.” Another chopstick-load of meat landed in her bowl. “You’d look better with a little weight on you anyway. Nice and soft.”

The truth — the embarrassing, never-to-be-voiced truth — was that this era of Eun-jung had been his favorite of them all, back in his first life. Short-hair Eun-jung was a limited edition; she grew it back out after 2015. Not that long hair ever looked bad on her. Short hair just carried this unaccountable sweetness, and his eyes had never once managed to stop snagging on it.

Eun-jung choked on the compliment. She looked up to glare — and caught him head-down over the pots, the line of his profile gone slightly soft-focus through the rising steam. Her brain, entirely unprompted, supplied: …huh. This man is kind of handsome.

“Yah! Do you have any idea what ‘weight management’ means for an idol?! …What do you mean, ‘nice and soft’? I’m already the heaviest one in the group — keep feeding me and they’ll have to retire me from the stage altogether!” Lips out, thoroughly scandalized.

He flipped a slice of hanwoo into his mouth and thought back to their first meeting — the weight of her on top of him that night had been barely a suggestion. If he’d wanted out, he’d have been out. He tilted his head at her. “You are, though. Remember when you had me flattened on the sidewalk? I could barely tell you were there.”

The camera puts ten pounds on everybody. In person, Eun-jung was noticeably smaller than she read onscreen — a hundred pounds, soaking wet, if that.

“Yah! Who told you to bring THAT night up?! I thought we’d already settled it!” The pink was climbing her ears again.

Sure, fine — she’d wronged him. But a grown man trailing a lone woman down a midnight street — he was entirely blameless in all of it, was he?

And what was this “could barely tell you were there”? She had a very specific memory of a certain someone shielding his head with his one free hand and apologizing to the pavement.

She was winding up for round two of the complaint — and then the man with the strainer scooped out another round, neat as you please, and put half of it in her bowl. She pressed her lips together. Fine. It was very hard to interrogate someone who kept feeding you.

“Hmph! I’m done talking to you!” She bent to her bowl with single-minded devotion. She loved food, truly and deeply — and this hotpot was legitimately excellent.

✦ ✦ ✦

Half an hour later, Eun-jung set her chopsticks down on the table, melted back into the chair, pressed both hands over her belly, and let out a long sigh that was fifty percent bliss and fifty percent regret.

“Finished. I physically cannot eat another bite.”

The dishes before her were stacked into a respectable hill range, and the four broths were maybe seventy percent demolished. His blanching cadence had simply been too fast — her mouth had never once stopped — and somewhere between one bite and the next she had filled herself to the tonsils.

“I’m about done too. Shall we?” He set the strainer down, blotted his mouth with a napkin, took one look at her — thoroughly puddled into her chair — and failed to keep the laugh in. “Good thing you wore the oversized hoodie. Anything fitted and people would be asking how many months along you are.”

She froze. Looked down at the small dome her stomach had become. Looked back up at him. Two bonfires ignited, cheek to cheek.

“YAH!” She grabbed the tissue box and cocked it back like a fastball — then only lifted it, thought better of it, and set it back down — and settled for pure eye-daggers. “Is there ANYTHING you’re afraid to say out loud?!”

For all the menace in the motion, to Ji-yeon she registered precisely as much as a kitten with its fur standing on end. All fluff, zero threat.

He raised both hands, grinning, the very picture of surrender — with not one gram of apology anywhere in his voice. “I’m complimenting your appetite. That’s all that was.”

He genuinely liked the atmosphere of this. Eun-jung was earth to the core — no celebrity altitude on her whatsoever. Being around her felt like being around a friend.

She stood in a huff, dragged the mask and cap back on, and sealed her face away completely. Only her ears were left out — and the tips of them were red enough to draw blood.

The door of the private room opened on Park Min-suk, waiting, bowing as the two of them emerged.

Eun-jung walked straight to the front counter and pulled a card out of her bag.

“I’m paying.”

He reached out and headed her off. “Eun-jung-ah. Nobody pays for dinner in my own restaurant. That’s just not a thing that happens.”

She turned, and under the cap brim her eyes were dead serious. “Tonight is my apology dinner. So tonight, I treat. If you want to treat me — next time.”

There was no give anywhere in it. He opened his mouth, met the immovable object, and finally stepped out of its way.

“Fine. Next time’s mine.”

The girl at the register glanced at her Sajangnim; at his small nod she accepted Eun-jung’s card and rang it up, clean and quick.

Young, round-faced, eyes bending into crescents when she smiled. She handed the card back with both hands and slid smoothly into the pitch. “Miss, would you like to register one of our membership cards? It’s free. Every purchase earns points, and points redeem for our signature side dishes — or any of our special services.”

Eun-jung, card halfway back into her bag, paused. She glanced, entirely without meaning to, at Ji-yeon — who was leaning against the counter’s edge, hands jammed in his pockets, the very portrait of not-my-business, mouth carrying the ghost of a smile.

“Special services? What services?” Now she was interested.

“Our special services include complimentary manicures, hand care, shoe shines, photo printing, and more — all redeemable with points in our entertainment zone.” Clearly a veteran of this speech; not one syllable stumbled. “Members also receive twenty percent off the entire bill on their birthday, plus a birthday celebration from the staff.”

Eun-jung blinked, and her gaze made one more lap of Song Ji-yeon. This man — who read a good few years younger than her — was out here running a hotpot restaurant like some kind of private members’ club.

“Let’s do one.” She turned back to the cashier. “Since I’ll… probably be coming here a lot anyway.”

Her logic was airtight, in its way: the hotpot was genuinely good, it was literally downstairs from her front door, the membership cost nothing and came with an absurd perks list. Why not.

“Great! Could I trouble you for your phone number?” The cashier already had the registration screen up.

Eun-jung recited it; the girl rattled across the keyboard; and in under a minute it was done. A brand-new blue membership card landed in her palm — minimalist gold-foil lettering, very good in the hand.

“Here you are, Miss. Tonight came to one hundred thousand won — that’s five hundred points.” A gesture toward the wide zone off to the right of the entrance. “That’s our entertainment zone. You’re welcome to redeem any time you like.”
