Chapter One Hundred Three

Night Talk Beneath the Stars

FRIDAY · DEEP NIGHT Gwacheon, Gyeonggi-do Seoul Grand Park — the meadow, far from the stream

The autumn night’s cool wind came down off the ridge carrying the green smell of grass and trees, and touched every face on the mat, one by one, gently, like a groundskeeper doing rounds.

Six of them lay side by side on the wide ground sheet, faces up, under a sky that had run out of room for any more stars.

Song Ji-yeon sat in the folding chair he’d parked a polite distance off the mat’s edge.

Was he tempted to lie down in the middle of the formation, one man bracketed by beautiful women on both flanks? Briefly. In the way one is tempted to nap on a highway. He was not, whatever the roster suggested, a creep — and an uninvited landing on that mat would be processed as an act of war, with six signatories.

Discretion. The better part of valor. The chair it is.

“Oppa! What are you doing all the way over there?! Come lie down with us — the stars are RIGHT HERE!”

He blinked. He’d only thought it — and the dragon had gone and issued the invitation anyway, sight unseen.

The trouble was arithmetic. The mat was generous by mat standards, but six adult women lying shoulder to shoulder had already used up every available centimeter of it. There was no vacancy posted.

“Pass. The mat holds exactly you six — I’ll take the sky from the chair, thanks.”

Ji-yeon sat up and ran a survey of the territory. Population map: herself, dead center; Hyo-min and Qri at her flanks; Eun-jung beyond Hyo-min; Bo-ram and So-yeon beyond Qri. She aimed one finger at the far edge.

“Oppa, hurry! There’s still room on Eun-jung-unnie’s side — we’ll squeeze and make it fit… Come on, oppa.”

Eun-jung said nothing at all. She simply migrated half a body toward Hyo-min’s side of the map, quiet as a tide going out.

He read the permission and took it.

He knew the operating rules by heart — Eun-jung wasn’t ready for the members to know about them, so the two of them ran a strict distance protocol whenever T-ara was in eyeshot. But tonight she hadn’t moved away, and Song Ji-yeon was constitutionally incapable of leaving an opened door unused.

The slot was small. It was, in fact, exactly his size.

And so there were seven people on one ground sheet, flat on their backs, under a wheeling field of stars. With the city’s noise and light pollution a world away, the sky out here didn’t twinkle so much as burn — dense, glittering, unembarrassed. The quiet underneath it was total except for a far-off insect somewhere and the stream going about its business.

“Wow…” Ji-yeon, arms folded behind her head, let out a long, satisfied sigh that broke the silence like a small bell. “It’s been forever since I’ve seen this many stars.”

“It really has.” Hyo-min’s voice came soft from the middle of the formation. “Since debut it’s been practice room, schedule, practice room, van. The ceiling I know best in this world is a broadcast studio’s. I’d honestly forgotten the sky even has a default setting.”

Nobody answered that, and nobody needed to. The whole mat hummed with agreement.

Being a top girl group meant an audit of losses that never quite closed. Too many of the ordinary girl’s hours had been surrendered at the door — and lying in the open doing nothing but looking up, thinking nothing, owing nothing, had become the kind of luxury you had to drive an hour out of the city to steal back.

Bo-ram turned her head. She looked down the line at her sisters, one by one, and then at the man lying at the far edge, and something warm pooled in her chest and refused to drain.

“Honestly… we really owe Song Ji-yeon-ssi a thank-you. If it weren’t for you, we’d be at home right now going gray with worry. Camping — we’d have forgotten the word existed.”

In cold accounting, it was true, he had done nothing for them yet.

Neither the China campaign nor the promised clearing of the bullying storm had actually been delivered — both still existed purely as his spoken word, and so far reality had cashed neither check.

And still. He had appeared at exactly the hour they’d stopped believing in appearances. He had believed in them, flatly, without conditions, at a moment when the whole Peninsula was enjoying not doing so — and then he had gone and simply bought them.

Bo-ram was built with a fine-grained heart, and from the start of the scandal it had been running overtime on two particular worries: Eun-jung and Ji-yeon — because no one in that group loved T-ara harder than those two. And through the worst months she’d watched Eun-jung go quieter and quieter, and watched Ji-yeon’s whole personality fold itself away, the aegyo and the noise packed into storage, and there had been nothing Bo-ram could do about any of it except worry.

Then Song Ji-yeon had arrived — and both of them had come back to factory settings. Eun-jung had brightened. Ji-yeon had reactivated.

…Reactivated somewhat past factory specifications, admittedly. There were evenings Bo-ram actively missed the quiet, melancholy Ji-yeon.

Eh. Fine. This was better. Either way — genuinely, from the bottom of her heart: thank you for showing up.

“What Bo-ram-unnie said!” Ji-yeon sat bolt upright and aimed both hands at the man on the edge like a herald announcing a title. “Oppa — you’re our savior!”

Lying beside Eun-jung, he heard the announcement land. He smiled, eyes still on the sky.

“Don’t put me on a pedestal. I made a sound investment. I’m still planning to build you into China’s number-one girl group and have you earn it back for me.”

He meant every syllable of it.

T-ara’s ceiling was beyond appraisal. With his hand on the wheel, the only direction that group traveled was up — and the China market he could see coming, ten years of hindsight wide, was the single largest stage on the planet. China’s number-one girl group was not a fantasy; it was a calendar entry. A few stadium dates in, the money would be lying on the ground waiting to be collected.

As for the ₩4 billion the purchase had cost — leave aside whatever the China entry eventually paid him. The Goddess Handbook’s rewards had already outrun the price tag on their own. The Stock Forecast Card riding on Eun-jung earned billions per use. The semaglutide synthesis technology sitting on Park Ji-yeon’s file was a cash cow patiently waiting for its decade to open. And if he ever managed to bind the remaining members of T-ara — he genuinely didn’t dare run that total.

The investment only bent one direction: toward the plus column. Historically speaking, the man had never once booked a real loss.

Qri swept her hair over one shoulder, sat up in one smooth motion, and looked across the mat at him with eyes carrying a fresh dose of curiosity and cross-examination.

“If Song Ji-yeon-ssi rates us that highly — then what does the actual plan look like? From what Ji-yeon and Hyo-min relayed: Japan first, then the China push.”

While Eun-jung and he had been off pitching tents, those two had broadcast the car briefing to the full membership with the fidelity of a news agency.

“There is a plan.” His eyes came off the sky and made one pass around the mat. “Next week, staff from the new company will contact you. First order of business: the Okinawa showcase and the Japan promotions for the new album, both closed out clean. Those contracts were signed under CCM, and we honor the terms. Understand the weight on top of that — this is T-ara’s first official appearance wearing the new company’s name. It goes out flawless.”

“Once the Japan dates wrap and we’re back on the Peninsula, systematic Chinese training begins.”

The tone sharpened by a full degree.

“Next year, our main battleground is China. Language is the first gate. If that gate doesn’t open, everything behind it stays on paper.”

“Chiiinese…” Ji-yeon groaned from the bottom of her soul and flopped back down onto the mat like a felled tree. “Oppa, Chinese is SO hard. My entire working vocabulary is nǐ hǎo and xiè xie.”

“Which is exactly why there will be training.” He didn’t budge. His immunity to dragon-grade aegyo had long since been completed and certified.

And because the dragon obeyed nothing that wasn’t physically enforced, one rider got attached:

“And don’t think about scheming, either. I’ll hire the best teachers on the market. Closed-door intensive. Daily checkpoints. Can’t recite the lesson — no dinner.”

“AH? That strict?!”

The second person to be startled out of the mat was Bo-ram.

She wasn’t afraid of studying — studying was, if anything, her department. She was the group’s resident language ace: Korean by birth, Japanese at genuinely conversational depth, English solid enough to hold her own. She had every confidence that Chinese, too, would eventually surrender.

But — recitation or famine? Was that not — excessive? She was a person who liked eating. Loudly. Daily. Study-without-dinner wasn’t education; it was persecution of her specifically, drafted with her name on it.

Conclusion: start studying in advance. Tonight. Immediately. Where was a textbook at this hour.

He had calibrated the scare to dragon specifications — and the dragon had sailed through it untouched while the hamster took the full blast at point-blank range.

Qri laughed under her breath and reached over to poke Bo-ram’s cheek.

“Bo-ram-unnie. You’re our eldest. Set the example. If the eldest can’t hack it, what face do the rest of us have to even try?”

“Yah, Lee Qri!” She knew a roast when she was the one cooking in it. She swatted the hand away, puffing up to full diameter. “I— I will obviously study properly!”

“Hahaha — unnie, don’t force it. Your legendary three-minute passion? I’m laying day-one odds you’re crying to quit by lunch.”

“I will NOT!”

While the bickering rekindled itself for the evening’s final round, Eun-jung watched with the helpless smile of a woman long past being surprised by any of it. Then her fingers found the hem of his sleeve and gave it a small tug — she was lying right beside him — and her voice came down to a channel that carried exactly two subscribers.

“Oppa… is it really going to be that strict?”

The voice was soft, and underneath the softness ran a thread of worry she hadn’t quite managed to hide.

She knew her members. Not one of them was afraid of suffering — they’d been professionally suffering since their teens. What ached was the imagining: a demon-camp language program, the specific dread of it. And she, better than anyone on the mat, knew precisely how hard Chinese was.

The group had run China schedules before. The company, chasing overseas visibility, had even commissioned them Chinese lessons once upon a time. Results: six members, a brace of fixed phrases, best speaker Hyo-min — with maybe five sentences of stage patter to her name. Teaching these six Chinese was a long march down a long road with no shortcuts posted anywhere.

At that rate, come training camp, many members of T-ara would be going hungry.

Including, she had to admit, herself.

He turned his head and met her eyes. In the dark, hers were brighter than anything the sky overhead had on offer.

“Eun-jung. I know what you’re afraid of.”

“But if we’re going to stand on Chinese ground, sincerity is the entry fee. You can’t keep a fandom you can’t speak to — and you can’t walk a long road on charm alone. I’m not building this group into a flash in the pan.”

Then he looked at her with a steadiness she had never seen him use before, and the words came out carrying weight.

“I took this group on. Which means you don’t fall off a summit a second time — not while I’m holding the rope. The front-loaded suffering is real, and it’s non-negotiable. …It won’t actually be that strict. The famine clause was dragon-control. Unscared, that one studies exactly nothing.”

Eun-jung looked back at him, motionless.

She read it all in one pass — the resolve, and the weight of the responsibility riding on it, and the fact that both of them were real. This man was actually, in real time, laying road under their future.

Something warm settled in her chest and stayed. She nodded, said nothing more —

— and when she lay back down, her body drifted half an inch closer without asking anyone’s permission, until her shoulder came to rest against his, almost edge to edge.

He felt the whole transaction. He didn’t move. He let the soft warmth press home and stay.

The faint clean scent of her shampoo arrived on the short distance between them, and the low static of his thoughts went quiet, one channel at a time.

He suspected, in the honest dark, that this really was a road with no exits on it.

The Goddess Handbook was bound to him, and that binding only ran forward. He would never get to be an ordinary man — one woman, one life, a love story with a clean plot. His life was going to keep acquiring people. That was the fine print, and he’d signed it.

But he looked around at the faces on this mat — fed, lit up, glowing with a future they’d stopped believing in a month ago — and the accounting came back positive anyway.

Since a singular love was off the table, he would give them a future no one could match.

The night deepened, and with it the cold crept in. A full day of play had finally run the six batteries flat, and the conversation thinned out, voice by voice, until only the stars were talking.

So-yeon yawned — a small, elegant, entirely un-filmable yawn — and rubbed her eyes.

“It’s late. I’m turning in.”

“Mm — go rest, all of you. Tomorrow we spend properly.” He sat up out of the constellation first.

Four tents. He held one of them, solo, by obvious right of conquest.

The remaining six sorted themselves into pairs by long-standing treaty: So-yeon with Hyo-min; Qri with Bo-ram; and Eun-jung with Ji-yeon — naturally, and with no debate.

“Good night, oppa!” Ji-yeon waved once and towed Eun-jung into the tent by the wrist, luggage-style.

“Good night.”

The good-nights went around the circle, and then the camp was down to one man and a lot of crickets.

Exactly as forecast, then.

One man. One tent. Zero company.

He exhaled at the sky, resigned, and went into his own tent like a law-abiding citizen serving a sentence.

SATURDAY MORNING Gwacheon, Gyeonggi-do Seoul Grand Park — striking camp

He woke to the birds running their morning shift directly over his tent, at a volume suggesting they were being paid by the syllable.

He dragged the zipper open and the morning came in — cool, clean, wet grass and turned earth — while the early sun came down through the trees and scattered coin-sized light across the lawn.

The girls were already up in stages, clustered around the washstand in various states of bedhead.

“Oppa — morning!” Ji-yeon greeted him around a toothbrush, intelligibility hovering near sixty percent.

“Morning.” He joined the queue, grinning.

A simple breakfast later, the strike began: tents down, folding furniture folded, garbage consolidated. Seven people, one assembly line, and — to everyone’s mild surprise — genuine throughput.

“YAH, JEON BO-RAM! Where did you PUT the stakes?!” Qri was elbow-deep in the tent’s stuff sack, which was not returning the missing parts.

“I— maybe… the black bag? The black one, I think?” One finger aimed into the middle distance of the equipment pile.

Qri marched over, opened it — and yes: stakes. Bedded neatly beside them: the beetles. Her terror weapons from yesterday, apparently preserved as a permanent collection, exhibit A through whatever.

“KYAAA—!!”

Bo-ram left the ground a second time in twelve hours, and the camp collapsed all over again, six-fold.

Struck and packed, the original ambition had been to do the park properly — botanical garden, zoo, amusement park, the full circuit while the chance was rare.

But it was Saturday. The park had filled to its rails — a slow river of people, wall to wall, from the gates to the far exhibits. And six of the more recognizable faces on the Peninsula, planted in the middle of that river, were not a scenery. Recognition in seconds, a crowd in minutes, a barricade in twenty — and by morning, entertainment front pages, all-caps.

T-ara, in this month of the Peninsula, carried an anti café four hundred thousand members deep. Better, all six agreed silently, not to manufacture work for Song Ji-yeon.

Eyes went around the circle — I dare you to say it, no, I dare you — until Bo-ram finally waved both hands in surrender.

“Never mind, never mind. Last night’s stars and grilled meat already maxed out the trip for me. Look at those crowds — the last thing we should do is go out there terrorizing the citizenry.”

The sentence landed in every heart at the same instant. The circle smiled, folded the ambition away with the tents, and the little convoy pulled out for home.

SATURDAY · MIDDAY Seoul-bound Then: Cheongdam-dong

The ride back ran a full register quieter than the ride out.

Inside the city limits, Qri’s car peeled off with a honk and a wave. The six lived scattered all over the capital — one convoy running door to door would eat the whole afternoon. So the fleet had divided by plan: Qri ferrying her passengers home; he taking Eun-jung, the dragon, and Hyo-min.

Worn down to the screws, Ji-yeon and Hyo-min slept in the back seat like sandbags with hair. Eun-jung, riding shotgun, said little — just watched the scenery run backward past the window with a small, unbroken smile riding her mouth.

He brought the car to a stop beneath Park Hyo-min’s apartment tower — a first visit to this particular building.

“We’re here.”

Hyo-min surfaced from sleep, identified her own address, and stepped out. Then she came around to the passenger window to hand in her exit interview.

“Eun-jung-unnie, Song Ji-yeon-ssi — bye! This was really, really fun. Next time — same team, same trip, please.”

“Bye, Hyo-min. Go get some real rest.” Eun-jung’s reply came back sweet enough to frame.

“There will be a next time. Once the company’s properly on its feet, I’m hosting an annual idol camping trip — you’re all on the permanent guest list. Just don’t get sick of it by year three.”

“Jinjja?! You host it, I attend it. Every year. Put it in writing.”

“Hahaha — go on up, before I start charging you rent on the curb.”

He idled at the kerb until she was through the lobby doors, then pulled back into traffic.

“Eun-jung. Home? Or the dragon’s place?”

Two passengers left. The dragon’s address required no asking — the woman technically lived one flight of stairs above his own flat. But Eun-jung was Apgujeong; the question was owed.

The dragon surfaced at the sound of her own address, right on schedule.

“Unnie~! Come to my place! I’m home alone and dying of boredom — come keep me company, we’ll game until our eyes fall out.”

Eun-jung gave it one honest beat of thought, then nodded.

“I’ll come along to Cheongdam-dong. That much equipment — you can’t haul it all alone. I’ll at least lighten the load.”

Gaming, for the record, had nothing to do with it.

Her actual arithmetic: one man, one mountain of equipment, and not enough hands. Someone had to supervise the loading of the dragon’s flanks, or carry them himself — and she had already decided, somewhere around Gwacheon, that it was not going to be himself.

“Yay — long live unnie!”
