Chapter Seventy

An Unhappy Parting

SIX O’CLOCK Apgujeong-dong, Seoul ONGI Apgujeong — the Bamboo room

“Why don’t we order first? We can talk while we eat—”

“There’s no need. Business first.”

The hostility wasn’t subtle anymore, and Song Ji-yeon still had no idea what he’d done to earn it. But he kept smiling anyway — the old rule from twenty-two years of family restaurants: nobody slaps a smiling face.

“The endorsement, then. Honestly, it’s less an offer than a question — I wanted to ask whether Victoria-ssi would even be interested. The soju is still in development; it hasn’t gone into production. But I have real confidence in it. When it launches, I believe the Peninsula’s younger generation is going to love it.”

And he laid the zero-sugar case out for her, plain and clean.

“Every soju on the market right now is heavy on calories. A lot of young people — women above all — want a drink and are terrified of the scale. So: a zero-sugar, zero-calorie soju. Clean on the palate, sharp on the bottle. Health and trend, in one product. That’s the whole idea.”

Sulli, listening beside her leader, nodded along so hard she was practically a second pitch.

“Right, right, RIGHT! Oppa’s idea is so good! If that soju actually existed, I’d drink it every single day!”

Victoria’s brow came down.

Her plan tonight had been simple. Take this Song Ji-yeon’s measure. Establish what the man was made of — because her fear had a specific shape: someone working an angle on Sulli, after her savings or something worse. The endorsement had been her pretext to sit across from him and check his teeth.

And then — before she’d tested him at all — he’d gone and opened his own mouth and done her work for her. The soju wasn’t in production. It existed nowhere but a lab bench.

A product that had never met a market, and the man was already shopping for a celebrity face?

She knew that playbook line by line.

The Peninsula had no shortage of merchants running it, and no shortage of trainees gutted by it — coaxed into “partnerships,” relieved of their money and worse. It worked on green kids. On idols with years in the industry, it had no chance whatsoever—

She shot a sideways glance at Sulli, and the string inside her pulled taut.

Wrong. She’d forgotten the most basic fact in the room. Years in the industry wasn’t a vaccine, and Sulli was the single greenest peach in the entire orchard.

Which settled it. The verdict came down clean and cold: this man was not a good piece of work. No product, no data, and he was already promising idol endorsements — that was the oldest game there was, all take and no give. Get close under deal-talk cover, then walk the silly girl out of her money step by step. And Sulli, bless her enormous heart, would hand him the combination herself.

No — correction.

He already had the money.

This dinner no longer needed to be eaten. Victoria let the fire come up her throat and out.

“Not even in production, and you’re already hunting for an endorser? What would you call that, Song Ji-yeon-ssi — if not a scam?”

The displeasure in it was not being disguised as anything else.

The ambush left him blank for a moment. Hold on. Madam. What did I ever do to you?

Sulli had known her leader too long to miss any of it. She shrank into her shoulders and spoke up in the smallest voice in the room.

“Vic-unnie… I’m the one who said I’d ask oppa for you… Don’t be mad at oppa…”

Victoria drew a long breath through her nose and pressed the fire back down. She rolled her eyes at Sulli — once — while somewhere behind them she cursed Song Ji-yeon through several more rounds.

What kind of hold does this man have, that you’d sit there shielding him?

She looked back at him, and her voice had finished cooling.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. I have no idea what business you’re actually in. But a soju that hasn’t launched — no market feedback of any kind — and you’re in a hurry to sign a face for it. Does that sound like a serious proposition to you?”

A pause, and she kept going, unhurried, laying each plank down flat.

“I’m not a business professional. But even I know what a brand brings before it asks for an endorser. A product, on shelves. Market data. A complete marketing plan. You have none of the three — and you want me to front your soju. Does that seem reasonable to you?”

For once, Song Ji-yeon had no comeback.

Because she wasn’t wrong. He had moved too fast. The zero-sugar soju was a good idea with a wide horizon, and he’d wanted the face locked in early — and cleanly forgotten that from where she sat, the entire offer was a blank check.

But he wasn’t going to stand there pleading. If Victoria’s verdict was fraud, then fine — no deal. She was f(x)’s leader, and famous, and the Peninsula was in no danger of running out of idols. If this one fell through, you brought in the next one. A failed dinner cost him nothing that a man in his position would notice. Song Ji-yeon did business one way and had never once done it the other way: never by force.

“You’re right, Victoria-ssi. I didn’t think it through.” His own temperature came down to match hers; the apology, though, he kept sincere. “This was presumptuous of me. I’m sorry you made the trip for nothing.”

The apology landed. It did not put out any fires.

Victoria stood and picked up her bag from beside the chair.

“Then let’s end it here. And one more thing — Sulli’s stock account no longer requires your management. Tomorrow I’m taking her to change the password.”

The temperature in that sentence was final. Nothing further was being invited.

Song Ji-yeon blinked — and finally understood the shape of the problem. Ah. She thinks I’m here to con Sulli out of her money.

…He was, whatever else was true, the sitting CEO of an actual company. What was he supposed to do with an idol’s savings, exactly?

The rebuttal got as far as his teeth, took one look around the room — the shuttered face, the packed-up patience — and lay back down.

Sulli shot up after her leader, panicked.

“Vic-unnie! Don’t be mad! Oppa really isn’t a fraud—”

“Sulli. We’re leaving.”

It was not a sentence with a door open in it. Victoria looked at Song Ji-yeon one last time and delivered her exit line — courteous to the millimeter, and just as distant.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. Thank you for the hospitality. Should there be anything next time — kindly go through the proper channels.”

The subtext needed no interpreter: stop going near Sulli.

Then she turned and walked out.

Sulli stood there wringing her hands — looked at her leader’s back, looked at Song Ji-yeon, and back — utterly at a loss.

“Oppa…”

“Go on.” He smiled at her, and kept it gentle. “Don’t keep your leader waiting.”

She bit her lip and hurried after Victoria — then stopped dead at the doorway and looked back. The apology was all over her face.

“Oppa… I’m sorry.”

Her voice had gone small, with a thread of grievance woven through it.

“Vic-unnie… she isn’t usually like this. It’s because she’s worried about me — that’s why she’s… she’s looking at you through tinted glasses. She isn’t targeting you, oppa. She isn’t…”

By the end, the words were barely making it out, and her eyes had gone red at the rims.

She understood perfectly well what was going on. Vic-unnie’s coldness, the unreasonableness — all of it traced back to one person. To her. Worry did that; put the glasses on before the man ever sat down, and after that nothing he said or did could be innocent. If it weren’t for her, Victoria would never have been like this.

Song Ji-yeon looked at the girl who was about thirty seconds from crying, and sighed inside. Victoria was a strong hand by nature — a woman didn’t carve a name like hers out of the Peninsula by being soft. And Sulli, wedged between the two of them, had the hardest seat in the room.

He walked over and patted her shoulder, lightly.

“It’s fine. I understand. Your leader worrying about you — that’s a good thing, Sulli.”

Nothing in his voice was holding anything against anyone.

“Go on, now. Don’t keep her waiting.”

Sulli sniffed hard, nodded with everything she had, turned, and trotted out after her leader.

Which left Song Ji-yeon alone in the Bamboo room.

A perfectly good dinner — scattered before a single dish hit the table. Parted on bad terms, as the saying went, before the first course.

He poured himself a cup of the Da Hong Pao — the pot that had arrived on schedule and sat politely unclaimed through the entire war — and drank it slowly, letting the simmer in his chest come off the boil.

He checked the time. Six-thirty.

Teacup down, coat on, out of the room.

Downstairs, Park Min-suk had seen everything a front desk needs to see: first, one beautiful woman in a mask storming out in a huff; then, minutes later, a second masked beauty with red-rimmed eyes hurrying out after her. By now she had a hundred episodes of chaebol melodrama fully scripted in her head, season arcs included.

She was deep in episode twelve when Song Ji-yeon came down. She hurried to meet him.

“Sajangnim — no dinner tonight? Those two young ladies just—”

“Not tonight, Min-suk noona. I’ve got somewhere to be.” He caught the gossip kindling in her eyes, shook his head, and smiled the rest of the question into silence.

He left ONGI, stopped at the convenience store for a bag of snacks, and walked over to the PIEN PLUS building — around to the right side, into the elevator, up to the sixth floor.
