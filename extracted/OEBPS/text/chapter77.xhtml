Chapter Seventy-Seven

Goo Hara: Gulp… I Just Discovered Something Huge

SATURDAY AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the f(x) dorm — curb outside

The Mercedes found the curb below the f(x) dorm and settled there. Song Ji-yeon didn’t get out. He leaned back in the driver’s seat, rolled the window down, and pulled out his phone to fire off a message.

Footsteps arrived before long, quick and light. He looked up — Sulli, trotting out of the dorm building.

She wore a cream-white knit sweater cut loose and cozy, jeans below, her ear-length hair tucked away under a blue beret. The whole effect was one part art student, two parts puppy.

“Oppa! Have you been waiting long?” Her voice arrived before she did, clear and bright, one arm waving hard from ten meters out.

He pocketed the phone and shook his head. “Just got here.”

She rounded the hood at a jog, pulled the passenger door open, folded herself in, and buckled up. “Go go go! We’re off!”

He smiled at all that brightness and started the car, and the complex slid away behind them.

“Oppa — guess who we’re picking up!” Sideways in her seat, eyes shining, a kid with a secret physically too large for her body.

“Either IU… or Goo Hara.” He said it mildly, eyes on the road. This wasn’t hard math — besides her groupmates, the little peach had exactly two close friends on this earth. He kept his face grave. “But you said a total beauty. Between the two of them — my money’s on Goo Hara.”

“Pfft—” The mock gravity broke her completely. “Oppa, how did you KNOW?! …Wait.” The grin sharpened into something with teeth. “So you’re saying Ji-eun-unnie ISN’T a beauty? That’s it. I’m telling Ji-eun-unnie you said that.”

He kept driving and felt the exasperation arrive like a thin line of black rain down the back of his head. There were rules against traps like this. Somewhere.

It wasn’t that IU wasn’t pretty. It was that 2012 IU was still unripe — the baby fat hadn’t fully retired from her face yet, and “total beauty” was, strictly speaking, a category error. A few years down the line, absolutely. He’d seen the receipts.

Sulli clocked his deflated silence and let her mouth curve in open triumph. “Kidding, oppa. As if I’d actually tell on you. Relax… Still — how’d you guess it was Hara-unnie?”

“What’s there to guess? Outside the group you’ve got exactly two friends — IU and Goo Hara. The press even runs a package deal on you three: the Human Trilogy.” A beat. “KARA’s got overseas work right now — hence, the airport.”

She nodded along, openly admiring. “Oppa got it! KARA just wrapped their tour in Japan. Hara-unnie flew back to Seoul first, for a few days of rest before the others. It’s just her this time.”

He nodded too, face doing absolutely nothing, mind already made up.

Goo Hara did genuinely interest him. Not only because she was one of KARA’s most popular members, and not only because she was lovely — but because in his past-life memory she was a woman who had left a mark, vivid and heartbreaking in equal measure. Not everyone could be that brave, and that kind, at the same time. Her death had sat strangely in his chest for a long time after he’d read the news in LA.

If a chance existed, he wanted to change a few things.

Besides — he was already saving one little peach. The mermaid could ride on the same paperwork. He’d transmigrated four thousand miles into 2012; skipping a rescue on technicalities seemed like a bad look for his conscience.

“Oppa.” She leaned in a few centimeters, eyes going pleading. “Actually — there’s a reason I wanted you to meet Hara-unnie.”

He side-eyed her, one brow up. “Oh? What reason?”

She blinked twice, and the playfulness drained out of her face into something rare: actual seriousness. “Hara-unnie was telling me recently — she’s saved up some money, and she wants to invest it. But she doesn’t understand any of it, and in our industry you hear about scams constantly, so she’s been too scared to do anything.” A pause. She watched him, and the trust in her eyes had no floor to it. “And then — I thought of oppa.”

He’d assembled the whole picture two sentences ago, but where was the fun in admitting that.

“Me. I’m not an investment advisor, you know. Your Vic-unnie has me filed under con man — and you’re handing me your Hara-unnie anyway?”

“Yah!” Fully indignant, both fists balled on her knees. “Oppa, you SAID you were over the Vic-unnie thing! Why do you keep bringing it up—”

“Messing with you. Where’d this temper even come from, huh?” One hand stayed on the wheel; the other reached over and ground a friendly knuckle into her hair.

She ducked away, patted the beret back into shape, and turned pointedly to the window. “That’s what you get for joking about Vic-unnie. I am being serious with you right now.”

He eased onto the gas and merged onto the overpass toward Gimpo. “Fine, fine — serious. Your Hara-unnie wants to invest. Property, stocks, either one works. Isn’t that what wealth-management firms are for?”

He wasn’t a charity, whatever the rumors said. Managing Sulli’s money he could do — the little peach trusted him, and that was reason enough. Goo Hara, though, required thought. He had zero interest in a replay of the Victoria misunderstanding — if Goo Hara also decided he was a swindler, the rescue plan would die in its crib.

They weren’t even friends. How do you save someone who won’t take your calls?

So: friends, yes. Money, no.

“Those people can’t be trusted.” She turned back from the window to study his profile. “It’s always in the news — some manager or some advisor makes off with an idol’s entire savings. And property… Hara-unnie already owns two apartments. Buy any more and the holding tax eats you alive, commercial space costs a fortune…” She trailed off toward the obvious. “So there’s only stocks. And Hara-unnie knows exactly as much about stocks as I do—”

He watched the road and caught himself smiling. “So you threw her to me.”

This little peach and her wholehearted, unshakeable trust. All right — it got him a little.

“Obviously.” Stated like a law of nature. “Both stocks oppa told me to buy — Samsung and Sewon — not one won lost, and a whole pile gained. So I trust oppa. In my heart, oppa is the Oracle of Seoul—”

He laughed the flattery off and let it go unanswered. But the conversation had genuinely moved something. Fine — he wouldn’t manage Goo Hara’s money. He could simply tell her which stock was about to make money. Whether she bought in was her own business. The funds would never so much as graze his hands — and then let anyone on the Peninsula try to file him under con man.

And that thought led him to a second, guiltier one: before Goo Hara… shouldn’t Eun-jung come first? The Stock Forecast Card had dropped out of her activation in the first place.

(Somewhere across Seoul, Ham Eun-jung sneezed hard enough to rattle a window. You pig. NOW you remember me?!)

He’d ask her tonight, when he got home. The six of them had no schedules these days — the group was running on fumes and back catalog. If he could help, he should. And anyway, the card had come out of Eun-jung; by rights, the first share was hers.

✦ ✦ ✦

FORTY MINUTES LATER Gimpo International Airport arrivals — the Tokyo flight

The car dropped into Gimpo’s underground lot and slid into a space near the elevator bank. Key out, door open.

Sulli was already out, mask on, towing him toward the elevators by the sleeve.

Up in the arrivals hall, the board said the flight from Tokyo had landed. Around the gate stood a scatter of drivers and placards; no crowd energy, no commotion.

“Hara-unnie kept this trip quiet. Impressive, right?” Sulli steered them a few steps aside and claimed a spot with a clean sightline.

He nodded. He’d seen an airport during a trending idol’s arrival — Jeju had taught him that much. “Sea of people” wasn’t a metaphor. It was a census.

They waited a little over ten minutes.

Then, from the corridor: the sound of suitcase wheels. A girl came through alone — a long gray trench coat, sunglasses big enough to swallow half her face, one small hand on the handle of an oversized silver suitcase.

She was tiny. The coat made her look smaller still, like the fabric had been issued to somebody twice her size. Head down, one hand wheeling the case, the other holding a phone she was reading as she walked — visibly scanning for someone.

“Hara-unnie!” Sulli pulled her mask down and hailed her.

Goo Hara looked up. The sunglasses came off. There were honest dark circles under her eyes and long-haul fatigue over everything — and then she spotted Sulli, and all of it was instantly outvoted by a wide, bright smile.

She sped up, suitcase clattering.

“Sulli!” The case was abandoned mid-roll, and her arms went around Sulli tight.

“You must be wrecked, unnie — flying home all by yourself.” Sulli patted her back.

“I’m okay. Just couldn’t sleep on the plane.” Only then, letting go, did she register the man standing behind Sulli.

Short-sleeved cardigan open over a tee, black suit trousers below. Tall — the height registered first — and standing with the settled stillness of furniture that had chosen where it lived.

“Unnie, this is the oppa I told you about — Song Ji-yeon-oppa. He’s the one who drove me out today.” Sulli stepped aside and presented him like a prize.

Goo Hara gave him a once-over — unhurried, professional — then bowed, small and correct. “Annyeonghaseyo. I’m Goo Hara. Thank you for making the trip.”

Polite. Reserved. A first-meeting quantity of guard.

“Annyeonghaseyo. Song Ji-yeon.” He nodded, reached over, and took the suitcase handle from her hand. “Car’s in the basement. Let’s head down — too many eyes out here.”

And he simply set off toward the elevators, the case rolling after him. The thing was visibly heavy and appeared to weigh nothing at all in his grip.

Goo Hara blinked once — then quickened her steps to catch up, falling in beside Sulli.

Underground, he stopped at the car, tapped the key, and the lights blinked twice. Trunk open; he took the suitcase two-handed, hoisted it in like a bag of groceries, closed the lid, then pulled the rear door open and looked at her.

“Thank you.” She sat.

Sulli claimed the passenger seat. He slid back behind the wheel and pointed the car toward the city.

Goo Hara slipped out of the trench coat and laid it on the seat beside her, answered two or three messages, then settled back and closed her eyes.

“Unnie… you’re really tired, huh.” Sulli twisted around to look at her.

“A little. We did three concerts back to back — I was living out of the practice room and the venue.” She opened her eyes and gave Sulli a small smile. “But I’m in Seoul now, and the company gave me four days off.”

“Then tonight we eat our strength back!” Sulli swung toward the front seat. “Oppa — barbecue. That same place we went last time.”

“Works.” He was hungry himself, come to think of it — his eyes flicked up to the rearview mirror. “Hara-ssi — that good for you?”

Her eyes happened to already be there. For exactly one second, two gazes met in the glass.

She nodded. “That’s fine. Sulli and I eat there all the time.”

The two of them talked on and off the whole way — idle, easy, daily life trading at par, one reporting a Japan tour while the other reported a drama set. Song Ji-yeon mostly drove.

✦ ✦ ✦

SATURDAY EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Umi barbecue house — the inner room

The car turned into a quiet Cheongdam alley and stopped in front of Umi — the same barbecue house Sulli had treated him at that first time, to thank him for the stock tip. He still remembered the proprietress.

Through the door:

“Ajumma! We’re back!”

“Sulli and Hara! And no warning at all — if you’d called ahead, I’d have saved you the best hanwoo in the house!”

“Ajumma’s hanwoo doesn’t need saving — every cut in this place is top shelf—”

The fortysomething proprietress laughed until she folded, like a chrysanthemum arriving at full bloom. She came out to receive them personally and led the three of them herself to the innermost ground-floor room.

“Eat well. If anything runs short, you come tell ajumma.”

The menu came down. Song Ji-yeon didn’t so much as glance at it — he slid it across the table to the girls.

“You two order.”

Sulli ordered like a girl with no enemies anywhere: the premium hanwoo platter — the most expensive item in the book — plus two more meats, a seafood stew, and a bowl of bibim naengmyeon. Goo Hara added a dolsot bibimbap.

Song Ji-yeon ordered the same bibimbap. He hadn’t eaten a single thing all day, and the hunger had recently upgraded from advisory to mandatory.

The proprietress gathered the menus, beaming. “Not enough, you tell ajumma — refills are on the house.”

“Thank you, ajumma — this is plenty.” Mask off, sweetness deployed at full volume.

“One moment then — I’ll have everything out right away. I’ll leave you to it!”

“Ne, ajumma.” The two girls answered together.

The charcoal arrived first, then the banchan, covering the table edge to edge. The meat came out on wooden boards — marbled hanwoo cut thick, portions with nothing shy about them.

Song Ji-yeon revised his thesis, once again: a cute face is a license. It really is.

A server moved to stay and grill for the table.

“We’re fine — we’ll handle it.” He waved the server out. He’d eaten enough meals with idols by now that the protocol ran on autopilot: tongs up, two slices of marbled beef down onto the grate. The charcoal was at full roar; the meat answered instantly — a hiss, and a bloom of white smoke.

Goo Hara went visibly uncomfortable. First meeting; letting an unfamiliar man work the grill was, table-manners-wise, somewhere between poor form and a crime. She shot a glare at Sulli — who was sitting with her chopsticks in her mouth, waiting to be fed — and reached out to relieve him of the tongs. “Song Ji-yeon-ssi — I’ve got this! We can’t make you—”

“It’s fine. I’ll grill.” He waved her off. “I run restaurants for a living. This is home turf.”

Sulli caught Hara’s arm and pulled her back down into her seat. “Unnie, just sit. Oppa’s technique is on another level — miles better than yours.”

Hara had no words. This isn’t about taste. This is about manners.

Little peach. And to think I never noticed you were this rude.

He reached over and tapped Sulli once on the forehead with one finger. “You little glutton.”

She giggled — thoroughly unrepentant — and fixed her eyes on his hands at the grill.

He worked the marbled beef with an easy, practiced rhythm — flip, check the fire, done, one motion — then took up the scissors, snipped the meat into bites, and dealt them out to the empty plates on both sides of the table.

“Eat.”

Sulli nabbed a piece, dragged it through the salt, and put it straight in her mouth — then sat there huffing air around the heat, absolutely refusing to spit it out. “Sooo good. Unnie, hurry, try it—”

Goo Hara picked up a piece with her chopsticks and ate it slowly.

She ate quietly. One piece finished, she lifted the barley-tea cup and took a small sip — and her eyes began making quiet little trips between Sulli and Song Ji-yeon, back and forth, back and forth, replaying every single interaction since the arrivals gate.

These two… weren’t they a little too close?

Gulp.

She might have just discovered something huge.

These two didn’t look like friends at all… If anything, they looked like—
