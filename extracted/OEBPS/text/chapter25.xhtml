Chapter Twenty-Five

Bae Joo-hyun and Kang Seul-gi

SEPTEMBER 2012 Cheongdam-dong, Seoul ATER — the weekend-morning queue

On a weekend morning, the drinks shop called Ater had a queue that reached out its glass door and a good way down the block.

Ji-yeon pocketed the Mercedes key and took his place at the end of the line, amusing himself with the room. The door swung shut behind him and sealed in the shop’s signature atmosphere — dark-roast beans, charred and bitter, braided together with something syrupy and sweet, like two people having an argument in the air.

His gaze drifted across two tall figures at the counter, mid-order — and snagged, hard, on the profile of one of the girls.

Most of her face was buried in the shadow of a deep-gray hood, but the line of that profile was pure advantage — features as if they’d been carved out of white porcelain by someone with a grudge against imperfection. Under the shop’s dim, cool lighting, she looked less like a person than like an oil painting that had wandered out of its frame.

Bae Joo-hyun. The name landed in his head before he’d finished looking. Of course it did — in another life, that face would spend a full decade at the top of every fourth-generation visual ranking in the business. Irene, before there was an Irene.

And the sight of her — green as she still was, the beauty already unreasonable — standing beside a thoroughly miserable-looking Kang Seul-gi, sent the needle of his memory spinning backward. Lightning-quick, he finally solved it: the girl curled up dead asleep on the office sofa that night. Who she had been.

Monolid eyes. A jawline with a soft, faintly dazed curve to it.

That was the future Red Velvet all-round ace, wasn’t it. Seulgi.

So Director Kang’s precious daughter was her — no wonder the girl had radiated that unplaceable familiarity all night. Honestly, his own fault. He’d spent an entire past life knowing her as a poster name, a screen presence, a position in a lineup — never as somebody’s daughter, waiting up for her father in a dim lobby. If he’d ever once thought of her that way, he’d have placed her at first glance.

The worldlines on this peninsula are converging at a genuinely unhinged rate.

While he was privately marveling, Kang Seul-gi — standing beside Bae Joo-hyun — happened to glance back, and her eyes collided head-on with his.

The girl jumped as if prodded. Her monolid eyes went perfectly round; then, moving like a rabbit that has heard a twig snap, she seized Joo-hyun’s sleeve and — right there, in the middle of the shop — bent into a textbook ninety-degree bow.

“Sajangnim! Annyeonghaseyo!”

She’d tried to keep her voice down. It didn’t help. The words came out sanded down to gravel — as if her throat had been dragged across a beach — and in the quiet morning shop, every head turned.

Bae Joo-hyun, bewildered but quick on the uptake, followed her friend’s lead and bent into a bow just as precise.

“Seul-gi-ssi. We meet again.” Ji-yeon stepped forward, smiling, waving the two of them upright. “Small world. Though — what happened to your voice? That night at the office, it didn’t sound anywhere near this bad.”

He looked her over, honestly puzzled. Seul-gi scratched at the back of her neck, cornered and sheepish, and answered in that shredded whisper of hers.

“Ne… it’s — the month-end evaluations are coming up, so practice ran long. I, um. Cracked it.”

“She went at the high notes like they owed her money.” The explanation came from beside her, soft and cool and lovely all at once. “The doctor says it’s the start of vocal nodules.”

Ji-yeon glanced over at the fourth-generation visual-in-waiting. The trainee green hadn’t worn off this one yet either — an oversized gray hoodie, a bare face glowing white even under bad lighting, and that cool, self-possessed quiet, which in a shop full of Saturday noise stood out like a held breath.

“The evaluations matter. But your voice is your capital — and if the capital breaks down, Director Kang will have to come to me for leave so he can stay home and nurse you back to health.”

The joke landed; the awkward weight in the air dissolved on the spot.

It was only hearing the unnie beside her speak that reminded Seul-gi of her manners: introductions.

“Oh — right. This is my same-class trainee, my chingu — Bae Joo-hyun.” She tugged the girl forward by the sleeve and presented her, then turned back around, earnest as a form letter. “Joo-hyun-unnie, this is my appa’s company’s Sajangnim. He’s really, really nice.”

Joo-hyun bowed again, shallow and exact, eyes clear.

“Annyeonghaseyo. I’m Bae Joo-hyun — I’m an SM trainee right now.”

Ji-yeon returned the bow, privately concluding that this girl’s beauty was simply unreasonable — there was no arguing with it, no context that softened it.

“An SM trainee — that’s a hard road. Seul-gi-ya, mind that throat of yours. Don’t lose the instrument before the evaluation even arrives.”

Seul-gi grinned her simple, sunny grin, then stole a curious look at him. Young as she was, she had quick instincts: she didn’t ask why on earth he’d appeared here of all places. Only, politely:

“What brings Sajangnim out buying drinks? Are you here alone?”

“I live just around the corner. Day off — I’m visiting a friend’s drama set over in Yeouido, so I stopped in to load up a carful of drinks for the crew.” He tipped his head at the Mercedes parked outside.

Then he made the mistake of looking at the menu.

Flashy, backlit, dense. English stacked on Korean stacked on acronyms, a full wall of it. His eyes began to water.

He’d been raised on hot tea — his mother’s boricha, from a kettle that was never once allowed to go cold — and to this day he kept a wary distance from the iced americano that the people of this peninsula appeared to regard as a vital organ. In his considered opinion, the stuff tasted like the hanyak his mother used to brew whenever he caught so much as a sniffle as a kid. Medicine. Boiled.

“I don’t usually come to places like this. You two are the professionals — what’s good? Something that isn’t bitter, and doesn’t coat your throat in syrup.”

He turned to his two consultants with an open face, ready to be taught.

The sight of a major company’s CEO stumped by a drinks menu proved too much for both of them. Seul-gi and Joo-hyun traded one look and had to bite down on matching smiles.

“If iced americano isn’t Song Ji-yeon-ssi’s thing, you could try the peach-oolong cold brew. It’s the house signature.” Joo-hyun reached out and tapped a spot on the menu, delivering the recommendation with complete seriousness. “Clean. And not too sweet.”

“Ne! And the iced grapefruit soda is so good — me and Joo-hyun-unnie get one every single time evaluations are over!” Seul-gi threw her whole heart into the second recommendation, shredded voice and all, sketching the shape of a very large cup in the air with both hands.

“Sold. On your recommendations, then.” He turned to the cashier and surrendered. “Thirty peach-oolong cold brews, twenty iced grapefruit sodas — plus whatever these two young ladies were ordering. All of it on my card.”

He handed the card over.

“Ah — Song Ji-yeon-ssi, you can’t—” Joo-hyun’s composure slipped for the first time, hands coming up in flustered refusal. Hers was a cool, slow-warming temperament; a stranger’s generosity sat on her like a coat borrowed in the middle of summer.

“Sajangnim, my appa says never spend other people’s money outside the house!” Seul-gi’s hands were waving too.

“Your appa isn’t here.”

The card was already back in his wallet.

“Besides — the two of you just solved a major ordering crisis for me. Consider the drinks your consulting fee. Go on, go wait by the pickup counter.” A nod at Seul-gi, gentle and unarguable. “And you — throat like that, ease off the ice.”

Faced with that smile — soft, and completely non-negotiable — the two of them gave up, shut their mouths, and bowed their thanks.

Ji-yeon didn’t try to stretch the conversation out. The Handbook hadn’t bound either of them; he barely knew them; forced small talk would only have embarrassed all three parties. Some meetings you let end while they’re still pleasant.

Half an hour later, a server came out balancing racks of fifty sealed cups. He said his goodbyes to Seul-gi and Joo-hyun, loaded the trunk, dropped into the driver’s seat, and pulled the Mercedes onto Olympic-daero, running smooth and quick toward Yeouido.

✦ ✦ ✦

Inside the shop, the two girls watched the car slide out of its spot and into traffic. Seul-gi let out a long, deflating breath.

“Whew. He’s gone. I did not take one full breath the entire time Sajangnim was standing there.”

Joo-hyun took an unhurried sip of her peach oolong.

“What, are you scared of him?”

“Please.” Seul-gi’s head shook, and her voice dropped into a wounded murmur. “That is my appa’s company’s Sajangnim we are discussing. You’re exactly the same when Lee Soo-man-sunsaengnim walks past.”

“But he’s so young. He doesn’t look like anyone’s Sajangnim.”

That stopped Seul-gi short. She honestly didn’t know the first thing about him — last time, she’d heard her father call him Sajangnim and picked up his name, and that was the entire file. She shook her head slowly, puppy eyes brimming with open confusion, and Joo-hyun watched her short-circuit with fond resignation.

“All right. Back to earth.” A hand waved once in front of Seul-gi’s eyes. “If you can’t work it out, stop working on it. We’ve been out a while — tidy up. Back to the company. There’s afternoon practice.”
