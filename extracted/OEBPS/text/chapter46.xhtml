Chapter Forty-Six

The Rules of Binding

SAME EVENING · CONTINUOUS Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Fūbutsu — the corner booth

Joo-hyun looked at him. Then at the table. Then at him again.

The air held still for two full seconds.

Then she studied his face — on which the words mortified and absolutely not saying so were printed side by side in bold — and something in her expression flickered dangerously toward a laugh.

She dug into her hoodie pocket, produced a bank card, and handed it to the server, who had materialized beside the table at the single worst-timed moment in the meal’s history.

“This one, please.”

The motion was crisp end to end — card out, card over, card back — and her voice stayed cool and level through all of it, as if the bill for this dinner had been hers to pay from the moment they sat down.

“…How much was it?” He watched the server walk away. “I’ll transfer it to you tonight.”

“No need.” The card went back into her pocket, her tone flat as still water. “Call it thanks. For catching Mushroom.”

She had been chased. He had stepped in. Somewhere in that sequence there was, technically, a rescue — but even she could hear how thin the logic rattled, and she applied it anyway.

His conscience, meanwhile, got louder by the second.

A company president. A dinner staged as a formal apology for his own dog. And the girl pays. In what ledger did that entry look acceptable?

“Absolutely not.” He stood, resolute enough to make it a principle. “This dinner was my apology — mine to make. Next time. Next time I’m treating, and I’m not taking arguments.”

“We’ll see.” She rose too, entirely noncommittal, in the tone of a person filing his promise under maybe, someday.

They stepped out of Fūbutsu one after the other, into a September night that had finally remembered it was autumn. The wind carried a thread of chill now; the lane was quiet, and the traffic of the main street was down to a distant tide.

Mushroom, freshly released from his nap, went instantly feral with joy, orbiting the two of them in tight figure eights.

“I’ll head off.” She tugged her hood up, the goodbye barely louder than the traffic.

“Wait.” He stopped her with a word before she could move. “Where are you headed — the company? The dorm?”

“Apgujeong. The practice rooms.”

“I’ll walk you.” He lifted the leash. “Call it a digestion walk.”

Her feet paused. She looked back at him.

The streetlight stood behind him, laying a soft blur of gold along his outline. His eyes were level, unhurried, asking for nothing — he simply intended to see her there, and that was the entire content of the offer.

“There’s no need. It’s not far — I’ll be there in two blocks.”

Apgujeong and Cheongdam were neighbors, both folded into Gangnam; the distance between them was a technicality.

“Still no.” Firmer this time. “You bought dinner, and then I send you off alone into the dark? What exactly does that make me?”

He stepped forward and simply fell into pace beside her, the matter no longer listed as open for discussion.

“Walk. I’ve been wanting a look at SM’s old building anyway.”

Whatever arguments she had left, she examined them, found them non-viable against that particular tone, and silently picked up her pace instead.

They walked down Cheongdam’s quiet streets, and neither spent a word for a while. Mushroom ran point up ahead, tail high, checking back over his shoulder at intervals. Faster, humans. Faster.

“Do you practice this late every night?” He broke the silence first. He remembered the file on her — the industry’s famous grinder, first one through the door in the morning, last one to leave it at night.

“More or less.” A small nod. “Month-end evaluations. There’s no letting up.”

“Is the pressure always like this? For all of you?”

“You get used to it.” Her voice had gone light, a breeze passing through. “If you don’t push, you get cut. It’s the same for everyone.”

The evenness of it — that was what sat wrong in his chest. He knew exactly what it took to stand in that building: nobody ordinary ever got through SM’s front door, and the hours these girls paid were denominated in years. What ordinary people imagined as effort, and what a trainee meant by it, were not the same unit.

“You’re going to debut.”

The same words as at dinner. This time he put more weight on them.

Her step checked, half a beat. She turned her head.

Under the streetlight his profile was all clean lines, and there was something in his eyes that made a person want to believe him on credit.

“How can you be so confident… about me?” It slipped out before she could reroute it.

“Intuition.” A small smile. “Mine doesn’t miss.”

She didn’t answer. Her head dropped, and the hood’s shadow swallowed her face — but at one corner of her mouth a curve rose that nobody had authorized.

Soon enough an aged three-story building came into view, its old beige face worn soft by the years.

“Here.” She stopped and lifted a hand toward it.

“So that’s SM’s original headquarters.” He tipped his head back and gave it an appraising once-over. “Respectable. It’s got the air.”

“Then… I’m going up.” She turned and bowed — small, exact, correct to the degree. “Thank you for dinner today.”

Even though she had paid for dinner today.

“It’s me who should be thanking you.” He rubbed the bridge of his nose, half-laughing, sheepish.

And looking at her standing there — cool as the first day he’d met her, courteous to the exact millimeter — the thought he’d been carrying in his back pocket all evening put a hand on his shoulder.

The experiment. The contact test.

He reached out and rested his hand lightly on the top of her head. One pat. Two.

“Fighting.”

Delivered with perfect sincerity, in the exact manner of a senior encouraging a junior — natural, warm, and entirely on purpose.

Joo-hyun went rigid from the collarbones up.

Her head snapped up. Her eyes — clear, wide — were fully occupied by shock, with a fine grain of panic running underneath it.

In her entire life, no man outside her own family had ever dared to pat her head.

She took one step back, automatic, wary as a stray shown a net.

He froze too — then read the situation directly off her face and ended up somewhere between laughing and apologizing.

“Don’t overthink it—” Both hands spread, the very portrait of innocence. “You’ve been grinding all day. It was encouragement! What exactly is running around loose in that head of yours?”

Her face caught fire from the ears down.

One second too late, she understood exactly what her reaction had just announced to the world.

“I— I wasn’t thinking anything!” Delivered chin-first, at full stubbornness. Then she turned and fled at a dead sprint, like something with fangs was gaining on her.

He laughed out loud at her retreating back.

A whole ice field, this girl, right up until the moment you poked her — and then it was bristle in every direction.

He stood where he was and watched until she had vanished through SM’s doors, and only then did the smile fold itself away.

He closed his eyes. In the privacy of his own skull, he knocked on the system’s door — politely, formally, the way you knock when you already know nobody is home.

Nothing.

“Figured. Still nothing.”

He sighed, gathered up a Mushroom who was reading the news off every lamp post, and turned back the way they had come.

So the contact mechanism was a far stranger animal than he had modeled. It wasn’t touch — not touch alone. It certainly wasn’t surface area; he had patted Sulli’s forehead like a temple drum and gotten silence for it, and tonight he had patted a head and gotten silence again. The sample kept growing, and the easy theory kept dying.

Eun-jung’s binding had come at the bottom of her worst hour — wrung out, frightened, leaning on him without even knowing she was leaning.

Ji-won’s had been a life snatched back at the hinge of a second.

And Sulli’s… Sulli’s he still couldn’t reconstruct, even now. That file remained open.

But the pattern was already standing on its own two feet, assembled from the two bindings he had witnessed: every one of them had arrived bolted to a spike of real emotion. Dependence. Gratitude. Shock. The genuine article, at full voltage.

And the head-pat he had just administered — from where Joo-hyun stood, it was a near-stranger putting a hand on her head uninvited. Abrupt. Presumptuous. A little bit flippant.

Hence: silence.

“Tch.” He shook his head slowly. “After all that — it only counts when it’s real.”

He looked down at Mushroom, who was wagging at him with the clear conscience of a dog who had never once, in his entire life, done anything wrong.

“So much for running the playboy playbook and farming rewards. The system won’t even extend credit.”

Man and dog disappeared slowly into the Cheongdam night.
