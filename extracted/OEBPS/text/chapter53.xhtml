Chapter Fifty-Three

If You Won’t Make a Move, I Will

SUNDAY · NOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s flat, first floor

Upstairs, Park Ji-yeon was still furnishing a home she didn’t yet hold keys to. Downstairs, Song Ji-yeon and rep Kim handled the half of the morning that came with numbers attached — term, deposit, monthly, utilities — and settled on tomorrow morning for the signing.

Business concluded, the three of them saw Kim off at the elevator and dropped back down one floor, into the flat.

“Done and dusted!” The door had barely shut behind them when Park Ji-yeon’s fingers snapped, crisp with delight. “To celebrate me finding a new home — big lunch today! My treat!”

“You only just got off a plane from Hong Kong this morning. Aren’t you tired?” Eun-jung eyed her, openly worried.

“Tired? Not even a little! Good news is better than any energy drink!” A wave of the hand, dismissal complete — and then her whole face swung toward the kitchen, expectant. “Oppa, what are you in the mood for? Barbecue? Or your own hotpot place?”

Song Ji-yeon checked his watch. Eleven-thirty. He weighed the maknae’s nuclear-battery spirits against the faint bruise of fatigue under Eun-jung’s eyes, and made his proposal.

“Eating out’s a hassle. Let’s just eat here — I stocked the fridge properly a couple of days ago. There’s everything.”

“Here?!” Park Ji-yeon’s eyes went somehow brighter still. “Yes! Yes yes yes — I’ve never once had your cooking, oppa!”

Eun-jung’s own heart tipped forward without permission. The mala crayfish he’d made that time was good enough that she could still taste it in memory, even now. The thought of his cooking again had her looking forward to it, a little more than she meant to.

“Then… are you sure it isn’t too much trouble?” Politeness made one last stand.

“No trouble.” He smiled. “Call it a housewarming dinner for my future neighbor. Paid in advance.”

That single sentence set Park Ji-yeon’s heart in full bloom.

“Oppa, you’re the BEST!”

And off she went — no invitation required, no hesitation shown — straight to the refrigerator, door open, head inside, inspecting his groceries like a customs officer.

Comfortable. Remarkably comfortable. Was there a step in there where she asked?

“Waah, oppa, your fridge is PACKED. Beef, pork belly, every vegetable known to science… let’s do barbecue today!”

He came over, bent into the fridge after her, and surfaced sheepishly, one hand at the back of his head.

“Barbecue… I don’t actually own a griddle. Stir-fry is what’s on offer.”

It wasn’t that he had anything against barbecue. But he’d been in this flat a month and change, cooking for an audience of one, and a man doesn’t buy a griddle to feed himself — casual wok nights had covered it, and the appliance aisle had never once called his name.

“No griddle?” Park Ji-yeon blinked, honestly thrown. “Oppa, your flat is THIS big. How do you not own a griddle?”

“Live alone, eat alone — barbecue never really comes up.” A shrug. “Stir-fry’s just as good, though. My cooking holds up. I won’t let you down.”

She tilted her head, gave it a moment of legislative review, then split into a grin.

“Fine by me! I love Chinese stir-fry too — that kung pao chicken at the Chinese place in Myeongdong last time? I put away three bowls of rice. THREE.”

From the side, Eun-jung’s mouth corner lifted without asking her. This child — in all her nineteen years, she had never once been picky about food.

“Then today you finally get a proper taste of Song Ji-yeon-ssi’s cooking.” A light push at the maknae’s shoulder, and a firm nod. “I’ll guarantee it — better than that Myeongdong place.”

“Chincha?!”

Her eyes flew wide — and then, behind them, the little gears spun up. Fast. Suspiciously fast. Hold on. How could unnie possibly be that confident about oppa’s cooking? Unless… she’d eaten it. Here. At his table.

By the time the gears stopped, Park Ji-yeon was certain of it, down to her socks: there was something between these two, and it was not nothing.

The dragon now deployed her genius eyes, pinning Eun-jung in place from across the living room. Keep your secrets, unnie. Go on. I’ve got you now~ EUN-JUNG-UNNIE!!!

Eun-jung bore the weird stare as long as she could, and genuine concern began to edge out confusion. The maknae had been strange all morning — was the aftermath of that whole bullying mess still rattling around in her head somewhere? She walked over, reached up, and patted Park Ji-yeon’s head gently.

“Ji-yeon-ah. Is something wrong with your head? You’ve been odd all day.”

“We settle accounts tonight,” came the whisper — one arm hooking her, one shove, and Eun-jung went down into the sofa cushions with the maknae on top of her, fingers dug into the soft spot above her waist. Then, volume up, bright and merciless, aimed at the kitchen: “I’ll hold you to that, oppa! First I have to go deal with Eun-jung-unnie — she says I’m sick in the head!”

Song Ji-yeon tied the apron strings, took one appreciative glance at the two-woman war breaking out in his living room, and left them to it.

“Go park yourselves somewhere. Won’t be long.”

Park Ji-yeon had Eun-jung pinned flat, hands poised at her unnie’s ticklish flank, and bent close to her ear.

“Unnie. Confess. When did you two get together?”

Eun-jung froze — one full second — and then the light arrived. She went scarlet from the collar up and shoved the maknae clean off her.

“Ji-yeon… what are you even saying. We are not together.”

Unbothered by the shove, Park Ji-yeon settled back on her heels, the tease sitting openly across her face.

“Then explain why oppa’s house is full of Mushroom’s things. And haven’t you been here before, unnie? And eaten his cooking!”

Eun-jung’s thoughts stopped dead. Since when was the group’s baby maknae this sharp? How — how had she worked that out?

Pinned under the teasing gaze, she felt panic arrive from nowhere in particular and start rearranging her sentences.

“We’re really not together… there was the Hong Kong trip, and — and nobody to watch Mushroom, so I asked him to dog-sit for a few days. And the meal — meals are just meals! That’s all that was!”

A reasonable explanation. Comprehensive. Airtight, even.

Park Ji-yeon believed approximately none of it.

Half a YEAR, unnie. You’ve had Mushroom half a year. Every overseas schedule before this one, you never once asked any other man on this earth to dog-sit!!!

So who exactly are these words supposed to fool?

She fixed Eun-jung with the particular look of a girl saying: you can fool me, unnie — just don’t fool yourself. Eun-jung wilted under it, stammered something without consonants, and fled — up off the sofa and into the open kitchen, to wash vegetables with the urgency of a woman escaping a fire.

Park Ji-yeon watched the retreat with deep satisfaction. The theory had been revised — fine, they weren’t together. Yet. But Eun-jung-unnie was undeniably, unmistakably plotting something with Song Ji-yeon-oppa. The only problem was the paper wall between them, and nobody had so much as breathed on it.

✦ ✦ ✦

In the kitchen, the roar of the wok wound down, and the last plate came off the fire — green pepper and pork slivers, landing on white china, jade against sauce-brown, appetite on sight alone.

Song Ji-yeon unhooked the apron and dropped it over a chair back. He came out with the dishes balanced up both arms.

“Food’s ready.”

The aroma had reached the living room well ahead of him. Park Ji-yeon, who seconds ago had been boneless against the sofa cushions, materialized at the table.

The dishes went down one by one. Wok-tossed beef. Pork with green chili. Tomato and egg. And a cabbage-and-tofu soup to hold the table together. Three dishes and a soup — the plainest home-style cooking there was, plated like it had somewhere to be.

“Waah—” Her eyes ran the whole table, glowing. “It looks SO good!”

The praise came out of her at full price, no discounts.

Eun-jung followed him out, a bowl of rice in each hand.

“Sit, sit — see what you think.”

She set the two bowls down in front of the other two, ducked back into the kitchen for her own, and pulled out a chair.

Park Ji-yeon’s patience had lasted exactly until the first bowl touched the table. Chopsticks up — and straight into the tomato and egg, a fat curl of it, gone.

The egg came out fluffy-soft, the tomato’s sweet-sour glaze wrapped clean around every edge of it. Rich, bright, appetite-tearing.

“Mmph!” Her eyes shut into slits of pure bliss, the words fighting their way around a full mouth. “Good. SO good!”

She swallowed, breathed, and opened the second front — the beef was next, already in her sights.

Eun-jung watched her eat, laughing before she knew it, and took up her own chopsticks. Small, unhurried bites. It really was good.

Above everything else — warm. The taste of home.

“Ji-yeon-ah. Slower. Nobody is racing you.”

“Unnie, you don’t understand. Figure management — I had honestly forgotten what meat tastes like.” A chopstick of pork, gesturing with it, pleading her case mid-felony. “Today I am recouping the principal. With interest.”

Song Ji-yeon watched the two of them orbit each other and found the whole show quietly excellent.

“If you like it, eat more. There’s plenty in the wok.”

“Thank you, oppa!”

Sweet-mouthed to the end — and her chopsticks never so much as paused. Somewhere behind the eating, a ledger was being drawn up: a man who could cook, installed directly downstairs, permanently. Honestly? Not a bad arrangement at all. She slid one eye at Eun-jung, head down over her rice, and dispatched a silent prayer heavenward: hurry up and land him, unnie. Think of the meals.

But then she looked at Eun-jung again — that serene, undisturbed, nothing-to-do-with-me face — and the fuse lit all over again.

If you won’t make a move, unnie — then I will!!!
