Chapter One Hundred Six

You Knew, and You Still Came Over

SUNDAY MORNING · CONTINUED Seodaemun-gu, Seoul Ewha Womans University — the bench on the main path, the lawn shoot next door

“Ridiculous? Not even slightly. If you want it, it’s possible.”

He didn’t decorate the answer. It came out clean and flat and certain, the way a scale reads a number.

He remembered, from another turn of the calendar, how her file had actually gone: out of the School of Performing Arts and straight past the college entrance exam — a top girl group’s schedule eats whole weeks, and the industry had long since ruled the two lives oil and water.

But memory was the old world’s business. Encouraging her was this world’s.

And besides — with Song Ji-yeon now standing in the river of this timeline, diverting its current, if everything still ran exactly along the old channel, what precisely had he come back for?

“Idol and entrance exam at once — yes, it’s brutal. Nobody said brutal means impossible. People have done it. I think you’re one of the people who can.”

Something in Sulli settled, like a floor finally arriving under her feet.

“Really?”

“Really. If you’ve decided you want it, then want it out loud. Stop rehearsing reasons it can’t work.”

She nodded — seemed about to reach for the next question—

— and a bright, unhurried voice arrived from behind them.

“What are you two whispering about over here?”

They turned, and Goo Hara was strolling toward the bench, dressed like the concept of autumn had been handed a styling budget.

Remove the idol from the frame and the photograph assembles itself: one campus goddess, off duty.

A NOTE ON THE PUBLICATION · ELLEGIRL

ELLEgirl — the younger sister title of America’s ELLE, aimed squarely at readers aged ten to twenty-two.

The American edition had folded in 2006, but the international editions lived on, running across a dozen markets with reach that was nothing to sneeze at.

“Hara-unnie!”

Sulli lit up and waved her over.

Hara covered the last few steps, cracked the bottle open, and took a long drink before dropping down onto the bench beside Sulli.

The bench being what it was — small — Song Ji-yeon judged three people to be a crowd, stood, and settled against the wooden backrest instead.

“What were you two so cozy about?”

“Nothing much. University life.” He kept it brief.

“University life, ah…”

The bottle came to rest on her knee, and her gaze drifted out to the lawn, to the loose clusters of students sunning themselves — and there it was again, the same naked longing, as if the two girls had been copying each other’s homework.

“Technically speaking, I’m a university student too, you know.”

Her smile bent itself into a wry shape.

“But because of the idol job, I’m either running to a schedule or in a van running to the next one, and when the day’s broadcasts finally end, the only thing I want from the world is my own bed. Campus life? I’ve basically never had one. The last time I even set foot on campus was the festival a month ago. The company put KARA on the stage, we performed, and the second it ended we were in the van racing to the next university’s festival. I never walked around a single one of them.” A pause, and the regret in her voice had real weight. “It’s a genuine shame.”

A NOTE ON THE CALENDAR · THE CAMPUS FESTIVAL

In Korea, a university festival is not an anniversary ceremony — it is a carnival. Twice a year, May and September, three days to a full week: the school books celebrities, the clubs perform, and food stalls colonize every path.

For idols, festival season runs at comeback-promotion intensity — a top girl group will hit three, four, five campuses in a single day. Stage, van, highway, stage.

KARA was a first-tier group now. A frantic festival calendar was simply the tax on that.

Sulli reached over and wrapped both hands around one of hers.

“Unni. There’s still time. The chances will come.”

“Mm.” A small smile.

He watched the university topic exhale its last breath, and checked his mental clock.

“Are you finished shooting?”

He’d left home a little past nine. It was now past eleven, and eleven was the front porch of lunch.

Hara shook her head, apology flickering across her face.

“One more set, I think, and we’re done.”

“Then hurry back, unni, before the staff start pacing.” Sulli turned solicitous.

“It’s fine — it’s the break. The photographer’s going through shots.” She waved this off, and then turned to Song Ji-yeon and produced a grin of suspicious wattage. “Is oppa hungry?”

Today’s whole arrangement was her invitation — she was the one who had left him waiting on a bench half the morning — and it sat awkwardly on her.

She’d asked about his birthday once, back near the beginning. Born in 1990 — a year ahead of her. The oppa was load-bearing.

“Not a bottomless pit, no. You came over, and I assumed you’d wrapped.”

“Wrapping? Far from it.” She laughed. “I saw you two laughing over here and couldn’t help drifting over. Why — oppa not welcoming? It sounds like I’m crashing something here…”

She let the pause stretch, and then dropped the words with an arch little curl at the end.

“…your little world of two.”

The phrase landed in Sulli’s chest like a stone into still water, and the rings went out and out and out.

Her face began its ascent toward red.

The truth of the two of them was a border with no fence drawn on it yet. There had been kisses — but those were her doing, ambushes she had launched herself, out of nowhere, before her courage could talk her out of it. Nothing had ever been said out loud. Nothing had been named.

And now, in the open air, in front of him, someone had put a label on it.

“Unni! What are you even saying?!”

It came out too fast, and the panic rode just under the surface, thin enough to hear.

Watching the not-remotely-subtle denial, Hara’s grin went from bright to blinding. She leaned in close, nudged her with an elbow, eyebrows doing choreography, and dropped her voice to a channel only the two of them received.

“Still pretending? Look at you — how red can one face even get? It’s a peach. A fully ripe peach. One more degree and somebody’s just going to take a bite.”

“I am not—!”

Sulli’s palms flew to her own cheeks and found them running a fever.

She wanted to fight back, and there was no opening. Claim they were just friends? They had kissed. Claim they were dating? He had never once said so.

An in-between thing like theirs is the thing most allergic to daylight. The more you try to explain it in public, the tighter the knot pulls.

She shot Hara a glare.

The glare weighed nothing. It was all soft edges — closer to pleading, closer to aegyo than to anger — and both of them knew it.

Delighted, Hara escalated. She swung toward the man of the pair, who had been watching all of this with nothing but amusement, and drew the words out long and deliberate, like taffy.

“Oppa. Am I right, or am I right? Am I not interrupting something? Something like a… date?”

She had budgeted for every reaction. A laugh and a dodge. A cough and a subject change. A what, us? We’re colleagues, delivered with the right amount of panic.

What she had not budgeted for was the man picking the ball up and running it straight through the middle of the field.

“You knew.” A beat. “And you still came over to interrupt.”

Delivered flat. Faint smile. No elevated pulse.

He knew she was joking. She knew he knew she was joking. He had simply decided to joke back with live ammunition.

Somewhere in the back of his mind, though, a small observation was being filed away: had Hara always been this warm? They had met exactly once before this, and on that occasion she had treated him with the crisp efficiency of a bank teller. Now here she was, lobbing jokes about him and Sulli.

Women, honestly. The weather changes slower.

Across the bench, Sulli’s entire operating system locked up. She stared up at him, mouth slightly open, the sentence still ringing in the air.

And on Hara’s face, the smile — slowly, frame by frame — froze.

She had imagined denial. She had imagined deflection. She had not, in any branch of any timeline, imagined him simply confirming it. Out loud. In front of both of them.

Hadn’t Sulli told her they weren’t official? Is this how a man acts when the ambiguous thing gets called out in public? She had only been joking!

The joke-er had just become the joke-ee.

“Yah… oppa, you…”

The sentence collapsed under its own weight.

It was like throwing her whole weight into a punch and hitting a mattress. The fist vanished into the soft; the mattress swallowed it; then the mattress pushed back, gently, and she staggered.

How is his skin this thick?! Who just — admits it?! States it, in the open, like announcing a lunch order?! Can he not see that Sulli is one degree from tunneling into the ground?!

It took several full seconds for her voice to find its way home. Then she slapped a hand over her chest with theatrical devastation.

“Wow — Daebak! I have never been so wrong about a human being! Song Ji-yeon-oppa, a face this thick should be running for president! Anything less is a waste of natural resources!”

Beside her, Sulli’s face had passed through red and was now exploring the territory beyond it.

The sentence was still going off inside her like a depth charge — wave after wave, ears ringing.

What did it mean?

Was he saying they were dating? Was he announcing it — staking a flag in front of Hara-unnie?

The thoughts came in a mob, shoving each other, and under the shame and the indignation there was a third thing — sweet, panicked, and unnamed — that she would not let herself look at directly.

She raised her head and delivered her strongest glare of the day.

It contained: reproach. Embarrassment. And, tucked into the corner where she couldn’t see it, a glint of smuggled joy.

He met the glare — those dewy peach-blossom eyes doing their utterly powerless worst — and his smile only widened.

Sulli, right now, was a kitten fluffed to maximum volume. All display, zero threat, absolutely asking to be petted.

Off to the side, Hara had gone quiet and philosophical: how, exactly, had she become the surplus person? One of them shameless, the other only capable of blushing — the universe had clearly machined them as a matched set.

From the shoot, a staff member’s voice carried over.

“Hara-ssi! Break’s over — next set!”

“Ne!”

She stood and reclaimed her bottle.

“Right — back to the salt mines. You two enjoy your ‘world of two.’ I’ll go be a lightbulb somewhere else.”

She jogged back to the lawn, and the bench held its population of two again.

Neither of them said anything. Sulli, because her vocal cords had not been informed of a restart time. Song Ji-yeon, because he had finished thinking it through — and the thinking had settled the matter.

The Goddess Handbook had already bound Sulli. He had already chosen the villain’s road.

In which case, what was the point of maintaining a devoted-leading-man act?

Cards on the table, then. Face up — harem and all.

Whether she could live with what the cards showed — that part was hers to decide, and he would leave her the room to decide it.

SUNDAY · NOON Behind Ewha’s back gate A Korean restaurant at the end of the lane

By the time the shoot truly, officially, finally wrapped, the sun had climbed to the roof of the sky.

Hara strode to the folding table beside the set, bent to exchange a few words with the photographer, then turned and bowed — one by one — to every staffer drifting in with the ritual great work today, before she finally walked over to the bench smiling like a released prisoner.

“It’s dooone!”

Both arms went up. A long exhale, like a boulder being set down.

Sulli laughed, caught her hand, and towed.

“You survived, unni! Food, now, immediately — my stomach has been filing complaints for an hour.”

“Oppa, Sulli — Mianhaeyo, truly, for the wait!” She was already steering them toward the street. “There’s a Korean place near campus everyone’s been chanting about. Shall we?”

“Anything! Anywhere unnie says!”

Song Ji-yeon nodded too. His requirements for food topped out at edible. Fuel was fuel.

Hara led them off the main street and into one of the quiet lanes bordering campus. At the end of it stood a Korean restaurant with a weathered, dignified front — the kind of facade that had outlived three fads.

No private rooms in a place like that. The three of them walked to the deepest table in the back, out of the door’s sightlines.

It was 2012, and the two women with him were very famous. Masked and capped, they had still drawn glances the entire walk — a pair of magnets on legs.

Seated, Hara annexed the menu with the grandeur of a general claiming a map.

“Today is mine, and nobody is fighting me for it. Three portions of the top marbled hanu beef. One seafood pajeon. One stir-fried spicy octopus. One stone-pot doenjang stew!”

He nodded. The two of them had small appetites — though, to be fair, idol appetites as a class ran small.

“That’s easily enough. Three people might not finish it.”

Order placed, Hara set the menu down, turned to face him — and the playfulness drained into something serious.

“Oppa. Today — me begging Sulli to drag you out here — was to thank you. For taking over my stock account.”

She had opened the account the day before, and stared at the screen for a while, uncomprehending.

GOO HARA · PERSONAL TRADING ACCOUNT — AS OF SATURDAY

Principal handed over₩1,500,000,000

Current balance₩2,160,000,000

Gain — five sessions+₩660,000,000 (≈ +44%)

Advisory fee20% of profits — her own offer, on day one

Her private theory, at the start, had been simple: a smooth-talker running a long game on a peach in love, and the ₩1.5 billion a probe — a test of what the man was actually made of.

The test, run to completion, had dredged up a god of wealth from the bottom of the river.

₩660 million.

She did the math the way she always did — how many airport dawns, how many overseas schedules, how many practice-room resets with the AC broken? And the man across the table had done it in five days.

Conclusion: this man was not conning anyone. Con men do not compound. Sulli had picked up a treasure on the street.

Which was, in full, the explanation for the change in the weather today.

“This is nothing, you know — aren’t you still paying my advisory fee either way?” He shook his head, smiling. “The gain this time ran past ₩660 million. Your fee alone comes to over a hundred million won.”

Twenty percent of profits — the terms she had volunteered herself, on the very first day.

He had no actual designs on the money. He’d been running Sulli’s account for ages now, and the word fee had never once come up with her.

He mentioned it now for exactly one purpose: so that Hara understood this was a service, not a favor. Debts of gratitude that don’t exist shouldn’t be collected.

Hara shook her head right back.

“The fee is the fee — I know exactly what it buys. I’m thanking you for choosing to take me on at all.”

She was no finance novice. She understood precisely what she was looking at: a track record like this meant a waiting list of wealthy clients stretching around the block, people who would beg him. And of all those people, he had picked her account to touch.

That was the courtesy, and she knew it.

And for that, the thanks went — once more — to the girl beside her. Sulli, my little lucky charm.

She turned and beamed at her — and reached over to pinch the little peach’s cheek.

Sulli, for her part, had just completed an important realization of her own: oppa had been running her account all this time and had never once mentioned being paid for it.

She opened her mouth to interrogate him—

— and her cheek was captured mid-syllable, and all that escaped was a soft, scandalized “Mmph—”.
