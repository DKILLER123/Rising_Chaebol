Chapter Twenty

A Light in the Black Ocean

SATURDAY, SEPTEMBER 8 · 7:00 PM ICC Jeju — Main Hall third row, center — in front of the extended stage

Seven o’clock sharp. The concert opened on time.

The instant the house lights dropped, the hall detonated. Thousands of screams arriving as one — a wall of sound with weight behind it, pressing in from all four sides, hard enough that the seats themselves shivered.

And in the dark, the lights came on. Thousands of light sticks and fan lamps igniting at once — it looked like nothing so much as the Milky Way tipped over and poured into the building.

Ji-yeon sat in the middle of it, ringed in other people’s colors, eyes drifting again and again to the stage. Waiting.

The opener was a VCR and a children’s choir. Jeju rolled across the big screens — waves detonating white on black rock, fields of volcanic stone greening at the edges — scored to thin, bright children’s voices. It was genuinely nice. He gave it three courteous minutes. Children’s choirs were not, in the end, the reason a man flies to an island.

Then 2AM, and the room finally had a pulse. Four tall men at standing mics, spotlights pouring down from the rigging and sealing each of them inside a circle of white. They sang like they meant every word — voices steady, harmonies tight enough to thread a needle with.

Somewhere to his side, a woman had a hand pressed over her mouth, eyes gone glassy, humming under her breath; at the interlude she got out an “Oppa, saranghae!” with a wobble on the landing that no amount of vocal training could have prevented.

He listened, and he granted it: live vocals that could actually hold a hall of this size without a net — rarer in this industry than it should have been. But boy-group stages did nothing to him personally. The girl-group engine — youth and precision and choreography polished to a shine — was the reason he was here. So the phone came out. Thumb. Scroll. Wait.

When Nine Muses took the stage, he finally sat up.

The girls came out in studded black leather and hit the choreography like one machine with too many precision parts to count. Body management at punishing standards — every movement pinned to its beat, every formation snapping true, even the head-whips looking protractor-certified.

The screaming stacked on itself. Even the two A Pink fans to his left were waving despite themselves.

He enjoyed every second of it. When the number ended he clapped along with the house, and his eyes followed the girls all the way offstage — a beat longer than anyone around him. Old habits. The visual-fan dies hard.

Then the bill swung back to the men. EXO-K, and screams with enough lift in them to take the dome off its moorings — young men dancing with real power, floor work clean and hard. He watched a while with a neutral face. Muscle and impact. Respectable, and not remotely his aesthetic. A few polite glances; the phone again.

Then BoA — and that was a different thing entirely. The sunbae singer occupied a stage the way weather occupies a valley. One woman, alone, and the entire venue bent toward her by a degree or two, like grass deciding which way to lean.

He checked the program: a crisply printed A4, every act timed to the minute. T-ara: eighth on the bill. Around eight-thirty.

Two acts to go.

He set the program down and pulled something out of the backpack.

A light board, lemon yellow, notebook-sized, its clear acrylic face printed with the group’s name and the crown. LED beads ran all the way around the border — flick the switch and it would burn a clean, unignorable yellow. In a dark crowd, the one color you could find from a stage.

He checked the batteries. Full. He clicked the switch once — a small private sunrise on his knees — then off again, and laid the board flat across his lap.

The fans beside him noticed.

The glasses guy on his right read the board. His eyes stopped on the word T-ara and stayed there for two full seconds. Something crossed his face; his lips started a word and gave up on it; he turned back to his phone.

The two A Pink fans on his left noticed too. One elbowed the other. A look passed between them, entire sentences long. Neither said anything. Both drifted a few centimeters farther down the row.

Ji-yeon registered all of it and said nothing.

The seventh act ended.

Every light on the stage died at once.

Then, in the center of all that black, a single spotlight came down.

“Day by Day” — T-ara · ICC Jeju, Main Stage

Six figures back to back on the riser, rising out of the floor as the intro synthesizer unspools — the mechanical groan of the lift swallowed whole by the music.

The spotlight sharpens. Six silhouettes come into focus: six black shapes, like six flowers opening in the dark.

Day by day… day by day…

The hook lands, and six bodies move as one.

Eun-jung stood left of the center line. The black stage costume traced a trim line at her waist; her long hair lay loose over her shoulders and burned deep brown where the light caught it. Her profile in the spotlight was white enough to read as its own light source — the jawline clean and elegant, the shadow of her nose falling soft across her cheek, the shadow of her lashes drawn out long.

And then, all across the hall, the lights began to go out.

Patch by patch. Row by row. Like dominoes tipping in the dark — from the floor to the stands, one fandom’s glow after another snuffed out. SHINee’s pearl-aqua first. Then EXO’s silver-white. The darkness spread faster than the lights had ever gone up — a soundless plague moving through the crowd, faster than breath. In a matter of seconds, the entire hall had gone completely, totally black.

Only the stage lights stayed on. Alone.

Six girls standing in the only brightness left in the building, and around them, on every side, a darkness with no visible far shore.

This was not the simple absence of light. It was a silence — conscious, collective, and hostile.

The six on stage could see the black ocean beneath them. Of course they could. That was the point of it.

Ji-yeon was close — third row close — close enough to watch it land on every face in the formation. The choreography stayed precise. The formations stayed clean. Every count still hit to the millimeter, because their bodies had no other setting. But the faces had changed.

He looked down. His fingers found the switch at the edge of the board.

Click.

A panel of lemon-yellow light woke in his hands.

In a hall gone black on all four sides, one small yellow rectangle in the third row — the first match struck in a very long night. Faint. And impossible to miss.

Onstage, several pairs of eyes came around to it at once.

He pulled a breath all the way down to the floor of his lungs. He lifted the board off his knees. And he stood.

The lemon-yellow light went up over his head — one lone star, holding its position in a dead sky.

And then, with everything he had in his lungs, he threw T-ara’s fan chant into the dark:

“Jeon Bo-ram! Lee Qri! Park So-yeon! Ham Eun-jung! Park Hyo-min! Park Ji-yeon! T-ara, GO! Let’s go together, T-ara! Chameleons of the music world! Day by Day, T-ara! T-ara, GO!”

In a hall gone eerily quiet, his voice carried absurdly, painfully far — clean across the floor, up into the stands. Heads turned. Brows knotted. Eyes rolled ceilingward. Phones came up all around him and pointed at him like periscopes surfacing.

A fine old Peninsula tradition, that — the herd’s hereditary will to power, handed down like an heirloom. Ji-yeon couldn’t have cared less.

He carried the chant to its final word with no backup, no pause, all six names in the right order — and then he started swinging the board to the beat, the yellow light drawing arcs through the dark, over and over and over.

Onstage, Ham Eun-jung had gone still for half a heartbeat — because from up there, it was impossible to miss. In all of that black, one point of yellow, burning.

Her gaze pushed through the glare of the stage lights, followed the yellow down — and found the figure underneath it. Short black hair. A clean, no-nonsense profile. The arm holding the board aloft, taut and trembling faintly with the effort of staying up.

Song Ji-yeon.

Her nose stung, sharp and sudden. Half a beat leaked out of a turn before she caught it and jammed it back in; she clamped her lower lip between her teeth and shoved the tears down to wherever they had come from.

She made herself look away from the little yellow point and finish the choreography. But the sourness and the warmth were rising together now, wave after wave, and together they very nearly went over her head.

His arm had been aching for two songs. He didn’t lower it. He held it up and kept it moving — through the bridge, through the last chorus, all the way to the final note of “Sexy Love” —

“Sexy Love” — T-ara

Sexy love… sexy love…

final stage of the set · ICC Jeju, main stage

— when it fell, and six girls locked into the ending pose, chests heaving like they had just run a mile in sand.

Down in the dark, the silence held. And in row three, one yellow light held on.

In the last second before they left the stage, Eun-jung turned her face toward the darkness — toward that lamp — and let her mouth curve. A very small smile. So small that, in all likelihood, nobody else in the world saw it.

Song Ji-yeon saw it.

He brought the arm down — it practically declined to bend — clicked the board off, laid it back across his knees, and let out a long, long breath.

“Whew… okay. Done.”

The fans around him were still staring, and had stopped pretending otherwise. One of the girls whispered something to her friend in Korean — too fast, or too quiet; either way, he couldn’t be bothered to run it down.

He knew perfectly well that by tomorrow he would probably be a thread on some forum somewhere — title along the lines of T-ara Die-Hard Spotted Holding It Down Alone in the Black Ocean. Fine. Let them. For an heir with his intentions — chaebol, the real thing — a fleck of notoriety was road under his feet. Passing clouds. Nothing he would need to carry.

He slid the board into the backpack, ran the zipper all the way home, shouldered it, and squeezed out along the row through a gauntlet of frankly incredulous stares.

Tonight had only ever been about one thing: standing behind Eun-jung. Making sure six girls on that stage knew the fans had not all gone — that somebody’s light was still on for them. A little warmth in the chest. Enough, maybe, to keep six people from sliding one by one into the same dark they had fallen into in his last life.

Mission accomplished — and he had precisely zero desire to stand crushed among ten thousand strangers, watching idols who weren’t T-ara sing and dance.
