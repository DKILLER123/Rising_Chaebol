Chapter Two

The Goddess Handbook

SEPTEMBER 2012 Apgujeong-dong, Seoul the corner off PIEN PLUS

At the corner not far from PIEN PLUS — one of those mixed-use towers, shops below, apartments above — Eun-jung bowed her apology. Again.

Ji-yeon was running low on grace. What was it with this corner of the world and the apology bow? Koreans had refined it into an art form, the Japanese had turned it into a national sport, and he was starting to feel like a hostage in an etiquette exchange program.

“Really, it’s nothing. Look — not a scratch on me.”

He raised both hands and wiggled his fingers like a doctor presenting a clean X-ray. Then he noticed the bag in her grip sagging under its own weight, and course-corrected fast — if he let her stay on this topic, she’d be bowing until sunrise.

“Anyway — it’s late, and you’re carrying all of that by yourself. Want me to walk you partway? Don’t get any ideas. Just to your building, that’s all.”

Okay. Full disclosure: his motives were about ninety percent gentleman. The other ten percent knew exactly what it was doing.

Eun-jung studied him for a few seconds, visibly weighing something. Then she shook her head.

“It’s all right. I live right around here — two minutes, really.”

She’d said no. He didn’t push it. Bros have pride too, okay.

“Then I’ll leave you to it.”

They said their goodbyes, and he set off down the street toward ONGI — only to hear footsteps trailing him the entire way.

He stopped. Turned around.

“…Why are you following me? I told you it’s fine. You really don’t have to feel bad.”

“Um… I live… upstairs from your hotpot restaurant…”

Her ears had gone pink. She had just turned down his offer to walk her home — and now, apparently, they were walking the same direction anyway. Mortifying. Possibly fatal.

Song Ji-yeon experienced a long, internal silence.

Fate really decided you two weren’t done for the night, huh.

He reached over and confiscated the plastic bag before she could object. Message received: same road anyway — I’ll carry it.

“Oh— um… thank you.”

The bag had changed hands so fast it startled her. The thanks came out at half volume.

They walked the rest of the way without a word — an oddly comfortable silence, considering how they’d met. In front of the restaurant, Ji-yeon was about to march straight past it on pure momentum when her voice caught him, small and hesitant.

“This is me. I take the elevator up here.”

He followed her finger. Along the side of the building: a discreet elevator lobby. He wasn’t about to turn it into a ceremony. He handed the bag back and nodded.

“Right. Then I won’t walk you up.”

Color flooded her cheeks — and somewhere behind it, something in her chest quietly unclenched. So he really was just… a decent guy. A guy she had accused of stalking, tackled onto a public street, and who had repaid her by carrying her snacks like a human bellhop.

Guilt — freshly whipped — rose right up. She took the bag from his hands and made herself ask.

“Ji-yeon-ssi… thank you for carrying that. Could I— may I buy you a meal? As a proper apology.”

“Huh?”

“Is that… not okay?”

“No — it’s fine. More than fine.”

He actually thought it over. In favor: one, he genuinely liked Eun-jung — and this was a superstar, a certified beauty. Put a face like that across the table and he could down two extra bowls of rice without noticing. Two, he wanted an honest civilian’s read on ONGI before opening week.

“Tell you what. The restaurant opens in a few days. If you really want to make it up to me — come treat me to hotpot at my own store. And while you’re at it, tell me everything that’s wrong with it.”

“Oh… then, when does it open?”

She had apparently been planning to drag him out for hanwoo barbecue tomorrow. But if the man had a schedule, the man’s schedule won.

“Sometime this week, I think. Don’t ask me the exact day — I’m the president, not the calendar.”

“Ah! Then — Kakao? So we can coordinate!”

They traded IDs. He waved, grinning.

“Deal. See you at opening, Eun-jung-ssi.”

“Ne.”

She nodded, her shoulder-length bob swaying with the motion, and stepped into the elevator. Just as the doors slid shut, she set the bag down between her feet, smiled, and waved one last time through the narrowing gap.

“Bye-bye, Ji-yeon-ssi!”

The doors closed on the smile.

Ji-yeon stood there for a moment, brain buffering.

“That was… dangerously sweet. That’s a foul. Someone check the rulebook.”

He hadn’t even finished the sentence when a small card materialized in the air in front of him — gold-edged, floating, impossible. On it, rendered in crisp, dashing detail — sharp-eyed, cool, faintly knightly — Ham Eun-jung.

Goddess Handbook — Opened

GoddessHam Eun-jung (T-ara)

Activation RewardSamsung Electronics — 2012 price chart

Goddess AbilityOne Stock Forecast Card per week — collect in person, through contact with Ham Eun-jung. Pending 1/1

Goddess Bond — “Jewel of the Crown”: gather all six members of the Crown.

3/6 — KakaoTalk equity 10% · 4/6 — 15% · 5/6 — 20% · 6/6 — 30%

“…Hold on. Is this real? I actually have a cheat?”

He eyed the little card — invisible to everyone but him, hovering smugly at eye level — and sniffed. Please. Rebirth was already the road to invincibility. Who bolts a jet engine onto a rocket?

Future knowledge in his head, a modest pile of seed money in the bank. He was going to thrive in this life with or without divine intervention.

And the much-hyped “Goddess Ability”? One Stock Forecast Card a week. One. It wasn’t as if he’d forgotten which companies were headed for the Fortune 500.

Still sneering, he skimmed the bond rewards.

KakaoTalk equity — ten percent.

KakaoTalk. Korea’s WeChat. The app an entire country ran its life on.

“Grandpa Handbook — sir — about that thing I said a minute ago. I got loud. I got arrogant. Write it off. Treat it like a fart and let it drift away on the wind. Please and thank you.”

He dropped to his knees right there on the sidewalk. Full dogeza energy. Forgive him — forgive his youth, his arrogance, his tragically inflated self-esteem.

So it wasn’t just Koreans and Japanese who knelt at the drop of a hat. Add one Korean-American to the list.

Lesson learned: don’t get cocky. A little humility is good for the organs.

KakaoTalk equity. A monster headed for a ten-billion-dollar valuation — and even completing the full six-piece set only earned thirty percent. Thirty percent was still enough to make a grown man hear colors.

Sure, with future knowledge he could guarantee himself a rich life. But wealth at that scale, the old-fashioned way? That was a decade of grinding, minimum.

The Handbook was offering it for a six-girl collection. Who wouldn’t be tempted?

Look into his eyes. Go on — look. Then tell him who could refuse.

Although—

How, exactly, did this thing decide to activate a card? Eun-jung’s had simply… popped open on its own. No manual. No tutorial. Not even a terms-of-service agreement. And “collect through contact” — was that even serious? He’d had physical contact with her tonight, plural instances, including one felony-grade takedown, and the Pending 1/1 button refused to press no matter how hard he poked it with his mind.

Clearly, more field research was required.

He rubbed the arm that still ached from the arrest, filed the entire subject under Homework, and walked into the restaurant.

SAME NIGHT ONGI Hotpot, Apgujeong final walkthrough

Gangnam’s ONGI flagship sat here in Apgujeong, and its manager was a thirty-five-year-old company man named Lim Woo-hyun — a SAVORY veteran with more than a decade in, promoted to flagship manager the moment Ji-yeon restructured the operation.

“Sajangnim — welcome.”

Lim spotted him from across the floor, recognized the company’s brand-new young president, tugged his jacket straight, and double-timed over.

“How’s the renovation holding up?”

A flick of the hand — skip the ceremony. On the clock, Ji-yeon was all business; small talk had a waiting list and would not be seen today.

Lim scratched his head and fell in behind him, briefing on the move.

“Essentially complete. Front of house plus kitchen, three hundred and fifty square meters altogether. Twenty-three booths across the open floor and five private rooms — every seat a U-shaped counter, exactly as ordered.”

Ji-yeon did a slow lap of the floor, nodding. The layout lined up with the service-first hotpot palaces he’d studied to death — not identical, but close enough to pass a paternity test. He toured the kitchen next, found no obvious sins of construction, left Lim with a few crisp instructions, and drove home.
