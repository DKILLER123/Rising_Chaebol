Chapter Ninety-Three

Poaching the Front Desk

MORNING · 8:30 A.M. Song Ji-yeon’s apartment — 1F, Cheongdam Daewoo the morning after

When Eun-jung opened her eyes the next morning, it took a few seconds of blinking before the room settled into focus. She turned her head on the pillow and found Song Ji-yeon’s sleeping face an inch away — and a sweetness with no name rose up in her chest and sat there, fat and warm.

Right behind the sweetness came the guilt.

Ji-yeon is asleep one floor up. If the little dragon finds out I spent the night at oppa’s—

She didn’t dare finish the thought.

They were both single, both adults, both unattached. There was nothing technically illegal about any of it. But “technically nothing illegal” and “easy to explain in front of the girl who signed a contract at your dinner table last night” were two very different scales of difficulty. And from tomorrow on — or, more precisely, from the moment Kim Kwang-soo’s signature dried on the acquisition — she and the other five would be not just his dinner guests but his artists. The last thing she wanted was for any of them to think the new boss had bought T-ara because he was sleeping with its leader.

She had to get out before Ji-yeon noticed. She had to get home without leaving a single piece of evidence upstairs.

Eun-jung peeled the blanket back, half an inch at a time, with the care of a woman defusing an ordnance. Bare feet found the floor — cool, but tolerable. She stood, took stock.

Song Ji-yeon, last night, had not been precious about where things landed.

Her dress was draped over the back of the chair. Her top had made it as far as the side of the bed and given up. Her underwear was—

Heat climbed her face. She bent, fished it out from under the foot of the bed, and stepped into it in record time, hopping on one foot, then the other.

She was mid-zip on the side of her dress, fingers shaking, when the lazy voice came from behind her, smiling all the way through.

“Leaving this early?”

Her hands stalled. She turned her head.

He was already awake. Propped on one elbow, the sheet pooled at his waist, the line of his shoulder catching the early light through the curtain gap. Watching her with a smile that wasn’t quite a smile.

Does this woman run from my apartment every time she wakes up in it? Last time, no goodbye. This time, no goodbye. At this rate I should install a turnstile.

“I— I have to leave before Ji-yeon wakes up. Running into her would be… impossible to explain.”

Eun-jung kept her voice low, the pitch of a burglar who has been caught and is now trying to negotiate.

She knew it wasn’t ideal. She also knew the dragon was exactly the kind of person who would notice.

“Explain what?” He sat up, the sheet sliding to his waist, the plane of his chest unbothered by morning. “Just say you slept here. What’s the catastrophe.”

“No!”

The word came out too fast, too loud. She winced at herself, crossed back to the bed in two quick steps, and knelt at the edge of the mattress so she could look him in the eye properly.

“Oppa. About us. I don’t want the members to know. Not yet.”

He lifted one eyebrow.

“Why?”

She dropped her gaze. Her fingers found the hem of her skirt and worried at the edge of it without asking permission.

“Because they’ll think too much. We’re about to sign. You’re about to be our Sajangnim. Right at this hinge of the whole thing — I don’t want any of them believing you bought T-ara because of me.”

She looked up. There was a request in her eyes, small and quiet and absolutely serious.

“Oppa. Can you understand that?”

He looked back at her — at the clear brown of those eyes — for two full seconds. Then his mouth softened.

He reached out and ruffled her hair, easy and unhurried.

“All right. Your call. You go public whenever you want to go public. I’ll wait.”

The breath left her in one long quiet exhale. A smile came up — small, grateful, a little wobbly at the corners.

“Thank you, oppa.”

She stood, turned toward the door, and her wrist was caught.

She looked back.

He had tipped his head up off the pillow. The look in his eyes had an edge of amusement under the warmth.

“You’re just going? Not even a good-morning kiss?”

Her face went scarlet from the throat up.

She did not, however, refuse.

She bent — quick, light, the kiss barely more than a press of her mouth against his — and straightened back up before he could turn it into anything else.

“There. Satisfied?” she asked, ears burning.

He ran his tongue once, slowly, over his lower lip, and only then let her wrist go.

While she gathered the last of yesterday’s clothes off the floor and folded them for the briefcase she did not have, he reached over to the nightstand and picked up his phone. Eight-thirty. By Ji-yeon’s constitutional habits, the dragon would not surface until noon at the earliest.

When she first moved in upstairs I thought she was a disciplined one. Morning jog, regular sleep, the works. Then I bought her a computer. Now she’s a creature of the night. Question for later: was buying her the PC a net positive or net negative for the Peninsula. Verdict pending.

“Eun-jung-ah.”

She turned at the bedroom door.

“Ji-yeon’s not up yet. You’re not going to bump into her — and anyway, I’m heading to the company this morning. Let me drop you off. Wait for me to wash up. We’ll go together.”

He had, today, the small matter of delivering the six signed consent forms to Kim Min-hyun.

Eun-jung thought it through. The oppa had a point. Sneaking out solo, heart hammering at every footstep on the stairs, vs. riding down in his car, clean and fast and over in three minutes. The math was not difficult.

“Okay.”

He swung out of bed, hit the bathroom, washed his face, brushed his teeth, and reappeared in a pressed white shirt that made him look like a different man from the one who’d been laughing into her hair an hour ago — sharper, more composed, ready to sign things. He crossed back to the nightstand, picked up yesterday’s briefcase — still where he’d tossed it — tucked it under his arm, and looked back at her with a half-grin.

“Let’s go.”

Elevator down. Underground parking. Into the car.

Not a single familiar face crossed their path — not the dragon, not a neighbor, not even the guard at the gate. Eun-jung’s morning-long tightrope walk finally ended. Her shoulders dropped a full inch.

Of course she was worried for nothing. Ji-yeon is a different species of mammal before noon. Yesterday I went up at ten-thirty and she was still face-down in the pillow. After last night’s drinking, she’ll surface around lunch. Maybe.

The engine caught. The Mercedes nosed out of the garage and turned toward Apgujeong-ro.

Eight-forty was the wrong end of rush hour. By ten-to-nine the road had packed itself solid and stopped trying.

Eun-jung, in the passenger seat, watched the crawl of red taillights ahead and felt the small discomfort of being the reason a man was going to be late for his own office.

“Oppa… let me out at the next intersection. I’ll catch a cab from there. If we sit in this any longer, you’re going to be late.”

He glanced at her, amused.

“Late. So what? It’s my company. Who’s going to dock my pay?”

A laugh broke out of her, half-shocked.

She considered the logic. The logic held.

“That’s true,” she murmured, “but it still looks bad.”

“It won’t.” He turned his head briefly toward her. “And besides — getting you home is worth being a little late for.”

Not that I have anything urgent at the office today anyway.

It was said in the same offhand register as everything else, but it landed in her chest in a place she hadn’t been braced for. She let the sweet weight of it settle, and stopped arguing.

She sat quiet in the passenger seat and watched the city slide past — always the same streets, always rearranged slightly, always there.

Neither of them spoke for a while. The silence wasn’t awkward. It was the silence of two people who had spent the night together and didn’t need to fill the morning with proof.

Eventually, he spoke first.

“After yesterday’s signing — were you nervous?”

She turned her head and watched the side of his face as he drove.

“A little,” she said, honestly. “It still feels like a dream. All of it — too fast.”

From the first time he’d said I’m going to buy T-ara, to the moment last night when all six of them had set pen to paper on the transfer consent, only a month had passed. One month. The whole thing had taken a month.

“It’s not a dream.” He smiled without taking his eyes off the road. “From now on, you’re all ShiningYouth’s. I’ll be the wall at your back.”

He wasn’t joking. T-ara had been the great what-could-have-been of his first life — the grief that had dragged him back across a decade. He had crossed the years for them. He would walk them up to the top of the Peninsula if he had to, and he would absolutely not do it the way Kim Kwang-soo had — strip-mining their value for his own balance sheet and calling it management.

“Mm.” She nodded. The ground under her feet felt a little more solid.

She hesitated. Then she asked the question that had been sitting on the bottom of her chest since the dinner.

“Oppa. The company side. Kim Kwang-soo-Sajangnim… will he really let us go? Smoothly?”

“He will.” His voice was matter-of-fact. “He can’t wait to get you off his books. I gave him a number he couldn’t refuse — there was no reason for him to refuse it. And now that you’ve all six signed the consent forms, there’s no contract clause left for him to lean on, either. He’ll take the money and walk. Cleaner that way.”

The confidence in his voice was the kind you couldn’t fake. The last cold knot inside her quietly dissolved.

“Good,” she breathed. “Then — what do we do next?”

“Wait.”

“Wait?”

“My legal team finishes the formal handover of your contracts from CCM. Fast — a couple of days. Slow — a week or two. During that window, you do nothing. Rest. Reset. Get your heads on straight.”

He shifted lanes around a bus.

“Once the contracts are across, I’ll sit you down with the producer and the management team — work out what comes next. China isn’t a sentence you say. There’s album prep, stage prep, language study. A whole year of foundations to pour. None of it happens overnight.”

ShiningYouth Entertainment, as a company, currently owned a name, a registration number, and a borrowed corner of SAVORY’s office. None of that stopped him from making the promise.

Listening to him lay it out — step by step, calmly, the way you’d lay out a meal on a table — the future, which had been a fog for half a year, slowly turned into a road. The stages she’d stopped believing in came back within reach.

“Oppa — thank you.” It came out of her whole and unguarded.

“For what?” He laughed, soft. “I told you. I’ll be responsible for you. All of you. Your job is just to trust me, and then do the work in front of you.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Stop-and-go, stop-and-go. Eventually the road loosened up. Ten minutes later the familiar apartment tower hove into view.

He brought the car to a clean stop at the foot of her building.

“Here we are.”

“Mm.” She unclipped the belt but didn’t move.

She turned to face him.

“Oppa. Don’t wear yourself out. Drive carefully.”

“Got it. Go on up.” He reached over and tapped the bridge of her nose with one finger, lightly.

Her face went warm. She opened the door, got out, and turned back to wave through the window.

“Going up. Hurry to the office.”

“Mm-hmm.”

He didn’t restart the engine. He sat and watched her turn, walk through the lobby doors, vanish into the stairwell — and only when she was gone did he press the gas and turn the wheel toward Mapo.

MORNING · 9:30 A.M. SAVORY F&B HQ, Mapo top floor — legal department

Half an hour later, the Mercedes nosed into the SAVORY underground lot.

He collected the briefcase off the passenger seat, greeted Lee Hye-jin at the front desk with a nod, and took the elevator straight to the top floor.

He did not go to his own office first. He went straight to the legal department.

The legal department was small — four people in total, including the head, Kim Min-hyun — and the office matched: a single room, no partitions, no private office for the director. Kim sat in the middle of his colleagues, head down in a stack of documents, when Song Ji-yeon knocked on the open door.

“Minister Kim.”

Kim looked up. Registered who it was. Stood immediately.

“Sajangnim. You’re in.”

“Mm.” He crossed the office, set the briefcase on Kim’s desk, and popped the clasps. From inside, six clear-sleeved documents. Each one signed at the bottom in careful, individual hands, each one stamped with a red thumbprint. He laid them out in a row.

“The six T-ara transfer consents. Take them.”

Kim took the documents. Looked down at the row of signatures, the row of red marks. A flicker of surprise crossed his face, briefly.

Yesterday’s brief, today’s paperwork. The Sajangnim doesn’t waste a day.

“Understood, Sajangnim.”

“Reach out to Kim Kwang-soo’s side today. Get a meeting on the calendar. Inside this week — I want the formal acquisition agreement inked. No extensions.”

“Yes, Sajangnim. I’ll contact them today.” Kim bowed his head.

“Good. Thank you.”

He left the legal department, walked the few paces back to his own office, shrugged out of his suit jacket and dropped it across the back of his chair, and slid into the wide leather seat behind the desk.

First order of business. He opened the laptop.

The brokerage software loaded. He typed in the ticker.

POSITION · SAMJIN GROUP (005630) · LIVE
Today’s move+8.0% and climbing
5-session projection (Handbook)Eun-jung’s ₩500M → ₩200M profit
Sajangnim’s principal₩10B+ → ~₩4.5B projected
StatusHolding · Stock Forecast Card, fully leveraged

He leaned back in the chair and watched the number for a while, in no hurry. The morning’s mood was the kind that didn’t need company.

He was just about to close the window and start the day’s actual paperwork when a low ache surfaced in his lower back.

Last night was, perhaps, on the indulgent side. The 2.5× constitution is genuinely something — apparently to the point where I forget to dial it back. Recovery day. No exceptions.

He frowned, pushed back from the desk, and stood. The break room would solve two problems at once: coffee, and a stretch.

✦ ✦ ✦

SAVORY’s break room was generous — fitted out like a small café, with a long wooden bar, a row of machines, and the kind of lighting that flattered everyone. It was the unofficial headquarters of the staff’s social life.

When he pushed the door open, the chatter inside cut off mid-laugh.

Lee Hye-jin was at the bar with a new face — younger, early twenties, holding her coffee cup with both hands the way a person holds a hot drink when they’re nervous. They’d been leaning toward each other, eyes bright, in the middle of what had clearly been a very good conversation.

Lee Hye-jin straightened the moment she saw him. The grin banked itself.

“Sajangnim!”

The girl beside her startled so hard her coffee sloshed. She bowed, fast, twice.

“Sajangnim, hello!”

“What were you two so happy about?” He crossed to the machine and started hunting for his usual beans, the question tossed over his shoulder, easy.

“Nothing — nothing,” Lee Hye-jin said, eyes rolling sideways toward a quick save. “Just telling the new girl some company stories.”

She elbowed the new girl.

“Sajangnim, this is our new front desk. Jeong Soo-ah.”

Song Ji-yeon glanced back. Early twenties, soft cheeks still carrying their last bit of childish roundness, the look of a person who had not yet learned how to be in the same room as management. The moment his eyes landed on her, her face went red from the chin up.

“Hello.” He smiled at her.

“S-Sajangnim, hello.” The voice was barely audible.

“Don’t be so nervous. I don’t bite.”

Lee Hye-jin, recovered, rode to her new colleague’s rescue.

“Right? Our Sajangnim’s the best. Zero airs. Right, Sajangnim?”

“You’re the one with too many words.” He shot her a look, slid a fresh Americano under the machine spout, and let it pour.

Lee Hye-jin was not afraid of him by any stretch. She leaned a hip against the bar and lowered her voice into the confidential register she used when she was about to be indiscreet.

“Sajangnim, you’re in a good mood today. Something happen?”

He took a slow sip. Raised an eyebrow at her over the rim.

“When am I not in a good mood?”

“This is different.” She held up a forefinger, the way a professor does when introducing the central thesis. “You walked through that lobby this morning and the corners of your mouth have not come down once. By my years of front-desk observation, sir, this is the signature of major good news.”

He laughed, half into his cup.

Is it that obvious? Well. About to close on T-ara. That’s not nothing.

“You’re wasted not being a detective, Hye-jin-ah.”

“I know,” Lee Hye-jin said, chin lifting in mock dignity. Then she turned to the new girl, as if remembering.

“Right. Soo-ah — yesterday’s thing. What did you decide?”

Soo-ah’s eyes lit like someone had flipped a switch.

“Unnie means the Girls’ Generation fanmeet next month? In Seoul?”

“That one!” Lee Hye-jin slapped her own thigh. Her eyes were actually shining. “First fanmeet since the third-album comeback. Taeyeon-unnie is going to sing the new songs. I watched the teaser — that high note. I am telling you. That high note.”

“I saw it too!” Soo-ah’s pitch had climbed a full octave. “I watched the fancam online — Yoona-unnie’s ending fairy at the end. Insane. I want to see it live. I need to see it live.”

“Right? Right?” Lee Hye-jin nodded at a frequency that suggested she was about to take off. “I want to go too. But the price — you know what the front-row tickets are going for? Three hundred and fifty thousand won. Salary hasn’t landed yet. My card is weeping.”

“Me too…” Soo-ah sighed and started counting on her fingers. “If I bought mid-tier and ate nothing but ramyeon for three months, I could maybe, maybe afford it.”

“Three months? The fanmeet’s over by then!”

“I know, I know.” Soo-ah’s face folded into a small tragedy. “Unnie. When are we ever going to be like those managers? See whoever they want, no ticket, walk straight into the backstage, watch the live every single day?”

“Dream on. Those managers are working.” Lee Hye-jin laughed and gave her a smack on the arm.

Song Ji-yeon, coffee in hand, hadn’t meant to eavesdrop. But the word manager had walked across his ear and pulled a thread.

Manager.

ShiningYouth Entertainment, today, has a registration number and nobody. The T-ara contracts come across in a week — six girls, schedules to run, music-show stages to staff. They’ll need handlers. They’ll need assistants. The org chart is empty.

These two aren’t pros. But — years at a front desk means they can read a room, talk a stranger down, keep a calendar, fix a problem. And fangirls — the fangirl’s knowledge of the industry is not a thing you can hire for money. You can only hire it for love.

He took a slow sip, looking at them over the rim of the cup.

“You two… like Girls’ Generation?”

Both heads snapped toward him, surprised to be addressed.

“Uh — yes, yes,” Lee Hye-jin said, embarrassed, nodding. “Is Sajangnim interested in that too?”

“Not interested.” He smiled. “But I was wondering — would you two be interested in changing jobs?”

“Changing jobs?” They looked at each other.

“Mm.” He set the coffee down on the bar, leaned a hip against it, and looked at them. “I just founded an entertainment company. ShiningYouth Entertainment. Planning to sign a girl group.”

“Which girl group?” Lee Hye-jin’s curiosity had risen off the chart.

“T-ara.”

“Ah?!”

Both girls’ voices left them at the same instant.

Lee Hye-jin’s eyes were the size of small plates. She did not appear to believe her own ears.

“Sajangnim — you mean that T-ara? The ‘Roly-Poly’ T-ara? Jeon Bo-ram, Lee Qri, Park So-yeon, Ham Eun-jung, Park Hyo-min, Park Ji-yeon — that T-ara?!”

“You know the members by heart.”

“Of course I do!” Lee Hye-jin was red to the ears. “Everybody knows T-ara! Hit machine! Even with the — even with everything, the talent is real.”

Soo-ah had found her voice again.

“I love Eun-jung-unnie! Her stage presence is unreal! Sajangnim — you signed T-ara?!”

“Still in process. Should be wrapped inside the week.” He watched the two of them vibrating in place, and the idea sat a little more firmly in his head. “So I was wondering if the two of you would be interested in coming to the new company. As managers.”

“Man… managers?” Lee Hye-jin’s jaw hung open.

“Mm-hmm.” He nodded. “T-ara’s managers. Running their schedules with them. Music-show stages. No ticket to buy. Straight into the backstage. See whoever you want, however long you want. And the salary — higher than front desk. By a comfortable margin.”

Both girls had gone fully, perfectly blank.

They looked at each other. Then they turned, in unison, to look at him. Their eyes were throwing off actual sparks.

“Sajangnim — you’re serious?!” Lee Hye-jin’s voice was shaking.

“What reason would I have to lie to you?”

“I— I’m in!” Lee Hye-jin’s hand shot up like a student in a classroom. “I’m in, I can do it. I’ll learn anything. Sharp tongue, smooth operator, can take a hit and give one back.”

“Me too!” Soo-ah was scarlet to the collarbones, both fists clenched at her sides. “Sajangnim — can I really? I’m new, but I learn fast. I can do photo editing too!”

“Photo editing.” He laughed. “Useful skill. Noted.”

The two girls were about three seconds from bouncing off the floor and wanted to sign on the dotted line on the spot.

“Sajangnim — when do we start? Tomorrow? No — this afternoon!”

“Not urgent.” He held up a hand. The excitement in the room dropped a half-inch. “The new company is still in setup. No office yet. You’d come in and have nowhere to sit.”

The excitement on their faces froze for a beat.

“So — for now, keep working here at SAVORY.” He picked up the coffee and took an unhurried sip. “The moment ShiningYouth has a roof over its head, I’ll process the onboarding personally. In the meantime — don’t sit idle. Start learning what a manager actually does. Prep ahead.”

Lee Hye-jin and Soo-ah traded a glance. The slight disappointment of not today was, on inspection, a small price to pay against the rest of it — running schedules with T-ara, music shows, backstage access, the whole ridiculous fantasy. And once they were inside the industry, getting close to Girls’ Generation, asking for autographs, the whole lot of it — how were they not supposed to be undone by that?

“Understood, Sajangnim!” Lee Hye-jin nodded with the force of a salute. “We’ll do you proud. Won’t embarrass you.”

“Me too!” Soo-ah echoed.

“All right. Finish your coffee. Get back to the desk.” He lifted his cup, turned, and walked toward the door.

“Sajangnim, take care!” They said it together.

✦ ✦ ✦

The moment his back disappeared through the break-room doorway, Lee Hye-jin broke.

She seized Soo-ah by both arms, lowered her voice to a whisper that was somehow louder than her normal speaking voice, and — in a single exhale — delivered the entirety of the news.

“Ahhhhhh! Soo-ah, did you hear that?! T-ara! We’re going to be T-ara’s managers!”

“I heard, I heard, I heard — unnie, please stop shaking me—” Soo-ah was rattling back and forth in Lee Hye-jin’s grip, but her own face was one wide smile. “God. I’m going to be a manager. My parents are never going to believe this.”

“Forget your parents — I don’t believe this.” Lee Hye-jin pressed a hand flat to her own chest and took one enormous breath. “That’s it. Decision made. Tonight. Every T-ara song, start to finish, in order. The full discography.”

“Me too.”
