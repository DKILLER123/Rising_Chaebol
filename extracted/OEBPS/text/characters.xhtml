The Man Who Came Back

Twenty-two years old by the calendar, thirty-something by memory. In his first life Song Ji-yeon was a Los Angeles restaurant family’s son — bussing tables in Koreatown before he could see over the counter, grinding ranked games in college with T-ara on repeat, watching from across the Pacific as the idols he loved were broken one by one by rumor, scandal, and silence. He died in the 2020s and woke in September 2012, one month before the storm: the T-ara bullying scandal, the Black Ocean, the fall of the Crown.

Armed with a decade of hindsight, a handbook that turns devotion into dividends, and the entire Song family fortune parked in Samsung Electronics, he set out to do the one thing a fan never gets to do — change the ending. Seven ONGI hotpot houses first. Then the entertainment company. Then the girls’ contracts themselves, bought outright from the old fox of CCM.

He cooks Sichuan food learned from the San Gabriel Valley, quotes American tipping culture as a moral philosophy, and holds exactly one sacred rule drilled into him by twenty-two years of waiting tables: the guest never lifts a finger.

The Female Lead

They met the worst possible way — she had him face-down on the pavement outside a convenience store, convinced he was a stalker, one hand on his wrist and the other holding a six-pack she was fully prepared to use as a blunt instrument. She had just been egged by a mob. Two days later she was apologizing in a ninety-degree bow; a week after that she was leaving convenience-store beer on his doorstep; and by the night of the Black Ocean, when one man’s light-stick refused to go dark in a stadium of silence, she understood exactly who had been holding it.

Soft-spoken, self-deprecating to a fault, trained in taekwondo and allergic to being a burden. The Handbook bound to her through her most fragile hour — and the Crown’s fate, Kakao’s equity, and one very real heart all became the same project. Under the Gwacheon stars (Ch. 103), the man who never explains anything explained himself — you don’t fall off a summit twice; not while I’m holding the rope — and she closed the last half-inch of distance between their shoulders without asking anyone’s permission.

The weekend the company found its feet, she walked all four floors of the new Cheongdam-dong headquarters and delivered her verdict on the spot: far better than anything we had at CCM (Ch. 104). The representative director overheard it, filed it, and will never once forget who he heard it from. The lease went upstairs to the fourth floor that same afternoon — ₩500 million deposit, signed without a blink — and ShiningYouth had its home before dinner.

The Peach

A girl raised by an entertainment industry that admired her face and overlooked everything else. Her earnings pass through her mother’s hands and never come back; her dinner is often a convenience-store bun eaten in a van; her smile is genuine anyway, deployed at maximum wattage, armor disguised as sunshine. She met Song Ji-yeon at a brokerage counter with a hundred million won and no idea what a stock was, followed his tip on pure gut instinct, and appointed herself his unofficial little sister on the spot. The little-sister arrangement held for exactly as long as little-sister arrangements do — until a Thursday-night finale, a curbside goodbye, and a kiss launched before her courage could talk her out of it. The peach, as the record will eventually show, moved first.

In another life, the world repaid that sunshine with cruelty until it went out. In this one, someone recognized the ending from a decade away and decided — quietly, without ever telling her why — that it would not be allowed to happen.

And on a Sunday bench at Ewha (Ch. 105), between one photo shoot and its next breath, she finally handed over the quiet part: she wants to act — properly, from the ground up, at a real university — and asked, holding it out like glass, whether it was too late. It wasn’t. The following morning (Ch. 106), when a certain mermaid teased the two of them about their “world of two,” the man who never explains anything declined to explain again — You knew. And you still came over to interrupt — and detonated her entire operating system. The blush went past red and kept going. Somewhere underneath it, a sweet, panicked, unnamed thing she refuses to look at directly took notes.

The Little Dragon

T-ara’s maknae, and the only person alive who responded to a Black Ocean by demanding to know who bought the only lit light-stick in the building. She leapt into Song Ji-yeon’s orbit calling him “oppa” within minutes of introduction, appointed herself the Ji-yeon-and-Eun-jung romance’s self-declared wingman (in flagrant violation of everyone’s privacy), and eventually rented the apartment directly upstairs so the campaign could continue at close range.

A rabid gamer, a shameless freeloader, an unexpectedly sharp schemer — and, like most children raised in practice rooms, a girl who doesn’t quite know what to do when someone feeds her a home-cooked meal and refuses to take money for it. Her Handbook binding arrived by accident, during a fit of giggling. Of course it did.

As of the company’s first week she holds three additional posts, self-appointed: chief of protective custody (one Han So-hee, towel-related incident, Chapter 111), director of competitor surveillance (the same, around the clock, with prejudice), and sole voting member of the committee that ruled — motion carried, zero abstentions — that oppa belongs to T-ara (Ch. 112).

The Queen

Introduced first as a voice on a telephone — terse, polite, apologizing for the inconvenience of being asked to negotiate her own lease, then hanging up first. The voice belonged to a drama-set rookie with a building to her name and a wariness of smooth-talking chaebol heirs that bordered on the professional. It took a collapsing light stand and a stranger’s body between her and the stage floor to change her mind about him — the Handbook bound the moment her heart did.

Wears dry wit like a coat, reads contracts for fun, and quietly keeps every receipt of everyone she has ever trusted. Then, after the banquet, she vanished — no texts, no posts, a museum exhibit of a contact — because maintaining a relationship costs energy, and her constitution refuses the expense. The universe handled re-introduction itself: a snack run in slippers and zero makeup, timed precisely to the afternoon he came house-hunting at her own tower. She has since acquired an official excuse for company — a rookie on the twelfth floor who must be looked after, by decree of a Sajangnim with a Healing Card on her file and a long game in his briefcase (Ch. 113).

The Little Mermaid

She arrived investigating a rumor — that a certain hotpot CEO was either a genius or a con man, and her darling Sulli couldn’t tell the difference. She left the interview having wired him her entire liquid fortune, ₩1.5 billion, with the calm of a woman who had already priced out the risk and decided the return was him being exactly who he claimed.

Playful, vain in the way of people who’ve earned it, and possessed of a needle-sharp instinct for other people’s feelings — she clocks every crush in a five-meter radius before the people involved do. Underneath the variety-show persona: someone who learned early that money is the only thing that never leaves.

The ₩1.5 billion she had handed over as a test came back ₩660 million heavier inside five sessions — plus forty-four percent, delivered by a man who barely looked up — and the little mermaid’s ledger flipped from investigate him to treasure him on the spot (Ch. 106). She marked the discovery by ambushing him on an Ewha bench with the two most dangerous words in her arsenal — world of two — and received, for her trouble, the single most shameless counterattack in the book: “You knew. And you still came over to interrupt.” She is currently re-evaluating everything, including her own judgment of faces.

The Center

The mystery voice from Chapter 34 turned out to be the most photographed face in Korea, doing her banking in a baseball cap. Yoon-ah bought the Cheongdam building as an asset-preservation play, treats her tenants with formal courtesy, and negotiates like someone who has quietly read every book on commercial real estate ever written.

She and the MC are the same age, which produces a peculiar, careful friendship: two people carrying the same load of being publicly beloved and privately exhausted, splitting convenience-store beer in a parking lot at one in the morning. The Handbook knocked once. She didn’t answer. He respected that — and so did it.

The Kid Leader

The leader of the biggest girl group on the Peninsula, disguised beyond recognition at ONGI’s Cheongdam opening — cap, oversized mask, and the terrified grip of a woman on her first unauthorized outing in months. Unmasked, she is entirely bare-faced and somehow better lit than the ceremony. The fandom’s certified introvert, who in theory never leaves the dorm; in practice, materialized on a Tuesday morning to drag Lim Yoon-ah through the Cheongdam fashion street until both their legs filed complaints, then ate a hotpot lunch that reset her opinion of the genre.

She bowed a precise ninety degrees to a restaurant CEO she’d known for four minutes, accepted the word visual like a verdict she’d been waiting for since 2007, and left declaring a nine-member company dinner. The MC, for his part, noted the era’s single most recognizable voice in his address book and privately began wondering what the Goddess Handbook would make of her (Ch. 108–109).

Mama V

Twelve time zones from home, the eldest and the only foreigner in her group, Victoria built her leadership the hard way: flawless work, zero scandals, and a smile that never slips even when her knees are giving out. Sulli pitched her to Song Ji-yeon as two outsiders who would understand each other — the boy from Los Angeles and the woman from Qingdao, both fluent in the language of not-quite-belonging.

Her early coldness toward him was not rudeness but arithmetic: she had watched opportunists circle f(x) for years, and here came another smiling young man with money. What finally moved her was an afternoon in the Bamboo room of ONGI Apgujeong — a sudden switch into easy, native Californian English, a self-made gyopo standing where she had been prosecuting a chaebol heir, and her own apology delivered with a full ninety-degree bow. After that, the endorsement talk was only paperwork. The bond, in time, followed. Two imports in one city, with a shared weakness for the girl who brought them together.

Before the Velvet

Four years into training, first in and last out of every practice, living on evaluation scores and quiet terror of the monthly cuts. She met Song Ji-yeon when a small white dog — Mushroom, Eun-jung’s, in his care for the walk — ambushed her on a Cheongdam street; he called the dinner that followed “Mushroom’s apology meal,” and then couldn’t be talked out of walking her home. The Handbook tested her and stayed silent — genuine emotional resonance, it turns out, is not something you can farm with casual pats. She fled the scene of the attempt at a dead sprint, cheeks burning, dignity in tatters.

She has two more years of practice rooms ahead of her before a stage of her own. He told her she’d debut. He would know.

The Leader

It was So-yeon who called the closed-door meeting after the Black Ocean, and So-yeon who sat across from a 22-year-old restaurant CEO in a hanwoo restaurant and asked the only question that mattered: why would you want us? His answer — brand value, the Chinese market, a two-year promise she could hold him to — was the kind of leverage she’d learned to want more than comfort.

Measured, unglamorous, and the group’s designated adult. She noticed early that the man who grilled the meat for everyone without being asked was nothing like the boyfriend who never did — and filed the observation somewhere it would keep.

The Fox

Qri trusts no one who arrives with perfect timing, perfect manners, and a perfectly sized check. For most of the acquisition arc she ran a one-woman opposition party — interrogating motives, cataloguing inconsistencies, glaring across dinner tables — until the simple arithmetic of the situation (a company that feeds you versus a company that bleeds you) wore her resistance down to a single resentful “I have no objections.”

The glare that accompanied it was, everyone agreed, practically an embrace.

Her post-acquisition caseload includes the Gwacheon campfire census (Ch. 102), in which the count of members suspected of harboring feelings for the Sajangnim was revised upward — from one, to three, pending further fieldwork.

The Uninvited

The ribbon-bearer at the Cheongdam opening — cool, fine-featured, naggingly familiar to a man who had seen her face on a hundred future screens and couldn’t place a single one. Nineteen, from Ulsan, raised by a grandmother on a pension and a wish (if only our So-hee could be on TV too), employed to carry trays because the honest wage beat the doors that opened any other way. The industry that kept rejecting her had no idea it was auditioning.

She spent the entire signing offer assuming the worst — sponsorship, cages, clauses — ran the numbers like a woman who had been robbed before, and accepted anyway, for the television and the grandmother, on the arithmetic of least-bad. Then she escalated on her own: contract signed unread, name decreed mid-car-ride, and — upon discovering the misunderstanding in full — one kiss, self-administered, to double the position. The Goddess Handbook rang like a bell. She is now the fifth goddess, a Gaepo-dong resident with Kim Ji-won three floors up as her assigned sunbae, and the proud owner of four prepaid years of rent she didn’t ask for and tried hard not to accept (Ch. 108–113).
