Chapter Forty-Five

Forgot the Wallet

SAME EVENING · CONTINUOUS Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the sidewalk outside SM Entertainment

Joo-hyun, for a moment, had no comeback for that. Her lips moved — a sentence had started assembling somewhere behind them — and then gave up, unable to find a shape that would survive being said out loud.

She glanced at the dog, still quivering with goodwill toward her specifically, drew one long breath through her nose, and talked her heartbeat back down to something civic. The convenience-store bag migrated a few more centimeters behind her back.

“What are you doing in this part of town?” A topic change, executed at full speed. She had no intention of revisiting the previous one.

“I live nearby. Dog-walking.” He jingled the leash by way of proof, then tipped his head at her. “You, though — aren’t SM’s trainees all supposed to be sweating it out in the old Apgujeong building? Last time I ran into you and Seul-gi, it was around here too.”

He was honestly curious.

By his recollection of this era, the new Cheongdam tower belonged to the artists who had already debuted — the showcases, the shoots, the polished faces. Trainees, the not-yet-anybodies like her, did their sweating in the older Apgujeong building, where the floors had history and the mirrors had seen everything.

Joo-hyun blinked, visibly unprepared for the question to arrive with that much specificity.

“Evaluation footage. Quarterly.” Terse — and with that, the familiar frost reasserted itself in her voice. “We film it here.”

Every quarter, SM put its trainees on camera — song, dance, the whole inventory — and the tape decided who stayed, who went, and who stood where in line whenever a debut finally opened. Inside the company it was the event of the season. Every season.

“Ah.” He nodded, filed it away.

Then his eyes dropped to the bag she was failing to hide — the one with the rectangular bulges of chip packets and the unmistakable silhouette of a cola bottle.

“That’s dinner?”

The pink that touched her cheeks was barely there, and it was there anyway. She nodded.

The company managed a trainee’s body the way a bank managed gold — fixed meals, fixed calories, everything audited. And after a full day of that training, the cafeteria’s bland rations had all the appeal of cardboard. A bag of snacks was the entire reward system.

Looking at her — the hoodie, the chips, the heartbeat still settling — something in his chest went quietly sideways.

What a life. A girl her age, in this city, and her whole evening was going to be junk food eaten standing up. And his dog had just chased the soul halfway out of her. Walking away now would make him the villain twice over.

Besides, his own stomach had opinions, and they were getting louder.

“Let’s get dinner.” The pitch of it was pure old-friend casual, as if his dog hadn’t given her a cardiac event ten minutes ago. “Call it Mushroom’s apology meal. Look what he did to you — you went white as a boiled dumpling.”

“I did not.” The denial fired before her brain cleared it for release. Her face, in open rebellion, went red — being frightened of a dog the size of a bread loaf was humiliating.

“See? Red.” He pointed at her cheek, the corner of his mouth losing its fight.

“That’s — that’s from the running!” Chin up. Doubling down. The full posture of a person prepared to litigate.

He declined to press charges. He only jingled the leash again.

“So — humor me? I’m hungry too, and eating alone is a sad business.”

She hesitated. A trainee, out at night, at a private dinner with a man she technically didn’t know — if a single photo of that escaped into the wild, there would be paperwork. Possibly executions.

But the invitation had sounded sincere. And — a small, factual voice inside her noted — she was hungry.

He watched her waver, and played the last card with a sunny face.

“Just a quick meal, somewhere close. I promise you, on my company’s entire balance sheet — I will not sell you.”

“Pfft—”

The laugh escaped before she could stop it, and her shoulders came down from around her ears for the first time since the dog had clocked her.

“…Fine. Okay.”

“Let’s go.” He was in a noticeably good mood all of a sudden, towing a Mushroom who was already sightseeing in three directions at once.

She fell in behind him, half a meter of careful distance between them.

“What do you feel like eating?”

“Anything’s fine.”

“Budae jjigae?”

“No.” Instant. Absolute. “Too many calories.”

He stopped walking and turned around.

“Then you pick.”

Harder to feed than Sulli. I honestly didn’t think the scale went any higher.

Under his look she wilted by degrees — she knew precisely how she sounded.

“Maybe… just bibimbap? Something simple?” It came out at half volume.

He ran a search through his mental map of Cheongdam restaurants — and actually, he did know a place.

“There’s a Japanese teishoku house near here. Quiet, clean flavors, nothing heavy. How’s that?”

“Japanese?” Her eyes lit, one full watt. “Okay.”

They walked, one in front and one behind, and neither of them spent anything on conversation. Mushroom led the expedition from up front, short legs at a businesslike trot, head swiveling back at intervals to confirm the humans were keeping up.

✦ ✦ ✦

SAME EVENING a back alley, Cheongdam-dong Fūbutsu — a Japanese teishoku house

The restaurant sat at the end of a quiet lane: a wooden sliding door, a white paper lantern burning above it, and the shop’s name — Fūbutsu — brushed down the lantern’s side in ink calligraphy.

He slid the door open, and warm rice-steam rolled out to meet them.

The room was nearly empty — two tables by the window, low conversation, nothing else.

They took the corner booth. He pushed the menu across the table to her.

“You order. I’m easy.”

She accepted the menu and read it with the focus of a student sitting an exam she intended to pass with honors. Grilled mackerel set. Tamagoyaki, as a side.

He ordered the unagi rice bowl.

While they waited, the quiet settled back over the table. Mushroom curled up against his sneaker and dozed off mid-thought, duty done.

Ji-yeon studied the girl across from him — spine straight, hands stacked on her knees, chin level. The posture of an elementary schooler at an entrance interview.

“Joo-hyun-ssi.”

“Ne?”

“How long have you been a trainee now?”

“Almost four years.” Quietly.

Four years. He did the arithmetic behind a neutral face — entered the company in 2009, ground it out ever since.

“So your debut should be right around the corner, no?”

He asked it lightly, playing beautifully dumb. He knew exactly when she’d debut, and it was not around any corner; the question was pure social lubricant, because two people sitting in silence over barley tea was its own particular kind of unbearable.

Her eyes dimmed — one shade, corrected almost at once, but he caught it.

“I don’t know yet. The company hasn’t said anything for certain.”

At SM there were trainees who had practiced seven years, eight, ten — and gone home with nothing at all. Four years wasn’t long. And by the industry’s arithmetic she was already an older trainee; whether she debuted at all was, from where she sat, an open file.

He watched the light go out of her, and heard himself answer it with the calm of a man reading tomorrow’s newspaper.

“You’re going to debut. I’d bet on it.”

Her eyes came back on like a struck match.

“Really? Song Ji-yeon-ssi thinks I’ll debut?”

“Ne.” He nodded, gravely, the way a man swears an oath in court. You’re going to be the face of an entire generation in this business, Joo-hyun-ssi. Of course you’ll debut.

The food arrived — two trays laid out with the neatness of a jewelry display. The mackerel’s skin had gone crackling gold; his unagi sat glazed and dark, the kabayaki laid on thick enough to cast a shadow.

“Eat.” He nudged the mackerel set toward her and went to work on his eel with none of the ceremony.

They ate in silence, and the silence had stopped being awkward somewhere around the first bite. He’d been here once before — the fish was as fresh as he remembered, the rice proper Akita Komachi, every grain its own little person.

The meal wound down to its last grains. He rose to settle the bill, patted the pocket of his hoodie —

— and the hand stopped moving.

Joo-hyun watched the entire performance. One eyebrow climbed.

“What’s wrong?”

He scratched his head, sheepish to the roots of his hair.

“I… left the house in a hurry tonight. I think — I think I forgot my wallet.”

He genuinely hadn’t meant it. He’d swapped his jacket for a hoodie to walk the dog, and the wallet had stayed in the jacket — safe, sound, and useless, hanging at home.

He was, privately, exasperated with the entire Peninsula. A developed country, top of every economic chart, and not a scrap of mobile payment anywhere. In his old life in LA he’d stopped carrying a wallet years ago — the phone did everything, from parking meters to rent. Here the phone was an expensive brick, and dinner ran on leather and paper.
