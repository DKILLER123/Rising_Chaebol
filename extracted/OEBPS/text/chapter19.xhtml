Chapter Nineteen

The Jeju Concert

FRIDAY NIGHT · SEPTEMBER 7 Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Ji-yeon’s apartment

Home. He shed his shoes at the door and tossed his keys into the tray on the entryway console; metal rang on metal, one bright note in the quiet.

The main lights were off. Only the floor lamp in the corner was doing its warm amber best. He drew a glass of water at the open kitchen counter and folded himself onto the sofa.

Phone out. One notification was waiting — from Ham Eun-jung, timed to somewhere in the middle of his drive home.

An attached photo: a suitcase, black, flung open on a bed. Inside, a handful of T-shirts folded with almost frightening precision — and, poured over and around them like a rockslide, a truly heroic quantity of snacks.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Ham Eun-jung

Help me check — what am I missing?

[ bunny emoticon: glancing left… glancing right… ]

Song Ji-yeon

At this snack-to-clothing ratio, are you playing a concert or catering a picnic?

He had zoomed in and given the photo a genuine, serious audit — the snacks held his attention for several full seconds — and the laugh had gotten away from him before he could stop it.

Ham Eun-jung

They’re for the members!!

[ three crying bunny emoticons in a row ]

We’re not allowed real food if we want to keep our figures. Backstage, this is the whole buffet.

He stared at the message, somewhere between fond and speechless. Peninsula idols ran half-starved for weeks around any comeback — the industry’s idea of figure management — to the point that girls fainting onstage from low blood sugar was practically its own news category. He had read enough of those headlines in his first life to be inured to it.

Except that he happened to know the stage these six were starving themselves for was going to be performed to a hall that wanted them gone. Did they really need to do this to themselves for that?

He typed a few words. Deleted them. Typed again. Deleted again. Finally he sent the only three that survived.

Song Ji-yeon

Is it worth it?

Send. Water. One mouthful. The screen lit again almost at once.

Ham Eun-jung

What’s ‘worth it’?

[ bunny emoticon: head tilted, one large question mark ]

He looked at that question mark for a long moment and didn’t answer.

He knew the Black Ocean was waiting for the six of them. That was exactly why every ounce of this looked unbearable to him — the hunger, the red-eye, the meticulously folded T-shirts. But there was no version of that sentence he could send, and even if he sent it, she wouldn’t believe it. Not everybody was carrying the next seven years around in their head. And the Ham Eun-jung of right now still believed — honestly, stubbornly — that one good stage could turn the whole group’s name around.

Song Ji-yeon

Nothing. Forget it. Finish packing — you’ve got a flight to catch. The concert is tomorrow and your company is flying you out tonight. What kind of death-march schedule is that?

The six of them were on the eleven-o’clock flight to Jeju tonight. Land in the small hours. Rehearsal in the morning. Show at night. Then straight onto the overnight leg back to Seoul. His bones ached by hearsay alone.

Eun-jung, for her part, minded not at all — and had risen to defend the company with the loyalty of a foot soldier: most Korean agencies booked the deep-night flights on purpose—

Ham Eun-jung

One, it saves money. Two, there’s no one at the airport at night — no crowds, no scenes.

[ bunny emoticon: shrugging, as if explaining the weather ]

He had no words. He looked at the grinning bunny avatar for a while, and the feeling in his chest refused to resolve into any single thing — heartache and helplessness, in roughly equal parts. Eun-jung, ever the company’s faithful workhorse: sold down the river, and still counting the money for the sellers. Whatever those red-eye tickets saved was a rounding error against what six girls pulled into that building every quarter.

Song Ji-yeon

Get some sleep when you land in Jeju.

Ham Eun-jung

Ne~ you too!

He killed the phone, dropped it on the table, lay flat on the sofa, and stared at the ceiling, turning the problem over and over. How to pry the six of them out from under the bullying scandal before it metastasized. No clean answer came. None.

So he sighed and let the decision settle — the one he had been carrying since the day Eun-jung’s card activated. Next month. Whatever the market was doing, however much the positions had grown: cash out. Take it all, and buy CCM Entertainment.

CCM’s management had fumbled the troll incident one stupid decision at a time and walked six girls steadily toward the cliff edge. If he were only a bystander, a couple of sighs about the unfairness of the world would have been the whole of his contribution. But he wasn’t a bystander anymore. He had the means to do something about it.

So he would.

SATURDAY, SEPTEMBER 8 · 4:00 PM Jeju International Airport show day at the ICC

The arrivals hall was a single solid organism made of people.

The ICC concert hadn’t invited T-ara alone. The heavyweights were a statement in themselves: BoA, G.NA. Boy groups: SHINee, 2AM, the EXO-K subunit. Girl groups: A Pink, Nine Muses. Between them they accounted for a fair share of the industry’s front line at that moment — on paper, the lineup was frankly decadent.

Which meant the airport was neck-deep in fans meeting the flights — slogan towels and banners and glowing gear, the arrivals hall packed to leaking. Passengers and fandoms flowed together in one current. Orderly, technically. Dense beyond all sense. Ji-yeon was extruded out of the airport by the crowd itself, the way a cork leaves a bottle.

Black backpack. He threaded through sideways, head down. Somebody’s shoulder rammed his; somebody’s heel found his shoe; a girl wielding a selfie stick nearly planted her phone in his eye socket with what he chose to read as enthusiasm. He took a breath, cinched the straps tighter, and shoved for daylight.

When the glass doors finally let him through, Jeju’s September heat rolled over him like a warm wet towel — and he discovered his back was soaked clean through.

Out front he grabbed a taxi, straight for the ICC venue. The cab ran the coastal road, and the September sea wind poured in through the window — briny, faintly fishy, somehow clean. It smelled like an island. Which, as far as Seoul was concerned, Jeju practically was.

“Here for the concert, I take it?” The driver was a man in his fifties, round-faced, eyes vanishing into slits when he smiled. He checked the mirror.

“Yes.”

“My daughter’s going too. She likes that — what is it — A Pink? Something about a ‘Chorong.’ Couldn’t sleep last night. Three in the morning, she’s pacing the living room until her mother went after her with a pillow.” He laughed and slapped the steering wheel lightly. There wasn’t a grain of reproach in it. If anything, the man was proud enough to burst.

Ji-yeon smiled. “A Pink performs today too.”

“That’s exactly why she went and queued at the venue before breakfast. Wouldn’t eat a bite.” The driver shook his head, a man thoroughly defeated and delighted about it. “Kids these days. For them, the group outranks meals. Outranks sleep. Outranks mothers.”

Out the window, a giant billboard for the ICC concert slid past — every performing artist’s face printed across it in rows. T-ara stood left of center: six figures in black gowns, faces cool and remote, nothing like the sweetness the group was known for.

His eyes stayed on the billboard until the road bent and a building took it away.

He hadn’t come early; the queues outside the venue had already put down roots.

The ICC hall was an oval shell, its silver-grey skin flashing in the sun — a giant metal egg laid along Jeju’s coastline.

Fencing ringed the grounds, and a dozen tents stood at the entrances: ticketing, merchandise, first aid, fan rest stations. The fandoms had sorted themselves into districts behind their banners, and the geography of it was almost beautiful. SHINee’s pearl-aqua green held the left flank, a fluorescent sea swaying in the sea wind. EXO’s silver-white held the right — overwhelmingly young girls, a good number in T-shirts printed with their favorite members’ faces. A Pink’s pink battalion held the very front, the deepest of all; a cluster of fans was holding up a banner the size of a bedsheet: CHORONG-AH, WE’LL ALWAYS BE WITH YOU.

And then he saw the others.

A few fans standing along the fence, each holding up a sheet of A4 paper with marker scrawled across it: T-ara OUT.

They weren’t chanting. They weren’t waving. They just stood there, expressionless, holding the signs at chest height — less like a protest than like a duty. Like people completing a rite.

Ji-yeon’s step hitched as he passed them. Then he took his eyes back and kept walking.

Ticket scanned. Inside, the concourse was roomier than the forecourt and still jammed shoulder to shoulder. Staff in matching fluorescent vests cut back and forth with radios, the way tide cuts through pilings.

The hall itself was bigger than he’d expected — a high dome, and lighting rigs hanging down from it so dense they read as a sky of constellations, suspended upside down.

His seat: third row, dead center ahead of the extended stage. The sightline ran straight into the heart of the main stage — close enough to read the shine on a performer’s lips.

It had cost him. He had gone through a string of scalpers to pry that one ticket loose.

He tested the angle and nodded once. At this distance, nothing on that stage would escape him. That had been the entire point.

The seats around him filled in. On his left, two girls in A Pink shirts, pink ribbons at their wrists, one sticking glitter decals along her cheekbone. On his right, a twenty-something in glasses with a SHINee light stick, head bowed over a fan-chat room, his thumb working like a loom.

Ji-yeon set the backpack by his feet, leaned back, and waited for the lights.
