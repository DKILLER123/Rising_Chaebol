Chapter Fifty-Five

Knowing Exactly How That Feels

SUNDAY · EARLY AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the flat, just the two of them

“The thing is… I should get going too.”

Eun-jung’s cheeks were still carrying heat. She could not afford one more second standing in this flat — something in here kept happening to her pulse.

“Wait.”

He called her back, dried his hands on the towel, and came out of the kitchen to stand in front of her.

“You look wiped out.” There was real worry in it. If his reflexes hadn’t covered for her at the sink, they’d have been sweeping up porcelain. “You haven’t actually rested since Hong Kong, have you.”

She blinked — and nodded before she’d decided to. Back-to-back schedules, a red-eye home, a morning dragged around show flats by the whirlwind, and now this. She was running, honestly, on vapor.

“The guest room’s made up fresh. Sleep here for a while — I’d rather not send you back out like this.”

He said it plainly, seriously, the way he said things he’d already decided.

Her resolve wobbled, and the want was loud. A real bed. A real hour or three. And… her eyes moved, once, over him. She couldn’t have explained it under questioning, but something about this flat made her feel safe.

“Then… it really isn’t too much trouble?” Small voice.

“None.” He smiled — it’s a nap, what trouble is a nap. “Sleep. When you wake up, I’ll drive you home.”

“…Okay.”

And as if something else had done the deciding for her, she nodded.

He walked her down the corridor to the second bedroom at the end of it.

The room kept the same modern-minimal skeleton as the master, only softer in palette — clean, quiet, the air holding the faint warm smell of bedding that had been out in the sun.

“You rest. Call out if you need anything.”

He stayed at the doorway and came no further.

“Mm.”

He drew the door gently shut for her.

In the room, Eun-jung stood a moment, looking at the closed door. That was it? He just… left? And up from somewhere beneath her dignity floated a thought she refused to sign her name to: is my face not enough, then…?

“Yah, Ham Eun-jung — what on earth is going on with you.”

She shook her head at herself, laughed once, gave it up, and went to the bed. Shoes off. In.

The mattress was soft enough to take her whole, and the pillow rose around her ears carrying a faint, clean scent.

The same scent he carried.

She closed her eyes — and the hand-touch played itself again, uninvited, frame by frame: the plate, his hand, the heat of his palm. She rolled over and pushed her burning face straight down into the pillow.

Done for.

She had — it seemed, apparently, without reservation — genuinely begun to fall.

✦ ✦ ✦

Song Ji-yeon came back out to the living room, took one look at the shut guest-room door, and confirmed it: down for the count. He switched off the television, got up without a sound, and carried himself into the study, closing that door too.

Desk. Laptop. The market page came up; he typed in the code and pulled Samsung Electronics’ candlestick chart.

Two days since he’d last looked. Two days had brought him almost nothing worth the trip. He shook his head, and the resolve set another degree harder — tomorrow, all of it goes. Out of Samsung, and into one short-term hold instead.

Today was Sunday. Exchange shut, no orders going anywhere. Everything waited for Monday.

He opened the Goddess Handbook. In Eun-jung’s card wallet, side by side, sat two Stock Forecast Cards — and they were the entire reason he was rotating out of Samsung at all. Each card named the single biggest gainer of the week ahead.

The plan was simple. Card picks the rocket. Liquidate everything, buy in all at once, sell on day five. Repeat. Run it right, and the compounding left long-term Samsung standing in the road.

“One card covers a week. Two cards — two weeks.” His fingers kept time on the desktop, tap, tap. “Done right, two weeks could multiply the pot several times over.”

He wouldn’t swear to a number, though. The cards drew at random — and his memory of 2012’s short-term rockets was, if he was honest with himself, fog. So it all came down to how far the card’s pick would actually run.

The last of the sun was coming through the blinds now, laying long slats of orange across the wood floor.

He pulled himself out of the charts, kneaded the ache out of his eyes, and went back out to the living room.

The big space held nothing but quiet.

On soft feet he came to the guest-room door. It stood ajar — not fully shut. Through the gap he could see her: lying on her side, breathing long and even, asleep in earnest. Still in her day clothes, coat long since off, curled up small into herself, hair fanned loose across the pillow — a fragility the stage never once showed.

Days of running, and whatever weight had been riding on top of the running — it had genuinely worn her down.

He stood at the door for a while and went no further. Then he turned, poured a glass of warm water in the kitchen, set it where she’d see it, and settled onto the sofa to wait — one hand idly rolling Mushroom’s belly while the little dog wriggled in place, hind legs pedaling at nothing.

Time slid by. The orange-red outside sank all the way into ink-blue, and the city put its neon on.

At some point the guest-room door eased open, and Eun-jung padded out, sleep still draped over her shoulders. She stood at the doorway blinking — the man on the sofa, the little dog gnawing his sleeve — and it took her a second to reassemble the world and remember where she was.

“Awake?” He set Mushroom down and smiled up at her.

“Mm…” A beat of recollection, a nod. The rasp of the just-woken was still all over her voice. “How long was I out?”

“Not long. Four hours, give or take.”

“THAT long?” She drifted over to the sofa, doziness and shock sharing the face. “Sorry — I’ve been endless trouble.”

“What trouble.” He was already up. “Hungry? I could make you something.”

“No, no—” Both hands waving it off, and then the phone came up as her evidence. “I should really get home. Eomma’s waiting for me.”

He glanced at the screen. KakaoTalk, open on a chat saved as Eomma — a short thread, all business.

KakaoTalk · Eomma — on Ham Eun-jung’s phone

Eomma

Eun-jung-ah, where did you get to?

Come home tonight. I’ll set dinner for you.

“Right. Then I’ll drive you.” He didn’t press any further, just reached for the car keys off the sofa arm.

“Okay…”

She hesitated, and agreed anyway. Then she went to the corner of the sofa and started gathering Mushroom’s things — the cushion, the toys — folding his little world into her arms.

“Thank you for looking after him all this while. I’ll take the baby home now.”

Watching her pack the dog’s life into a neat bundle, a lyric from his first life surfaced from absolutely nowhere — some heartbreak ballad off the LA radio of his memory, the kind that played in every diner at 2 a.m.: take the house, take the ring, take every last thing you want — but leave me the dog.

He was, it turned out, genuinely a little attached. A few days of dog duty, and the feelings had already put down roots — so that was it. That was what the man who’d sung it was going through, back when he wrote it. Song Ji-yeon finally knew exactly how that felt.

Somewhere across the Pacific, presumably, the singer would have had notes: Buddy, I wrote that mid-breakup, over a woman, in a rented apartment. You have never once dated anyone. What exactly is it you “know”?

One man, one woman, one dog — a small procession, out of the apartment and into the night. Cheongdam-dong after dark had shed the whole day’s noise; the breeze ran cool and easy through it, and it peeled the last of the sleepiness off Eun-jung’s shoulders.

The car moved smooth and quiet through the homegoing streets, nothing in the cabin but the radio, playing something slow.

Eun-jung watched the city run backward past her window — streetlight after streetlight sweeping across her face, bright, then dark, then bright.

“Today… thank you.” Her voice found the quiet and broke it gently. “For Ji-yeon’s place, and for…”

She didn’t finish the sentence. He knew the shape of it anyway.

“It was nothing.” His eyes stayed on the road, his voice easy. “Besides — Ji-yeon’s my neighbor now. Neighbors looking out for neighbors. That’s the whole job description.”

The radio played on, and the night kept rolling past the windows.
