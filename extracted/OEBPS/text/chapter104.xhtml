Chapter One Hundred Four

The Raccoon and the Shiba

SATURDAY · NOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Daewoo Royal County — the underground garage, then Unit 201

The charcoal Mercedes slid into the Daewoo Royal County garage and settled into its slot with the smooth, decelerating hush of a car that knew the way home.

Song Ji-yeon set the brake, unbuckled, and turned around to announce their arrival to the back seat—

Too late. The door was already open and the dragon was already out.

“I’m HOOOME!”

A stretch of pure, shameless amplitude — arms overhead, back arched, every joint petitioning for reinstatement.

Last night’s stars had been five-star, no complaints. But gold nest, silver nest — none of it beats your own doghouse, and the moment familiar walls closed around her, the little dragon’s whole body went slack with contentment.

Song Ji-yeon looked back at the crater she’d left in the seat, speechless. The girl had slept like a casualty the entire drive — and now, one foot on concrete, she was running at a brightness that could light the garage.

“That’s Ji-yeon for you.” Eun-jung laughed, gathering her bag. “Asleep the second the door shuts, fully charged the second it opens. Every ride. Both directions.”

“Now I’ve seen the spec sheet in person…” He smiled and pushed his own door open. “All right. We’re here. Out, both of you.”

“Mm.”

He came around to the trunk and lifted the gate — and let out a long breath at the mountain range crammed inside.

“Yes, the stretch is over. Come carry something. Every last thing on this pile is something you campaigned for.”

Eun-jung was at his shoulder before he could reach in, lifting out two of the lighter totes, her smile as soft as ever.

“I’ll take these.”

Her own building stood in Apgujeong, and her own bed was in it. She had ridden up to Cheongdam anyway — for no stated reason, and one obvious one: one man, one mountain of gear, and not enough hands.

Like a line of ants, the three of them ferried the camp home. It took two full round trips before the last tent finally wedged into the storage room.

By then, the wall clock stood at straight-up noon.

Park Ji-yeon achieved the sofa at terminal velocity, spread flat into a starfish, and rubbed her deflated stomach with both hands. A long, tragic howl filled the apartment.

“Oppaaa… I’m starving…”

The pout deployed. The big, dewy eyes turned on him, brimming. The voice came out in that sweet, clingy register that could pull taffy into strands.

“We only had a tiny bit of bread at the campsite this morning. And then all that hard labor just now — my stomach is literally drumming. Oppa. Cook for me? Pretty, pretty please?”

He looked down at the picture she made — flushed, boneless, fully committed to the bit — and felt the corner of his mouth climbing of its own accord. He shook his head, indulgent to the bone.

“You. Carrying two bags of chips counts as hard labor now? I genuinely do not understand where the food goes. There is no warehouse in there.”

He clapped the dust off his palms. “Fine. Let me see what this kitchen can do.”

What the kitchen could do was this: a refrigerator containing several bottles of drink and a stack of sheet masks. Not one item of food. Not a scallion. Not an egg. A walk-in this empty, in the restaurants he’d grown up in, would have gotten somebody written up by close of shift.

“Park Ji-yeon. This fridge is cleaner than your bare face.”

“Aigoo, I don’t really cook, oppa~” floated back from the living room, entirely shameless. “Just run downstairs and raid your own kitchen!”

So down he went, one flight, in resigned obedience to his fate.

His own refrigerator turned out to be barely better — nowhere near enough fresh ingredients for three people. He took inventory at a glance, made an executive decision, and came back up with three packs of instant jajangmyeon, a clutch of eggs, and a tub of kimchi.

The black-bean noodles of every Koreatown childhood, in other words. Fine dining had its place; today was not that place.

The kitchen filled up with small, busy sounds.

Noodles into the pot. Drained, rinsed cold, then folded through the glossy black chunjang until every strand shone like lacquer. Oil into the pan next. Eggs cracked in and fried to the exact degree he wanted — edges crisp and golden-brown, the yolks still molten and trembling.

“Food’s ready.”

Three steaming bowls reached the table.

Park Ji-yeon materialized at the first thread of smell, chopsticks already drawn. She stabbed a yolk, watched the gold flood out across the noodles, stirred the whole thing into a riot, and started shoveling.

“Mmm—mmph…” The praise fought its way out around a full mouth. “It’s SO good. Oppa, your instant noodles beat actual restaurants. Actual. Restaurants.”

Eun-jung ate the way she did everything — quietly, elegantly, all the way to the end — and still found a free moment between bites to pass him a napkin, satisfaction folded into the curve of her eyes.

One simple lunch, eaten clean by three people.

Fed and watered, the three of them migrated to the wide sofa and dissolved into it, conversation drifting nowhere in particular.

Afternoon light came through the floor-to-ceiling windows, warm and honeyed — the specific grade of warmth that manufactures drowsiness on contact.

Song Ji-yeon, for one, had a sleep debt to report. A sleeping bag was not a bed, and the girls’ tents had held a summit well past midnight — four tents pitched close, a soft rustling murmur bleeding through the nylon, words indistinguishable, duration extremely distinguishable. He had dropped off embarrassingly late.

And this morning, the girls had been up and functional before he was.

He had marveled at the time. So this was girl-group sleep quality?

He’d heard the legend before — certain idol groups running promos on four, five hours of sleep a day — and had filed it under industry mythology. Their stages never showed a gram of it, after all. He was a believer now.

High-energy people, every last one of them.

He glanced at the two still chattering away on the sofa, decided they required nothing from him, and poured himself into the single seat for a nap.

He had just gotten his eyes to the closing stage when the phone buzzed in his pocket.

Park Seon-young.

Phone · Park Seon-young → Song Ji-yeon

“Seon-young. What’s up?” The greeting came out at half speed.

“Sajangnim! Great news!” The voice arrived at parade volume, excitement pouring through the speaker with no effort at containment. “You told me to scout office space — I’ve got one!”

His eyebrows went up, and the nap began evacuating his body. “That fast? Talk.”

He’d handed the man the assignment yesterday. Results were already on the line.

“A four-story building, Sajangnim. Grand frontage, roomy floors — big enough to carry ShiningYouth through the entire launch phase. The rent sits comfortably inside what the company can carry. Everything on my side is ready; it’s only waiting on your eyes. Could you come see it today?”

“The efficiency, honestly, borders on the unnatural. Where is it?”

“Cheongdam-dong! Ten minutes by car from your home. As prime as prime gets on this Peninsula.”

Ten minutes from home — the rich quarter, the entertainment cluster, prime by every measure that mattered. Still: a building is judged on foot.

“Ten minutes from home. Hard to say no without looking — I’ll come see it.”

“Of course, Sajangnim. I’m on-site. I’ll wait as long as the waiting takes.”

He hadn’t stepped away for the call, the living room was church-quiet, and Park Seon-young’s voice could probably have crossed the Han River unassisted. Every word had landed in full.

The girls had stopped chatting. Two pairs of eyes hung on him, shining at a very specific wattage — the exact wattage of kittens who have just discovered a yarn store.

This was going to be their company.

To an idol, the company building was a second home. There was no version of the universe in which they didn’t care.

The call had barely ended before the dragon rolled up off the sofa and materialized in front of him, sparkling at maximum.

“Oppa. Is it our new company’s building? Did they find it?!”

He looked from her to Eun-jung — two faces carrying the same undisguised hope — and nodded, smiling.

“WAH! Can we come see it?!” She had his arm by now and was shaking it like a vending machine that owed her a drink. “Take us with you! Pleeease, oppa, please?”

What, honestly, did he know about judging an office building? He had never once studied how an entertainment company was supposed to be laid out. Two working idols on the walk-through meant two sets of professional instincts he didn’t have.

And the company existed for T-ara in the first place. They’d spend half their lives inside those walls soon enough — the walls might as well submit to inspection now.

“Sure. Let’s all go.”

“Yay! Oppa’s the best!”

She came up off the floor, arms flying wide for a flying-tackle hug, lips already pursing in on his cheek—

His palm caught her square on the forehead and walked her back half a step.

“Whoa, whoa. Restraint.”

A sidelong glance went to Eun-jung, followed by one meaningful cough.

This kid. Eun-jung was standing right there. If that kiss had actually connected, what exactly was he supposed to say about it afterward?

Eun-jung watched the whole exchange with a hand hiding her mouth and her shoulders quietly shaking — one more of Ji-yeon’s clingy episodes, filed under ordinary. In her ledger, Ji-yeon had always been exactly this attached to him. Nothing deeper to audit.

SATURDAY AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul The new headquarters — one corner up from JYP

Soon after, the three of them were cleaned up, changed into light clothes, and back in the Mercedes.

Guided by the address, the car threaded the streets of Cheongdam-dong, smooth and unhurried. A little over ten minutes later, it slowed in front of a building with a sharply modern face—

—and from the back seat came a sound of pure, short-circuiting disbelief.

On the facade, in enormous, unmissable letters: JYP ENTERTAINMENT.

“Oppa, did you take a wrong turn?” One finger aimed at the window, scandalized. “That’s JYP’s headquarters. What, did you buy JYP or something?!”

He blinked too. The navigation was checked; the navigation was certain. This was, definitively, the address.

The phone came out.

Phone · Park Seon-young → Song Ji-yeon

“The address you sent me is JYP? Don’t tell me you rented Park Jin-young’s building out from under him.”

“Aigoo, Sajangnim — where would I get that kind of reach! Our building is right beside JYP’s. Drive a little further up the street. I’m waiting at the corner for you.”

Mystery solved. He hung up and rolled the car another few hundred meters up the street.

And there, at the intersection, stood Park Seon-young — round-faced, up on his toes, scanning traffic like a lifeguard.

Song Ji-yeon pulled to the kerb.

The moment the boss’s car was identified, Park Seon-young’s face rearranged itself into its most devoted setting, and he came over at a trot, solicitude pouring off him in sheets.

“Sajangnim, welcome, welcome! Was the drive rough? Cheongdam traffic, honestly — it’s a contact sport out here…”

The passenger door opened, and Ham Eun-jung stepped out in a ball cap.

Then the rear door, and out hopped Park Ji-yeon, armored in cap and mask to the same standard.

Park Seon-young’s mouth stopped moving mid-syllable.

He recognized them instantly, hats or no hats. Ham Eun-jung and Park Ji-yeon — the two women at the center of the loudest entertainment story on the Peninsula right now.

His brain spun up to full RPM. A man who had survived two decades in corporate Korea did not require a diagram.

The boss had registered an entertainment company one week — and dropped a fortune to sign T-ara, scandal and all, the next. And here all three of them came climbing out of a single car, settled into one another’s company like family.

No relationship?

A ghost wouldn’t buy it.

Conclusion, stamped in triplicate: these two women were never, under any circumstances, to be offended. They went straight into the highest tier of his Do-Not-Provoke registry, and his smile widened until the creases around his eyes ran out of room.

“Aigoo — and Miss Eun-jung and Miss Ji-yeon have come too! Sajangnim, why didn’t you say so ahead of time? I’d have prepared proper meeting gifts!”

Park Ji-yeon cracked up and leaned into Eun-jung, whispering that this ajusshi was a trip.

“Enough.” Song Ji-yeon cut through. “Save the flattery for the mirror. Show us the building.”

“Ne, Sajangnim. This way, please.”

The building stood just off the street, a guard booth at its gate — unstaffed — and they walked straight through.

Under Park Seon-young’s conducting hand, the three of them entered four floors of empty, sunlit potential.

“Sajangnim, look. Built fresh this year. The previous tenant was a video-game company — the owner ran it aground within months, and now he’s desperate to rent it back out and recover something. Game company, entertainment company — the floor plans are cousins, honestly. All the infrastructure is in place, everything from wiring to fixtures. We clear out two spacious practice rooms, and the building goes to work immediately.”

They circulated — ground floor, up, back down, all four stories under their feet.

“What do you think?” He turned to Eun-jung, voice pitched low.

She nodded, and the satisfaction had reached her whole face.

“It’s lovely. Generous spaces, wonderful light. It’s a far better environment than anything we had at CCM.”

Beside her, the dragon was nodding along at a frequency normally reserved for metronomes.

Off at one side, Park Seon-young observed the boss going specifically to Miss Eun-jung for her verdict — noted the graceful, settled way she delivered it — and achieved perfect mental clarity.

The theory in his head hardened into certainty: this Miss Eun-jung was, nine times out of ten, the future Madam President of ShiningYouth. Rank would be observed accordingly, and her rank was first.

With both of them sold, Song Ji-yeon stopped deliberating.

“Eun-jung — take Ji-yeon and wander the floors. Get familiar; it’s yours. Seon-young. Upstairs with me. Landlord.”

The arrangements had been made before he came: Park Seon-young had already sat down with the building’s owner once, settled the shape of a deal, and left the man waiting in the fourth-floor reception room for precisely this.

Greetings, bows, tea. The owner’s desperation required no translation — a tenant for the whole building, to him, was less a negotiation than a rescue.

They sat down in the office and went straight to the numbers. By the 2012 Cheongdam-dong market, after a short, brisk exchange, the shape of the deal landed:

LEASE · SHININGYOUTH ENTERTAINMENT — HEADQUARTERS, TERM SHEET

Four-story building, Cheongdam-dong — former video-game company premises, completed this year; handed over inclusive of fixtures and equipment.

Conversion works: two practice rooms to be fitted out before Wednesday, October 10.

Security deposit — ₩500,000,000

Monthly rent — ₩40,000,000 · term: one year, executed in full

Signed and sealed: Song Ji-yeon, CEO, ShiningYouth Entertainment Co., Ltd.

He didn’t blink. The pen came out and the signature went down.

Five hundred million down, forty million a month — frankly, modest. He signed the entire year in one stroke.

Ink dried. Done. ShiningYouth Entertainment officially had a home.

Contract executed, keys handed over, the two men came downstairs, collected the girls, and walked out of the building together.

He turned to Park Seon-young and started issuing the follow-ups.

“Seon-young. From here, speed matters. Get a cleaning crew through the building. Get the company’s frame standing.”

“Leave it to me, Sajangnim!” A palm thumped his own chest like a warranty. “The moment your orders landed, I posted the job listings and engaged the headhunters. Interviews run tomorrow — the frame will be standing, staff on deck, by Monday!”

“Department heads first. The rank-and-file, let each head hire their own.”

“Understood!”

“And one item with a hard deadline.” The voice flattened into business. “By Wednesday of next week, I want this building presentable — minimum one working practice room. The week after, T-ara is in Japan on the Okinawa showcase and the album promotions. They need to be back in practice before the plane is.”

“Mission guaranteed, Sajangnim!”

SATURDAY EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul A Japanese dining bar, around the corner from the new office

Instructions issued, the three of them saw Park Seon-young off.

With this street about to become a workplace, nobody was in a hurry to leave it. They walked the neighborhood instead, learning its shape — the café on the corner, the crosswalk, the convenience store that would matter at midnight.

Somewhere along the way the light went amber, and it was past five.

“Walked enough. Let’s find dinner.”

A Japanese dining bar on the corner caught his eye — clean lines, soft lantern glow — and he led the two of them in.

They had settled in and gotten an order placed when the wind chime over the door rang out, bright and clear.

Ching—

Two girls pushed in.

He glanced over out of idle politeness — and something snagged. There was a strange, misplaced familiarity about the pair of faces.

His brain served the answer before he’d finished asking the question.

TWICE. Hirai Momo. Minatozaki Sana.

Momo. And Sana.

Both girls wore the specific, recognizable fatigue of people freshly released from a day of practice. As they scanned the room for seats, one gaze swept across it — caught on Eun-jung’s face — then caught again, harder, on the little dragon sitting beside her.

The whole expression transformed.

T-ara was huge in Japan; recognition took no effort at all. The man at the table was nobody she knew — but handsome, seated with T-ara members? By elimination, some sunbae or other.

Excitement detonated. Momo seized Sana’s sleeve and towed her to the table, where the two of them bowed — deep, hurried, drilled to perfection.

“Hel—hello, sunbaenims…”

The Korean came out soft and round-cornered. Unmistakably foreign. Ten meters from JYP’s doorstep, the math solved itself: trainees. Japanese trainees.

Eun-jung and Park Ji-yeon traded a quick glance. Trainee hoobaes — at a random restaurant? It was a little awkward, truthfully — but they smiled and nodded back with real warmth.

“Hello there. Take care of yourselves — work hard, okay?”

“Thank you, sunbaenim… we will work hard…”

Eun-jung offered a few more words of encouragement — the hard stretch now is the stretch that matters most, and may your debut come soon and land softly — and the two girls bowed again and again, gratitude overflowing.

But reading the profound, solemn wisdom shining in both pairs of eyes, Song Ji-yeon reached a quieter conclusion: the raccoon and the shiba had not understood a single word.

By the calendar, they couldn’t have been in the country long at all. The language was still under construction.

Watching the two of them nod along with total, uncomprehending gravity, he cracked an involuntary smile.

After their bows, the pair made their way to an empty booth by the wall — placed, as it happened, directly on the diagonal from him.
