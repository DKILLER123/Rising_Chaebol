Knock-knock.

Eight in the morning, and Song Ji-yeon stood at Park Ji-yeon’s door with his knuckles doing the talking.

He had the door code. Of course he had the door code. But some lessons install themselves permanently, and the last time he had keyed himself in unannounced, his eyes had met sights not cleared for his clearance level. A man learns. A man knocks.

“Ji-yeon! So-hee! Up — company time.”

Silence from within.

Knock-knock.

“Park Ji-yeon! Han So-hee! On the count of nothing, I’m entering the code myself.”

His thumb was already migrating toward the keypad when the lock gave way on its own.

Click.

The door swung — and the person holding it was not Park Ji-yeon.

Fighting skills, then, and a weekly charm card — a modest haul by the Handbook’s standards. But free is free, and he’d never once met a free thing he didn’t like. His mood toward the girl downstairs had adjusted accordingly: the more he looked at her, the more agreeable the looking got.

How close are these two, exactly? A neighbor — fine. But neighbors don’t have door codes, and door-code holders don’t do bedroom entries. What is the actual relationship here?

“Calling doesn’t work on this one.”

A faint smile, the smile of a man citing settled case law.

“But she’s a girl—”

The bedroom door was already open, and he was already through it, leaving Han So-hee alone in the hallway to process the jurisdictional questions.

The bedroom delivered as advertised: one Park Ji-yeon, fully cocooned, a single tuft of hair breaking the silk of the blanket like a periscope.

He walked over, took the blanket’s edge in one fist, and peeled the whole thing off in a single stroke.

“Ah! Oppa!”

The dragon sat bolt upright — and the pale strap of a sleep top had slid entirely off one shoulder, and from this altitude the topography blurred precisely where his manners most needed contour lines: shoulder, collarbone, and one soft suggestion of a northern hemisphere, unregulated. Hair a war zone. Eyes at forty percent and closing.

His gaze hung for exactly one frame. The tip of his nose reported an unseasonable warmth — the evening’s residual fire, he ruled, banked but not extinguished, now feeding on fresh kindling at seven-fifty-eight in the morning.

He re-aimed his eyes at the curtain rod with visible discipline.

“Yah, oppa — the blanket. Return it. Now.”

She yanked a pillow into defensive position and sat cross-legged behind it, negotiating from a position of zero strength.

“Ten minutes. Eight-ten, and I swear I rise voluntarily.”

“Not one minute. Eun-jung just texted again — they’ve already left for the building.” He dropped the blanket on a chair, out of her arc of influence.

The dragon and Eun-jung had both seen the new company already; no novelty there. But So-yeon, Bo-ram, Hyo-min, and Qri hadn’t. The four of them had risen early, dressed, and set out together in a convoy of anticipation — the tour-by-hearsay from their two bandmates no substitute for the seeing. Four phones had rung one dragon-sized number, and one dragon had not answered, and the escalation path had ended, as it always did, at Song Ji-yeon’s door.

“What? So-yeon-unnie’s gone already? Why is everyone so awake— what time do they think it is… you’d never catch them this diligent before—!”

She scratched her hair into worse chaos, stretched until something audibly realigned, and capitulated.

“Fine, fine — you win, all of you. I’m up. Witness it.”

She slid off the mattress by millimeters, shuffled past him in slippers, and delivered one retaliatory pat to his arm on the way out, which stung nothing but her honor.

In the living room, Han So-hee sat with a glass of water, having heard approximately everything through a door that was never designed for acoustics.

“Is Ji-yeon-sunbaenim awake?”

“Awake. Brushing. We give her a window.” He settled into the single sofa.

Han So-hee nodded, and deployed the first practical question of her new career.

“Oppa — the profile photos. What are they for? How long does it take? Should I have brought clothes?”

“They’re your face, filed. Once a year, every artist shoots a set — it goes on the company site, your official page, the works. And the business-events team carries that file to every meeting: the photo is the cold call.”

“Business… so I get to act soon? Roles?”

He laughed before he could sand the edge off it.

“You’re getting ahead of your own debut. Right now you’re a rookie through and through — the company can put modeling work in front of you, that’s the ceiling. Directors come after the fame; even then it depends on whether any of them likes your file.”

“Ah—!”

The disappointment landed with an audible thud. The ultimate price — paid. Seen in full, kissed with interest. And it doesn’t even purchase a speaking role?

A NOTE ON THE LADDER · THE ACTING QUEUE

Count the exits before you queue. The Peninsula’s performing-arts academies graduate a small city every year; the industry has walk-on parts for a village of them. The ones who surface usually detoured first — catalog modeling, CFs, music-video faces, one-line parts in cable dramas — building a reel the way a beach builds a shoreline, one grain at a time.

A crash course buys technique, not parts. It teaches the craft of the ladder; it cannot relocate the ladder. What it can do is ensure that when a director finally does look up, the face waiting there knows what to do with a camera. The tax on impatience, in this industry, is paid in years.

He watched the arithmetic run across her face and headed it off at the pass — one fingertip, flicked lightly against her forehead.

“You think acting is easy? Look at how many academy graduates stamp out of school each spring, and count how many names you actually know by summer. If they can’t surface, what does a girl with two weeks of hotpot on her résumé expect?”

Her mouth went into a small aggrieved pout — and Song Ji-yeon’s entire worldview paused to log the event.

I had this one filed as permanently arctic. Sub-zero corridor, glacier desk manner, the works. The mouth does that?

So the ice statue had an aegyo setting. The discovery of the decade, as far as he was concerned, and his hand was moving before the review committee could convene — thumb and finger closing gently on the pouted mouth, one light squeeze of the cheeks like a man testing a peach at a market stall.

Gulp.

The statue went rigid.

This is— we are in her home. I’ve committed, fine, I committed last night — but here? If that woman walks out of that hallway right now, what is the sentence I use?

Han So-hee broke backward, dropped onto the sofa, and — priorities of a professional — whipped her head toward the bedroom first, confirming the hallway was still dragon-free. Only then did she exhale.

He was already laughing at her, openly.

“So brave yesterday. And look at you now — frightened of a hallway.”

The scarlet came back and colonized her ears, and she turned her face to the window and revoked his speaking rights for the foreseeable morning.

Fine by him. He settled into the single sofa and waited out the dragon’s beauty cycle like a man at a very slow train crossing.

Ham Eun-jung was already turning back toward the sound console.

“Come on — So-yeon-unnie’s calling lineup. Stretch, then positions.”

The little dragon stood alone for a moment, watching her unnie’s retreating back, and made a quiet executive decision with nobody left to veto it.

Fine. The unnie’s out. Then the dragon handles it herself — personally, around the clock, with prejudice. No newcomer walks in and walks off with the oppa. Not while the sixth floor of this company still draws breath.

Motion carried, one member voting, zero abstentions: oppa belongs to T-ara.
