Chapter Ninety-Five

A Chance Encounter with Joo-hyun and Seul-gi

ALMOST NOON Apgujeong-dong, Seoul ONGI Apgujeong — the Bamboo room, minutes later

Song Ji-yeon looked at the woman across the table — cheeks blazing, visibly negotiating with the floor for a crack to disappear into — and the last gram of displeasure in him evaporated.

What remained was the honest verdict: the whole affair was funnier than every comedy he’d watched in his last life. Combined. Rewatched.

“Apology declined.”

He leaned back, arms crossed, entirely at his leisure now.

“But — now that the misunderstanding has been formally executed — can we try this conversation again? Peacefully. Like adults. Possibly like acquaintances.”

Victoria, meanwhile, was busy constructing a Barbie Dreamhouse with her toes. Inside her shoes. At speed. This was, by every internal metric, a public execution — and she had been the executioner, the accused, and the sole witness, simultaneously.

She wasn’t a combative woman by nature. That was the thing. It was only Sulli. Sulli turned her judgment to soup — care did that, care made you skip the homework — and so she’d filed this man on vibes, and the vibes had said: who rushes out an endorsement for a product that doesn’t exist? Hence: the farce. Had she known he was a gyopo — an import, like her — she would still have worried about Sulli, heaven knew, but she would never have stormed the restaurant. As for the endorsement: she’d have handed it to her manager to vet, and if it cleared the company’s bar she’d have walked it up the chain herself. For people who’d crossed an ocean to build something on this Peninsula, she held a soft spot the size of the Pacific. And she had gone and detonated a misunderstanding directly on top of the one place the soft spot applied.

She nodded. The sound that came out was roughly mosquito-grade.

“Okay.”

“First — why I’m in Sulli’s orbit at all.” He laid it out clean and unhurried, the way you stack firewood. “Like I said: we met by pure accident. She helped me when it counted. I repaid it with investment advice. Everything after that is just two people getting used to each other. There’s no mission. There was never a mission.”

“Second — the feelings question.” He had to pause there, because his own face was doing something. “Sulli is a lovely girl. Bright past the legal limit — being her friend is like keeping a sunlamp in the house. It is genuinely, uncomplicatedly fun.”

“And as for anything beyond that — I don’t have any. On that you have my word. The word of a former con man, if you like”—the grin slipped out at the corner—“but it’s on the table all the same.”

He meant it, too. What he felt for the little peach was the little-sister configuration — plus, yes, a walking rebate dispenser. Saying it that way was mercenary; he’d say it that way anyway; some facts were just shaped like that.

As for the future — no guarantees, and he knew better than to counterfeit any. The peach was, worryingly, maybe a little bit in love with him already. The rebate cards would keep putting them in the same room. Where two people end up after enough shared rooms is a question the gods answer with dice.

At his word, the stone Victoria had been carrying since the front door finally touched down on solid ground. That had been the fear — the whole of it. Sulli’s heart was a one-way valve: easy in, no reverse gear. Whoever taught it a lesson, the tuition would be paid in full by Sulli alone.

“I understand.” She nodded again — then lifted her head all the way, and there was nothing guarded left in her face, only apology worn openly, like a white shirt. “Song Ji-yeon-ssi. I was rash. I was presumptuous. In this industry, on this Peninsula, I’ve watched too many ugly stories unfold at close range — where Sulli is concerned, my nerves fire before my brain does. You’ve been caught in the blast radius of that. Twice now. I’m sorry. Again.”

“It’s fine. I understand.” A hand waved it off. “You were protecting your little sister. The instinct is correct. The method—” a beat, diplomatic to the last, “—was perhaps a touch special-forces.”

Her face went through another re-flare cycle.

“But actually—” he pivoted, and now the curiosity in his voice was real. “—in all of Sulli’s oppa-this and oppa-that, did the nationality never come up? Not once?”

Victoria rewound the tapes. Oppa helped me buy stock. Oppa got me a watch. Oppa took me to dinner. Worship, gratitude, joy — the entire highlight reel, on constant rotation. Basic biographical data — nationality, hometown, the small matter of an ocean — …deleted. Somewhere, somehow, that file had simply never been attached.

She looked up, somewhere between laughter and despair.

“She… appears to have been too busy reporting what you do for her. The other facts never made the broadcast.”

“That little dork.”

So extremely Sulli. The two of them looked at each other — and cracked, at last, in the same direction.

What was left of the war evaporated on the spot, and in its place settled something stranger and much warmer: the particular peace of two outsiders who’d spent a season misreading each other, and then discovered, mid-explosion, that they’d both been guarding the same loneliness.

“By the way.” She picked up the next topic herself — anything, anything but the last five minutes. “You came over alone too, didn’t you? To build all of this. …It’s not an easy country for that.”

The reasons were simple enough. Guilt, for one — she had wronged him, and guilt made people chatty. For another: the Peninsula was not, by any census, crowded with people who had arrived by ocean, and her corner of it least of all — an industry that ran on hometown networks and Seoul-born schedules, where she was forever the one translating herself. Meeting a peer who’d done the crossing as an adult, with a company and an accent to show for it — well. Her mouth had gotten ahead of her protocol.

And there was a third reason she would never have admitted: sitting across from him in silence was its own category of unbearable.

He smiled and shook his head.

“Not exactly alone. My parents moved over when the company crossed the Pacific — they run the Korean side now, out in Mapo. But I grew up in LA. USC. I came over straight out of school to run this arm of the business.”

“Wait — straight out of school? How old are you?”

“Nineteen-ninety.”

“You’re a baby.” The words were out before her manners could stop them — and since they were out anyway, she kept going. “I have stylists older than you. And you’re doing— all of this? Sulli said you ran a restaurant company. And the trading — Sulli showed me the account. The one you manage. Five years in this industry and I don’t know a single investor with numbers like that.”

With the misunderstanding dead and buried, the praise came out of her freely. Without the Sulli fog, this man simply registered as what he was: absurdly young, and absurdly accomplished.

“Family business.” Palms up — the standard self-deprecating model, half joke, half true. “I was standing in the right doorway when the elevator arrived. This ONGI is one of ours — we’re still small, still calibrating. The current plan is most of the Peninsula’s hotpot market, inside three years.”

“Then you’re genuinely impressive.” Not flattery — the flat, considered tone of somebody filing a fact. Building something real, in a country with the Peninsula’s… let us say documented feelings about outsiders — that did not happen by accident. She knew exactly how hard the wind blows here when your accent gives you away. She’d been leaning into it since she was twenty.

“Eh. Keeping the lights on.” He brushed it off, then tipped his head at her, the tone going easy again. “One more thing — since we’re both imports in this town, drop the -ssi. Every time you say Song Ji-yeon-ssi I feel like I’m being served papers. Ji-yeon. Just Ji-yeon.”

He meant that, too. The Peninsula’s seniority religion — sunbae this, hoobae that, every sentence weighed against somebody’s birth certificate — had been the single hardest culture shock of his relocation. He’d survived LA freeways; he refused to be intimidated by a phone directory. Between two people who had both crossed an ocean to end up in this room, the bowing arithmetic could be waived.

“Okay.” A beat. Then, simply: “Song Qian.”

“I know. f(x)’s leader.” The grin resurfaced. “Victoria’s the stage name, though, isn’t it. Song Qian is the real one — the one from home.”

She blinked — then had to laugh at herself, quietly, because of course he knew; the whole country had known her face for years. He’s been swimming in f(x) since before we ever met. Of course.

“If anything ever comes up — and I mean anything — you can reach me. KakaoTalk?” He was already producing his phone. The arithmetic was simple: more friends, more roads. And this one was the leader of f(x), sitting across from a man whose entertainment company was, at that very moment, a name and a borrowed office corner — but wouldn’t stay one. Somewhere down the timeline, their industries might shake hands.

“Okay.” She didn’t refuse.

Two phones came out. Two friend requests flew.

Added. Done. Victoria looked at the new name sitting in her contact list — Song Ji-yeon, SAVORY — and privately marveled at the mathematics of her own day. She had left the dorm that morning armed to break up a romance, club in hand, ducks in her sights. She had missed the ducks entirely, dropped the club on her own foot, and walked away with the male lead’s phone number.

“Right.” He stood, coat settling into place. “Misunderstanding executed, numbers exchanged. I won’t hold your day hostage any longer — company things.”

An excuse, naturally. They were precisely one conversation deep as acquaintances, and small talk at this altitude required oxygen masks. Better a clean exit.

“Mm. I should head back too.” She rose as well.

They came out of the Bamboo room together, through the hush of the pre-lunch floor, and stopped at ONGI’s door — the last neutral ground.

“Watch yourself on the way back to the dorm.”

“You too. Drive gently.”

Plain goodbyes, Korean standard issue. Then two people turned toward two different directions, and the street took them.

✦ ✦ ✦

LUNCH HOUR Apgujeong-ro, Seoul the road home to Cheongdam-dong

He dropped into the driver’s seat, fired the engine — and then didn’t move. Just sat there, one wrist draped over the wheel, replaying the last hour. The laugh finally got its audience: nobody. A grown man, alone in a parked Mercedes, cackling at his own steering wheel.

What a day. What a farce. And yet — with the great Victoria Misunderstanding formally executed and buried, his mood stood at a quarterly high.

He pulled out and did not turn toward Mapo. The company could run one afternoon without him.

Nor did he point the car at PIEN PLUS, one building over — Eun-jung wasn’t home. Her message had come in while he was still in the Bamboo room:

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Ham Eun-jung

Oppa — CCM called. All six of us, this afternoon. Kwang-soo-Sajangnim wants to start the transfer talks. I’m heading over with the members now.

Song Ji-yeon

Good. From here it’s a formality — breathe. Say less than they do. Sign nothing Min-hyun hasn’t read.

Ham Eun-jung

Ne, Sajangnim. 😏 …Come by for dinner after? I’m cooking this time. Properly. In an actual kitchen.

So — no Eun-jung at PIEN PLUS. And at the company, nothing was on fire: Kim Min-hyun was somewhere underwater in the T-ara contract files, surfacing for air every few hours; Park Seon-young was out at the stores, living the ONGI expansion one small crisis at a time. If he drove back to the office now, he’d just be furniture with a lanyard. Better use of the afternoon: home. Recover like a person.

He swung the wheel over and pointed the Mercedes at Cheongdam-dong, and the city opened up around him.

The drive was easy and the mood held. The farce with Victoria — resolved. The process: excruciating. The result: excellent. On balance, a win.

The car ran smooth, block after block, putting distance between itself and ONGI — and then, as he came up on a crossing, something at the edge of the windshield tugged at his eye. His foot found the brake on instinct, gentle, and the car sighed over to the curb.

At the roadside, two girls stood shoulder to shoulder, hunting a taxi with rising despair.

Lunch hour. Every cab in sight had its roof light off — mid-fare, one after another, a whole fleet of occupied fish. And this was 2012: the future in which your phone simply materialized a car had not yet shipped to the Peninsula. The only technology available was the raised hand, and the two of them were operating it at full power, waving at a street that refused to cooperate.

Which was the only reason he’d noticed them at all.

He rolled the window down and leaned across the seat.

“Yah — Joo-hyun! Seul-gi! Where are you two headed?”

Both girls jumped out of their skins. Two heads whipped around — then the recognition landed, and they snapped into bows so fast it was nearly choreography.

“Ji-yeon-ssi. Annyeonghaseyo.” Bae Joo-hyun, first upright, first composed: the voice cool as tap water, the manners measured to the millimeter.

Beside her, Kang Seul-gi bowed the same textbook ninety degrees and stayed in it — silent, eyes down, her whole honest face stiff with the particular terror of a trainee who has been addressed by a stranger with a company attached.

He looked them over, easy.

“What’s going on? You two look like the deadline’s escaping.”

Joo-hyun’s eyes flicked once to Seul-gi — a small, guilty glance — and she explained, low, half to the pavement.

“Ji-yeon-oppa… it’s Seul-gi. Her throat still isn’t right. I’m taking her back to the hospital for another look.”

The throat again.

He’d known it was bad since the drinks shop — the cracked whisper, the girl recommending a grapefruit soda she couldn’t drink. That had been weeks ago. Still not better?

He nodded once and skipped the diplomacy.

“Which hospital? Get in. I’ll drive you.” Lunch hour meant the cab lottery, and the lottery was rigged; the two of them could grow old at that curb.

“What— no. No, we couldn’t—” Joo-hyun’s hands came up, waving the offer away like smoke. A company CEO — an actual Sajangnim — personally driving two trainees to a hospital? The idea had weight; it bent her posture. And there was a second layer she was too polite to say out loud: of the two of them, one was the daughter of his own finance director. If a photo like that started circulating — the CEO of SAVORY, a trainee, a back seat — the story would write itself, and the story would be wrong.

“It’s no trouble.” The register that does not negotiate. “It’s on my way. In, both of you — you’re burning hospital hours standing here.”

On my way was, of course, a creative statement. Neither girl was in a position to audit it. They traded one look — an entire conversation, Glances: Intermediate — and the math resolved: refuse again, and it stops being modesty and starts being insult.

“Then… thank you, Ji-yeon-oppa. Truly.”

One more bow, precise as ever. She gathered the still-hovering Seul-gi by the sleeve, piloted her to the rear door, and the two of them folded into the back seat with the care of people boarding a boat they didn’t own.

The Mercedes pulled back into the noon current.

And the cabin went quiet. Oppressively, strategically quiet. Both girls sat in full attendance posture — spine vertical, knees together, hands stacked on their laps like items surrendered at customs — breathing at reduced volume, as though the oxygen in the car were billed monthly.

He checked the mirror. Honestly. Was he that frightening?

He could guess at the mechanics of it, though. To two trainees with no debut date and no public names, a Sajangnim was less a person than a physics event: the title had mass, and things orbited it. He’d watched new hires orbit his father the same way at the LA restaurants — kids from three countries, all fluent in one shared language: silence in front of the owner. Some laws survived every ocean.

Driving, he let his thoughts drift — because there was something genuinely odd sitting in his back seat.

Truthfully, he knew next to nothing about the girl group these two were training toward. In his last life, 2014 had delivered his diploma and then, immediately, the job market — and the job market had taken him whole. Work had eaten the years, and the Peninsula’s new generations of girl groups had scrolled past unwatched. In the museum of his youth, Girls’ Generation and T-ara still held the permanent galleries. Everything after was a blur of thumbnails.

One face had survived the blur. The one in his back seat.

No help for it — the face was simply too famous to filter. Even a casual fan lived downstream of it: it arrived in every feed, every year-end montage, every top-visual ranking the future could generate — the face the industry itself had crowned, the fourth generation’s number one visual, no serious dissent on file. His first proper look at it, in another life, had produced the standard reaction: jaw, meet floor. Which was why the ID had cost him zero seconds. Bae Joo-hyun was a fixed landmark in any timeline.

Kang Seul-gi, by contrast — subtract Director Kang from the equation, and he might never have filed her at all. His complete archive on her consisted of a single entry: her position in the group, last time around. Main dancer. The ACE.

Which was exactly what snagged in his head now.

A dancer. Dancers break at the waist, the knees, the ankles — the warranty issues are mechanical, structural, load-bearing. Who has ever heard of a dancer whose recurring injury is the throat? Had the heavens opened her character sheet, taken one look, and misallocated every skill point?

Carrying that question, he reached up and broke the silence himself.

“Seul-gi-ya.” Gentle, through the mirror. “The throat — still the nodules from before?”

The named girl went visibly rigid, one vertebra at a time. Her eyes darted up, met his in the glass — one second of direct eye contact, borrowed at high interest — and then she dropped her head like a hamster who has heard the cat, and gave her full attention to her own knees.

Beside her, Joo-hyun took the question on her behalf.

“Yes, Ji-yeon-oppa. The doctor says it isn’t resolving on its own — he wants a full workup this time.” The worry ran under the cool voice like a current under ice. “And if it doesn’t improve… the next option is surgery. Only—” Her hands tightened around each other in her lap. “Surgery changes the voice. The high notes would be gone — permanently. And Seul-gi’s dream… Seul-gi wants to be a main vocalist. So she won’t hear of the surgery. She’d rather keep whispering.”

Ah. So that was the shape of it.

The girl’s real weapon was never the dancing — it was the voice. The heavens had, in fact, allocated her skill points exactly right, and then issued her a throat with a manufacturing defect. And going by the last life — where she’d taken the stage as a dancer — the voice had never come back on its own.

Then again… she had debuted. In the timeline he remembered, whatever the throat decided, the girl had gotten there in the end.

The advice assembled itself on his tongue, ready to go — have the surgery, kid. Have it now, today. A voice you can trust beats a voice you can only hope for — and then he examined it, and swallowed it back down. Who could certify the future? Not him; he merely lived in its shadow. And to these girls — girls who ran laps of a practice room at midnight for a maybe — debut was not a goal. It was the only light in the sky. A stranger’s forecast of their own futures weighed nothing against it. Nothing he said here would be believed, and he had a personal rule about free advice: don’t give it.

So he said nothing more.

For a while, the three of them rode in silence — the man in front driving, a half-step gentler than before, and the two girls in the back, one guarding her throat and one guarding her friend, each carrying a different future in her lap. Outside the glass, lunchtime Seoul went on being Seoul: buses, bridges, the river flashing silver between buildings, the whole city in motion at once.

The Mercedes carried them toward the hospital through all of it, quiet as a held breath.
