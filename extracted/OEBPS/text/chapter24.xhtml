Chapter Twenty-Four

The Stock Forecast Card

SUNDAY, SEPTEMBER 9 · JUST PAST MIDNIGHT Gimpo International Airport the underground parking lot

Seoul. Gimpo International Airport.

The underground lot at that hour was hollow and cold, lit by a few yellowed vapor-proof lamps that dragged the group’s shadows out long across the concrete. The van’s engine was already running, exhaust pooling white in the chill — clotting, unraveling, clotting again.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi — truly, thank you. Flying all the way out to Jeju to hold a light up for us.”

So-yeon stood beside the van in a thick padded coat, giving him a small, correct bow. She was running on the last teaspoon of her strength, and the leader’s etiquette did not dip with the fuel gauge.

“You’re too kind, So-yeon-ssi.”

One hand in his trouser pocket, the other holding the black backpack, he gave the six of them a small smile.

They had shed every last trace of the stage. Baggy street clothes now, caps and masks, shoulders sagging under their own weight. Park Ji-yeon had gone one step further and hung herself bodily over Hyo-min’s back, eyes shut, mumbling something with no consonants in it — a koala at zero percent battery.

“We’ll head back to the dorm, then. Drive safely, Song Ji-yeon-ssi.”

Qri made her courteous farewell, tugged her mask straight, and shepherded herself and the others in through the sliding door at the speed of deep exhaustion.

Eun-jung came last.

She stopped by the open door and turned back to him. The mask covered everything below her eyes — and her eyes were bright, catching the lot’s flat white light, giving the whole tired night one last flicker of wattage.

“So…”

She was picking at her nail polish, circling the sentence before committing to it.

“Hm?”

He raised an eyebrow and waited, entirely at his leisure.

“What you said on the plane. No take-backs.”

Her voice was muffled inside the mask — soft, round, with a filament of aegyo pout threaded through it that she would have denied under oath.

“What did I say?”

He grinned the grin of a man who knew precisely what he had said.

Her temper went up like a match head. She took one step in close and dropped her voice to a furious whisper.

“Yah! The cooking! You said once we were back in Seoul you’d cook for me yourself! I already wrote it down. In my little notebook!”

“Ohhh. That.”

He let the vowel stretch out nice and long, watching her feathers rise, and finally laughed.

“Relax. In business, my word is the one asset I’ve never had to write down. Whenever you’re free — one Kakao message, and the stove goes on.”

With a satisfactory answer secured, she let out a breath and rewarded him with a proud little hmph.

“That’s more like it. I’m off, then. I’ll message you when I’m home.”

“Ne. Get in — it’s warm in there.”

He waved her in. She climbed up and disappeared, and the door ran shut along its track with a rattle, sealing the noise and the warmth in and him out. The van rolled out of its slot and away toward Gangnam.

He stood there and watched the red taillights go, all the way to the corner of the ramp, until they were gone — then turned and walked toward his own black Mercedes, parked off in the corner.

Key in. Engine on. The heater roared to life and finally began to chip away at the night’s fatigue.

Gimpo to the Cheongdam-dong apartment was a clear run, and the 1 AM streets of Seoul were quiet to the point of the uncanny — a few night-shift taxis streaking through the lamplight, and nothing else moving.

He walked in his door at one o’clock exactly.

Jacket onto the hook in the entryway, shoes abandoned at a careless angle, straight into the bathroom. Hot water came down out of the showerhead in sheets; steam grayed over the half-length mirror inside a minute. He stood under it with his eyes closed and let the heat work its way into everything the day had ruined, feeling every pore loosen one by one.

Out of the shower: grey cotton sleep pants, towel over damp hair, and he tipped backward into the big soft bed and sank — sank well in — and let out a long, satisfied sigh from somewhere around his ankles.

In his mind’s eye, the gold-edged Goddess Handbook lay glowing its faint, patient glow.

A thought, and the attribute panel unfolded. Beneath Eun-jung’s exquisite portrait card, an item card lay quietly shimmering — purple light running around its borders like slow liquid.

Goddess Handbook — Item

ItemStock Forecast Card (collected)

EffectFor one designated stock — the maximum gain range within the coming week, and the optimal point to sell

Status: stored in handbook space · unactivated

He studied the card a while. His finger sketched two idle arcs in the empty air.

He did not activate it.

“Using it now would be a waste.”

He shook his head, the corner of his mouth bending into a wry curve.

Private Trading Log · Sunday, September 9 · 1:00 AM

PositionSamsung Electronics (KRX: 005930) · 2× leveraged ETF

Capital deployed₩8,000,000,000 — locked mid-trend

Free capitalEffectively nil

Stock Forecast CardBanked — unusable until this leg closes

Every won he could move was already buried in Samsung Electronics — 2× leverage on top of it — a full ₩8 billion of it sitting in the trench and refusing to budge. Until this Samsung run finished paying out, he had nothing spare to aim at another stock, forecast or no forecast.

“Fine. Banked, then. There’s no expiry date on money waiting to be made.”

He slid the card back into the handbook’s space and folded the panel away.

Outside the window, Cheongdam-dong’s night lay as deep as ever.

The Jeju round trip had scraped his tank to the bare metal; his eyelids had acquired lead linings. He dragged the duvet over himself, and inside two minutes he was gone.

SUNDAY, SEPTEMBER 9 · 10:21 AM Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment

When he opened his eyes, the room was paved in sunlight, wall to wall.

He reached over and squinted at his phone. 10:21.

“Tsk. Slept like the dead.”

He rubbed the stiffness out of his neck and sat up.

Washed, brushed, and parked in front of the wide floor-to-ceiling windows, he looked out at Seoul sunning itself below.

Sunday.

Which meant that tomorrow, Monday, it was back to the SAVORY F&B offices and the avalanche of paperwork that had piled up while he was off being a fan, plus rounds of the stores, still finding their rhythm in their first month. Which meant, in practical terms: his free time consisted of today, and today only.

“So. What does one do with a Sunday?”

He rubbed his chin — and a small face surfaced in his memory. Exquisite. And faintly, unfairly aggrieved.

Sulli.

That silly Peach had been out on a drama set for weeks now. At the barbecue dinner she had made him swear — hand raised, absolutely solemn — that he would come visit the shoot. And then ONGI had happened, and Jeju had happened, and the entire matter had fallen out of his head without leaving a forwarding address.

Now that it came back to him, it came back with a small, nagging weight of guilt attached.

He picked up the phone, found her number, and called.

Two rings, and the line snapped open — and the first thing through it, behind her voice, was set noise: an assistant director barking through a megaphone, footsteps, wind.

Outgoing call · Song Ji-yeon → Choi Sulli

“Song Ji-yeon oppa!”

The surprise came through the speaker with its shoes off. She was delighted — instantly, audibly, all the way down the line.

“I’m not interrupting the shoot, am I?”

He was smiling already, walking to the fridge for a carton of milk.

“No, no! I just wrapped a scene — it’s break time right now!”

Her voice was crisp and running at its usual double speed.

“Oppa, what made you call me today? Is it hotpot? Are you treating me to hotpot?”

“No hotpot today.”

He drank his milk against the kitchen counter.

“As it happens, I have a free day. I was thinking of coming to visit your set. Unless Sulli doesn’t want me there?”

“DAEBAK!”

The shriek cracked cleanly through the receiver, followed immediately by the sound of fast footsteps — a girl sprinting for somewhere quieter, phone first.

“Oppa’s coming to the set?! Chincha? Promise! No lying!”

“No lying. Send me the address — I’m leaving now.”

“NE! We’re shooting exteriors at the temporary set in Yeouido — the Han River Park side! I’ll send my assistant to wait for you at the convenience store by the park entrance — just call him when you get there!”

Directions at fan-letter speed, then a few more sticky instructions — dress for the wind, text when you’re close, don’t get lost — delivered in the tone of a small migrating bird that had imprinted on him, before she finally, reluctantly, let him go.

Listening to the busy tone where her voice had been, he shook his head.

This little Peach. Exactly as bouncy as advertised.

He sank the empty milk carton in the kitchen bin and went to the walk-in closet to re-arm.

The sun was good today, but September had started smuggling a chill into the Seoul wind. He went with an off-white waffle-knit hoodie, dark grey casual slacks, and a pair of clean white sneakers — the whole effect crisp, collegiate, harmless. A man who had recently escaped from a university campus and was adjusting well to life outside.

Mercedes keys off the hook. Out the door.

The car rolled up out of the Cheongdam-dong underground garage and merged into the Sunday traffic.

He was partway down the road when his hand paused on the wheel, gaze snagging on a shop at the curb — one of those trendy new drinks places, with a line out the door and down the block.

Showing up to a set visit empty-handed was, by the Peninsula’s entertainment arithmetic, a violation of protocol. The industry ran on precisely this species of face-work. And besides — that giant silly Peach talked a devoted game about watching her figure, but her actual religion was sweets, and anything poured over ice.

“Drinks, then.”

He checked the mirror, rolled the wheel over, and slotted the Mercedes neatly into a temporary spot at the curb.
