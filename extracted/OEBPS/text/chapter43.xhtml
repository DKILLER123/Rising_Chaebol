Chapter Forty-Three

Mushroom the Dog

THIRTY MINUTES LATER Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment — the doorbell

Half an hour later, to the minute, the doorbell rang.

Song Ji-yeon had only just finished washing up. He came to the door in a loose gray hoodie, hair in a state of half-negotiated truce with a comb, and opened it.

On the landing stood Ham Eun-jung, holding a ball of white fluff.

Today she was in a cream knit sweater and light jeans, hair simply tied back, a few loose strands drifting across her forehead — and the effect was entirely different from the Eun-jung on a stage. Softer. A degree warmer. An ordinary, very pretty girl holding her dog.

The dog in question was swaddled in a small pale-blue blanket, its fluffy head poking out over the hem. Two round, black-marble eyes were fixed on Song Ji-yeon with total, unblinking attention.

Song Ji-yeon looked down at it.

Two seconds passed.

Mushroom spoke first. “Woof.”

Song Ji-yeon nodded at him, courteous. “Hello.”

Eun-jung blinked — and then the laugh broke out of her before she could stop it, eyes bending into crescents.

“He can’t understand you.”

“That’s fine — sincerity is universal. Besides, how do you know he can’t?”

He stepped aside to let her in.

Eun-jung carried Mushroom over the threshold and bent to change her shoes — an operation involving one hand cradling the dog and the other steadying her against the shoe cabinet, which went about as smoothly as it sounds. Song Ji-yeon reached out a hand to help; she ducked back from it, suddenly shy.

Then she registered that the maneuver genuinely did require three hands, and sheepishly held the dog out to him instead.

“He gets a little nervous in a new place.”

Song Ji-yeon took Mushroom into his arms. The little body was soft all over and warm as a fresh hot-water bottle. The dog gave his cuff one thorough sniff — then rested its chin on his forearm, wholly at ease. If there had ever been any nerves, they had already filed out of the building.

Eun-jung stared at this, lips parting slightly.

“…Strange.”

“Hm?”

“He almost never warms up to a stranger this fast.” She looked from the dog to the man, openly marveling. “What did you do to him? Cast a spell?”

Song Ji-yeon looked down at Mushroom, and tapped him once, gently, on the nose.

“It means he has excellent taste.”

Mushroom blinked. “Woof.”

“See? He seconds the motion.”

Eun-jung laughed, shook her head, and gave up on him, walking into the living room with the armload of bags she’d brought and setting them down beside the sofa.

“This is his food. These are his snacks — but two treats a day, no more. This is the blanket he sleeps with; it should go next to his bed at night. And this little ball — he loves this ball.”

She squatted as she enumerated, laying each item out in a neat row on the floor: food, treats, blanket, ball. The procedure was meticulous. It had the gravity of a classified document handover.

Song Ji-yeon stood by with Mushroom in his arms, watching her be serious to the point of nerves about a two-day dogsitting arrangement, and something in him began to shake quietly with held-in laughter.

“Eun-jung-ah.”

“Mm?”

“You look like a mom dropping her kid off at kindergarten.”

The dog-food bag stopped mid-air. The tips of her ears went pink, then red, in one smooth wave.

She jerked her head up and glared at him — why does this man tease me every single chance he gets —

“Yah, Song Ji-yeon!”

He set Mushroom down on the floor and raised both hands in surrender.

“My mistake.”

Aigoo. Thinnest skin in the entire industry. One joke and the whole face files a complaint.

Though really, thinking about it — Eun-jung was all charisma and stage-cool on camera, and off it the gap was a canyon. In the interviews he remembered, the ones doing all the talking for T-ara had been So-yeon and Eun-jung, every single time. It had never occurred to him before what that actually meant: the two honest ones, drafted against their will, sent out to the microphone because nobody else would go.

The moment Mushroom’s paws hit the floor, he went off like a wound-up toy. One circuit of the sofa legs. A lap of the kitchen doorway. Then he trotted back with his little ball in his mouth and deposited it, with a soft tock, at Song Ji-yeon’s feet.

Song Ji-yeon looked down.

Mushroom looked up.

Man and dog regarded each other a second time.

“What’s he doing?” He’d never kept a dog; the gesture was a cipher to him.

Eun-jung came over, bent down, and stroked Mushroom’s head.

“He wants you to play with him.”

“He’s decided I’m family already? That was fast.”

He bent, picked up the ball, and lobbed it toward the far end of the living room.

Mushroom exploded after it. Four short legs drumming the floorboards — tap-tap-tap-tap — the whole way to the window. He snatched the ball up, spun, and came rocketing back with his tail wagging so hard it threatened to achieve liftoff.

Song Ji-yeon took the ball and threw it again.

Eun-jung leaned against the sofa and watched, the last of her tension dissolving, until what was left in her eyes was mostly smile.

“He really does seem to like you.”

“Naturally.” He tipped his chin up. “Small animals have always liked me.”

“Did you have a dog growing up?”

“No.”

“Then how do you know?”

“Instinct.”

Eun-jung looked at him for a long moment.

Then she delivered a small, skeptical hmph.

He turned his head.

“You don’t believe me.”

“No.”

“Then what do you call Mushroom?”

Eun-jung glanced down at the dog, who was orbiting Song Ji-yeon’s ankles in tight, joyful circles. She was quiet for exactly two seconds, and then held her ground anyway.

“He’s young. Young things are easy to fool.”

“So now I’m a con artist.”

“More or less.”

He had to laugh at that, and after a beat, so did she. Then she seemed to remember she was here on business, and dug a folded sheet out of her bag, dense with handwriting, and held it out.

“His routine — I wrote it all down.”

Song Ji-yeon took it and read.

Eun-jung’s instructions — for Mushroom

Breakfast at 7:30. Walk around 5 in the afternoon. Ten minutes of play before bed. NO chocolate. NO grapes. Hates baths. Hates loud noises.

He went down the list line by line, and by the end of it had arrived at a quiet, humbling conclusion: this dog lives better than I do.

“Is this raising a dog or raising a son?”

“Mushroom is my little brother.” She missed the joke entirely and answered with complete sincerity.

He nodded, beaten, and accepted the family register as amended. Then he carried the note to the refrigerator, taped it to the door, and pressed it flat under a magnet until not one corner dared curl.

“What time is your flight?”

“One o’clock.”

“Then you should probably get moving.” He checked his phone — 10:06 a.m.

Eun-jung looked down at Mushroom, who was trotting to and fro with his ball as though he’d lived here for a year — and her feet didn’t move.

She pressed her lips together, then called, softly: “Mushroom.”

The dog dropped the ball instantly and came running. She crouched and gathered him into her arms, one palm smoothing down his back, over and over, very gently.

“Be good, okay? Don’t make trouble for Song Ji-yeon-ssi.”

Mushroom whimpered twice and put out his tongue to lick the back of her hand. Her eyes went soft on the spot, and she lowered her face until her nose was resting against the top of his head.

Song Ji-yeon stood to one side, watching this farewell ceremony, and privately observed that his very nice apartment had become a temporary daycare. Young mother drops the child off; the designated guardian takes over; the vibe of the place tilts somewhere irreversibly strange.

He leaned down and scooped Mushroom up.

“Don’t worry. I’ll send him back to you nice and round.”

Eun-jung’s head came up at once, alarmed.

“No random feeding! He’s barely a year old — nothing weird, nothing off your plate.”

“Ouch.” He pressed his free hand to his chest, mortally wounded. “So little faith in me. Did you forget who cooked you that crayfish dinner? And there I was, thinking ‘delicious’ meant delicious.”

She broke — the laugh escaped, and with it the last of the alarm.

“Fine, fine. Chef Song. Mushroom is in your hands.”

She went to the door and stepped into her shoes — and then, at the threshold, looked back one more time.

Mushroom was in Song Ji-yeon’s arms, chin resting on his forearm, no longer fussing. Just those round eyes, following her steadily.

“I’m off.”

“Safe trip.”

“Walk him tonight — remember.”

“Remembered.”

“And keep the leash on. He jumps at people.”

“Got it.”

“If he cries at night, don’t scold him—”

“Eun-jung.”

“Mm?”

“Keep going at this rate and you’ll miss the flight.”

She puffed her cheeks out. When she spoke again it was half a size quieter.

“I just don’t want you messing it up.”

He shifted Mushroom into one arm and raised the other hand to her, palm out.

“I’ve got him.”

He paused there — and looked her in the eye, and his voice had none of the teasing in it anymore.

Eun-jung stood in the doorway looking back at him, fingers resting on the handle, for several seconds.

At last she gave one small nod.

“Ne.”

✦ ✦ ✦

The door clicked shut, and the apartment went quiet.

Mushroom wriggled out of his arms, trotted over to the door, sat down, and stared at it for a long, long time.

Song Ji-yeon crouched beside him and rubbed his ears.

“No use staring, buddy. She’s back in two days.”

Mushroom tilted his head at him.

He picked up the little ball and waggled it.

“Come on, then. Playtime first.”

One man and one dog wrecked the living room in shifts for a good long while after that.

It was only when Mushroom finally ran out of battery and flopped onto the cushion Eun-jung had brought that Song Ji-yeon remembered it was Wednesday, and that he technically owed the office his presence. He considered the matter for a moment — concluded that the company contained nothing whatsoever that needed him in person — took out his phone, and called Park Seon-young: keep scouting storefronts in Seoul, all districts, report anything promising. As for the Sajangnim himself, he would not be coming in today.

There was no help for it. Being the boss meant being allowed to be exactly this irresponsible.

After the call he fooled with the dog a while longer, and then went to sort out lunch — party of one man, party of one dog. Eun-jung’s kibble, on inspection, struck him as nutritionally pathetic, and so a new policy was adopted on the spot: whatever Song Ji-yeon ate, Mushroom ate.

To be specific — Mushroom ate what Song Ji-yeon had left over.

That was exactly how the dogs had always been fed at the family restaurants back in LA, and every one of those dogs had grown up big, glossy, and strong.

Mushroom, denied a vote on the new policy: …I would honestly rather have the kibble.
