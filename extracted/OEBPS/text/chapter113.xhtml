Chapter One Hundred Thirteen

House Hunting and a Chance Encounter

WEDNESDAY · MIDDAY ShiningYouth Entertainment — all floors A Sajangnim on foot, auditing his own kingdom

Out of the practice room, Song Ji-yeon did not head straight back to the fourth floor. He wandered instead — a landlord counting doorframes.

SY Entertainment ran eight departments: Artist Management, PR & Publicity, Music Production, Commercial & Live Events, New Media & Fan Operations, Finance, Legal, and Overseas Distribution.

INTERNAL · SHININGYOUTH ENTERTAINMENT

Headquarters Operating Board — Floors & Functions

Four stories · sixteen rooms per floor · eight departments · October 2012

1F — Lobby and reception; Legal (three attorneys); Finance (four accountants and change). The quiet floor. Money and law both prefer low ceilings and no witnesses.

2F — PR & Publicity; Music Production; Commercial & Live Events; New Media & Fan Operations; Overseas Distribution — five departments, thirty-odd heads, every keyboard and every phone line lit at once.

3F — Practice wing. Four double studios, load-bearing walls removed by executive order; T-ara in residence, right-rear room, sunrise side.

4F — Artist Management — the manager teams, the Sajangnim’s office, and the company’s entire acting roster: one person, hired Tuesday.

The ground floor wore its quiet openly: two partitioned offices beside the lobby, one housing three lawyers, the other four-and-a-half accountants, both looking like the waiting room of a very honest clinic.

The second floor compensated. Five departments shoehorned onto one level — each a skeleton crew, a few bodies here and there, thirty people all told — but the sound came off it like a trading floor: keyboards in chords, phones lit like runways.

Since buying T-ara out of CCM, he had put the company’s first standing order on the internet itself: bring down the noise. The assignment had landed on these fresh hires, and PR & Fan Operations were, without contest, the two busiest rooms in the building. The method was nothing clever — formal requests through channels, platform by platform, for the takedown of the fabricated T-ara material. The results, these past days, had started to show.

The noise was down; the reputation had not yet followed it down any path that led back up. He knew the real ledger: until the troll incident itself was settled in public, the wound stayed open — and settling it was a campaign, not a memo. Not this quarter, then. His own sequence had the group’s overseas breakthrough first, the reckoning after; patience was cheaper than timing it wrong.

He did one lap of the second floor, absorbing the greetings of employees who bowed like they were being graded on it, took the stairs up, and pushed open his office door.

Han So-hee sat on the sofa, tidily, hands in her lap — delivered and waiting.

The profile shoot was already done: she and Lee Hye-jin had gone out to a photography studio, the company not yet owning so much as a lightbox. He hadn’t expected her back inside the hour.

“Photos done already?”

She nodded — and permitted herself one visible gram of pride.

“Ne. Hye-jin-unnie said there isn’t a bad angle of me to find, so it went quickly.”

He accepted the report at face value, because it was, in the technical sense, true. Some photography is art; most photography is logistics; and the variable with the highest success rate is the face in front of the lens. With raw material like hers, the shooter’s job is mostly to stay out of the way. She had, in fact, earned her gram.

“Then let’s go see apartments. You can’t sleep in Park Ji-yeon’s spare room forever — the dragon’s territorial.”

The morning had established the hostility beyond appeal; leaving the two of them under one roof a minute longer than necessary was scheduling a war. Housing first, peace after.

WEDNESDAY AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong — the realtor’s desk, then the streets Three luxury flats, and a client impossible to please

They drove to the realty office, where he asked for the one agent he knew by face — the man who had sold him his own apartment, and who had once, as a tip of goodwill, pointed a young actress named Park Ji-yeon at the unit upstairs. Old customers, older habits.

The agent all but stood at attention — and delivered. Cheongdam’s nicer blocks, one after another, three showings deep.

Han So-hee rejected all three.

Too far from the station. Too much street noise. A tree blocked the view. The reasons arrived pre-dressed and did not survive inspection: he could read the actual line item running underneath all three rent — with Cheongdam pricing attached.

Which amused him no end, privately. Extraordinary. Committed to the sponsorship arrangement — took the leap, filed the paperwork, even paid the opening installment in person — and now she’s out here protecting my wallet from my own lease. That is not in any gold-digger’s user manual I’ve read.

Her actual arithmetic ran plainer than that. One person did not need a three-bedroom flat — every unit shown was a three-bedroom flat. And whatever the rent was, it would be large, and not-hers-to-pay did not translate to not-hers-to-feel.

His money is still money. Whoever’s name is on the transfer, none of it arrived by wind. Waste is waste.

He reached over and dismantled the whole concern in one sentence.

“Next listing, and stop auditing the rent. The company’s paying.”

Agencies housed their people as a matter of course — trainees stacked four to a dorm, rookies in shared flats, everyone in sight of a manager. SY had exactly one artist to house, so there was no stack to join; he had simply been showing her the units he’d be embarrassed not to. If she wanted smaller, smaller she would get.

Han So-hee pressed her lips together and said nothing — while her whole face continued to file the objection in triplicate.

The realtor, a professional in the science of faces, had completed the same reading an hour earlier. He smiled the smile of a man about to save everyone’s afternoon.

“Well now. We’ve seen what Cheongdam has for standing-room — how about a change of air? Gaepo, or over toward Suseo — the single-professionals’ towers. Quiet, private, built precisely for one person living cleanly. Shall we run out and look?”

Song Ji-yeon, facing the one expenditure philosophy in the city more stubborn than his own, capitulated with grace.

“Sure. Let’s look.”

Han So-hee’s eyes came up, bright by one full degree, and she nodded along.

My standards have been right-sized by the market: one clean bed, a door that locks, and silence that doesn’t bill hourly. In this economy, that’s a palace.

WEDNESDAY · LATE AFTERNOON Gaepo-dong, Gangnam-gu A quiet street, one twenty-story tower — and the wrongest possible timing

They followed the agent’s car out along the Gaepo direction, past the busier arteries, until the streets narrowed into green and quiet, and stopped at last below a twenty-story singles’ tower. Clean landscaping, tidy hedges, none of the foot traffic of the Cheongdam blocks.

Three doors opened. Song Ji-yeon squared up at the building, approved the premises — and had taken exactly one step toward the lobby when his foot stopped on its own authority.

By the convenience store at the tower’s foot stood Kim Ji-won.

OFF-CAMERA · THE CONVENIENCE RUN “SHUT-IN, DEPLOYED”

The Queen of prime time, mid-errand — glamour suspended for maintenance, forty meters from her own front door.

Set — one baggy home-loungewear two-piece, washed to obedience, cuffs gone soft with use

Hair — tied back with the minimum viable effort; two strands escaped at the temples and formed no apology

Footwear — flat slippers, the outdoor kind, rated for one snack round trip and zero witnesses

Carry — a plastic convenience-store bag at full stretch: snacks, drinks, the pantry of a scheduled indoor weekend

Face — bare. Not “light makeup.” Bare, and at present undergoing a software crash

The screen siren at resting state: the woman who owns a building, buying instant noodles like a fugitive crossing a checkpoint

In the Peninsula’s unwritten dress code, going out unpolished is a courtesy violation — and the penalty scales with who catches you.

She was scrolling her phone one-handed, bag swinging, entirely at peace with her undercover operation.

Song Ji-yeon’s eyes narrowed by a degree. That’s… Kim Ji-won?

She looked up — some sixth sense reporting a gaze — and found his, dead center.

One full second of eye contact. Then her hand flew up on pure reflex and covered half her face, and the plastic bag almost didn’t survive the maneuver.

Her mind white-screened.

The whole day had been a retreat indoors — days of it, actually, until the walls began closing in and the stomach filed its complaint. Hence the operation: the most ordinary loungewear in the drawer, slippers, one snack run, no makeup, hair on technical minimum. In and out, forty meters, no witnesses.

And the witness had been him.

Anyone else, and this is a two-second anecdote she forgets by dinner. But it was him — and since the night he’d caught the falling light-stand, the file she kept on Song Ji-yeon had been quietly accruing interest. Good family, good face, good manners; the market had opinions and she’d heard them all.

But she was, constitutionally, a shut-in — the load-bearing kind. The mere thought of initiating, texting first, maintaining a correspondence, feeding a relationship — her whole body filed it under labor. She would rather sit alone in her apartment for a week than spend the energy on one round of you’re-welcome-no-you’re-welcome.

So: contacts exchanged at the banquet, and never once used. Her side, anyway. In her doctrine of romance, if the man doesn’t advance, the woman does not exist. She had assumed the thread had simply dissolved — that they’d never cross again, and it would be nobody’s loss, officially.

And now the universe had scheduled the collision for the one afternoon she looked like a laundromat had feelings about her.

This face. He is seeing this face. Is there a crack in this pavement large enough for a full-grown woman—

…No. Stop. Composure. She lowered the hand by administrative order — there is nothing between us; I owe this man exactly zero glamor — and looked back at him, level, deliberately.

Whereupon her eyes, entirely without authorization, slid to the woman behind his shoulder.

Young. Fair. Fine cold-cut features, a straight spine, an unexplained closeness to him on a weekday afternoon in a residential district.

Snap.

Somewhere in Kim Ji-won’s chest, one small branch of secret goodwill broke cleanly in two.

Well. There’s the answer. A woman like that, at his elbow on a Tuesday — what else would she be. The girlfriend, obviously.

Fine. Then the past weeks of quiet, embryonic, absolutely-private feelings have now been fed to the dog. Best day ever.

For the record: Kim Ji-won, shut-in, was not an unwooed woman. Suitors existed; she ignored them at industrial scale. And she was not about to start pining after a man with a girlfriend — she had standards, and a surprising quantity of pride for someone dressed like a sofa.

She cut her eyes away and prepared the exit strategy: un-see, pivot, walk.

…They were walking the same direction. Both parties, vector analysis confirmed, were headed for the tower’s front door.

No. No, this is a coincidence of geometry. Speed up — beat him to the lobby, vanish into the elevator, die privately on the sixteenth floor.

She accelerated, slippers slapping double-time—

“Ji-won?”

Her feet locked to the pavement. Her entire face resigned from her employment.

Concluded. Cancelled. Dead.

He watched her frozen back with a helplessness that was two parts sympathy and six parts comedy. Of all the towers in Gangnam — the woman lived here. Of all the afternoons — this one.

He hadn’t seen her in a while, if he was honest with himself. Not for lack of interest: the Healing Card sat on her file in the Goddess Handbook — one per week, collect in person — and he had a use waiting for it, with a name attached. Kang Seul-gi’s throat. But since the banquet she had simply evaporated; her KakaoTalk was a museum exhibit, quiet in every direction, and he had never once found a legitimate reason to poke it.

If the Handbook didn’t keep her name on an active page, he’d half believe he’d imagined the entire acquaintance.

Now that the universe had performed the introduction for free, letting her escape was not on the menu. The thread needed re-tying, and this was the free end, sticking straight up out of the pavement, wearing slippers.

Kim Ji-won rotated on her heel with the enthusiasm of a woman walking to her own audit, and manufactured something adjacent to a smile.

“Song Ji-yeon-oppa… what brings you here?”

“House hunting — for her.” He nodded at the tower behind Kim Ji-won. “New artist, new address. Didn’t expect the neighborhood to come with you attached.”

“…House hunting?” Her eyes performed one more unauthorized sweep backward. “And this person is—?”

“Han So-hee — signed to the company yesterday. Our new actress.” He flagged the introduction through. “So-hee — a friend of mine. Kim Ji-won. She’s been on your television plenty.”

Oh?

The word formed silently in Kim Ji-won’s head, and the mathematics reversed themselves at speed.

Not the girlfriend. An artist. A newly signed one — a colleague. An employee, even.

The wire that had been strung through her since the eye contact slackened, all at once, without her permission.

She stole one more look at Han So-hee anyway. Very beautiful — that was simply the data — and yes, standing beside him, the pairing photographed. But photographing well beside him and being that to him were different files entirely, and the second one had just been closed by the man himself.

Then… fine. Fine. Everything is fine.

“Ne — ne. Hello.”

“Annyeonghaseyo, sunbaenim.”

The two women exchanged the mandatory dry kilometer of politeness, and Kim Ji-won rotated back to the main channel.

“Oppa — but why Gaepo, all of a sudden?”

“The quiet. Downtown’s too loud for anyone with a schedule — and my artist needs somewhere that doesn’t come with an audience.” He rode the opening straight through. “You live here. Verdict on the building?”

She considered it — actually considered it, which was more deliberation than she had spent on any of her own life decisions that year.

“It’s good. Really good. The area’s quiet, it’s private, the station’s a manageable walk. Comfortable to exist in, easy to leave. I’d know — I exist here at industrial scale.”

“Then So-hee takes a place here, she’s your neighbor from now on.” He tipped his chin at the floors above. “Which floor are you on? The senior looks after the rookie — company policy, as of this sentence.”

“Sixteenth…?” One blink. “Ah. Looking after. The rookie. Yes.”

“Exactly. Newcomer, veteran — the veteran carries the clipboard. Isn’t that how this industry runs?”

Kim Ji-won received the whole stack of it with no visible place to set it down, laughed twice on credit, and muttered at a volume calibrated to be overheard.

“I’m barely off my own debut runway, and you’re assigning me a dependent… she should be looking after me…”

He heard it perfectly and let it live. The more she protests, the more the protest sounds like a schedule forming.

“Kidding, kidding. Look after yourself first — and anyway, So-hee hasn’t even approved the building yet.”

The sentence was technically true and strategically fraudulent. From the second he’d recognized her by the convenience store, the decision had already been made, countersigned, and filed: this building, this tower, this ZIP code — because now it came with a reason attached.

Here was the whole architecture of it. Kim Ji-won: zero posts, zero texts, zero initiated contact in the entire modern era; a woman you cannot reach without a reason, who vanishes between sightings like a stage effect. The Healing Card on her file — the one he needed within arm’s reach eventually — did not travel. It waited.

But an artist of his, resident in her building? That was a standing invitation with a company logo on it. Visiting my talent. Inspecting the company housing. Either excuse opened the lobby doors any day of the week, legally, morally, and in perfect daylight.

In Seoul, even serendipity files paperwork: he came to see an apartment, and left holding an alibi good for years.

Han So-hee, eyes lowered, had missed none of the exchange.

Yesterday the next-door neighbor. Today this one — beautiful, an actress, and radiating ownership of him in at least two directions. How many more does he keep? What is the full count of the swarm?

The confidence she’d rebuilt overnight sagged a measurable degree.

One kiss is thin collateral. I need the entire document — signed, sealed, notarized, and soon. Before the market closes.

“Oppa, that—” Kim Ji-won rallied for one more conversational run, and then registered the gaze aimed at her from the customer’s side of the transaction — Han So-hee watching her with unreadable attention — and re-budgeted the afternoon on the spot. “Since you two are here to view — I won’t hold up the showing. I’ll head up. Before the ice cream negotiates with the sun.”

“Mm. Dinner sometime — my treat.” He lifted a hand, half wave, all smile.

She nodded, hoisted the plastic bag, and set off for the lobby at speed.

Flip-flip-flip.

The slippers crossed the lobby at flank speed, the elevator swallowed one escaped actress, and only when the doors had fully closed did Song Ji-yeon reclaim his attention.

“Right. Let’s go up — the unit’s waiting.”

“Right this way, please!”

The elevator climbed to twelve. On the walk from the lobby he had, in the interests of thorough research, already extracted her floor: sixteen. Twelve to sixteen — three floors of separation.

He ran the numbers once in his head and found them satisfactory.

The twelfth-floor unit was spotless — one bedroom, one living room, one bath, modest square footage, tall windows. The light came in clean, and the view was all green.

Han So-hee stood at the window, taking in the trees, and for the first time all day filed no complaint.

“Verdict?”

“It’s good.” A pause, and then, honestly: “Better than all the others.”

“Then this is home. Favorite color? I’ll have some things sent over — curtains, a throw, something with an opinion.”

“…Don’t trouble yourself, oppa.” Quietly. “It’s one person. Simple is fine.”

“Suit yourself.” He let it go — her household was now her jurisdiction. “We’ll stop for your things on the way back, pick up bedding after, and you move in today. Officially.”

“Ne.”

He turned to the agent and opened the numbers conversation. A singles’ unit priced for singles — reasonable, even modest, by the district’s standards. And since the calendar in his head had her breakout still a few years out, he did the arithmetic the way he did all arithmetic when the future was already known: one stroke, four years of rent, prepaid.

On the company’s books.

Four years of rent, one signature, the company’s account. In this city even patience has a landlord — and he pays his deposits in advance, in full, and never mentions it twice.
