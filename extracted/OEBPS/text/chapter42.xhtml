Chapter Forty-Two

You Could Just Keep Me Instead

NEAR MIDNIGHT Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment

By the time Song Ji-yeon got back to the Cheongdam-dong apartment, it was edging up on eleven.

The elevator opened onto a silent corridor — nothing in it but the faint hum of the air conditioning and his own footsteps, which were slower tonight than usual. One hand kneaded the back of his neck; the other swung the car keys in loose circles.

From the set visit to the hotpot to delivering Sulli and Kim Ji-won back to their hotel, the day had been one errand stacked on another, and it was only now, standing at his own door, that the fatigue finally caught up and tapped him on the shoulder.

The fingerprint lock gave its soft click.

The entryway light came on, laying warm yellow across the floor. He bent to change his shoes, tossed the keys into the tray — one clean metallic ring — and hung his jacket on the stand.

He didn’t head for the shower. He headed for the kitchen first, and poured himself a glass of ice water.

The cold hit his throat and snapped a shiver down through him — and the fizz of excitement in his head, which the water was supposed to drown, only crackled louder.

Today’s plan, as written, had been modest: use Sulli to run a live test on the Tenfold Rebate Card’s trigger conditions. That was all. One experiment.

The experiment had yielded the card. On the way out, it had also yielded a rescued Kim Ji-won — and, tucked under his arm, an entire second gold mine.

“What do you even call that?”

He stood at the kitchen island talking to the glass, and laughed once, alone.

“You go out to pick up money off the sidewalk — and you come home with the key to a vault, too.”

He carried the glass into the study and lowered himself into the desk chair. When he set it down, a bead of condensation slid the length of the glass and found his fingertips, and the cold at last put a damper on things.

Spread across the desk were the new-site documents for ONGI — Cheongdam storefronts, their rent quotes circled in red, aggressive numbers penciled beside them. He gave them one glance and had no head for any of it. He tipped back against the chair, closed his eyes, and let his mind move.

The Goddess Handbook opened in the dark behind his eyelids, slow and weightless, page by page.

Ham Eun-jung. Choi Sulli. Kim Ji-won.

Three small golden cards hung side by side, each one running with its own faint light. Eun-jung’s card was warm and still, a stone left in sunlight. Sulli’s was bright and restless, all flicker. Kim Ji-won’s sat between them like a piece of jade polished a second ago — translucent, and cold at the edges.

His gaze dropped first to the space beneath Sulli’s card.

A card of deep, dark gold lay there quietly, waiting.

Goddess Handbook — Item

ItemTenfold Rebate Card

EffectApply the rebate to one legitimate purchase; receive ten times the purchase price back in cash.

LimitOne purchase only — ₩100,000,000 maximum. Rebate funds are deposited into the host’s account as fully compliant business income.

Single-use item. Vanishes on activation.

Song Ji-yeon stared at the words ₩100,000,000 maximum for several seconds. Something in his eyes went flatly, reluctantly disappointed.

“A billion back. Cap.”

He ran the arithmetic under his breath. His brow did not uncrease.

A billion won. It sounded like a lot of money — it was a lot of money. Dropped into the plans he actually had queued up, it wasn’t even a foundation. It was one stepping-stone, and a modest one.

Buying T-ara out of CCM would cost money. ONGI’s expansion would eat money. And now the Saero zero-sugar soju technology was sitting in his hands, and turning technology into cash flow meant money on a different order entirely — acquiring a mid-sized distillery, rebuilding production lines, buying equipment, building the brand, opening distribution channels, then burying the country in marketing. Not one of those line items was a problem a billion won could look in the eye.

The Korean liquor market was not a roadside tteokbokki stand. If he wanted “zero-sugar soju” to mean anything, it had to arrive as a product no consumer could ignore — from day one, at full scale.

He wasn’t after pocket change. He was after a chunk of meat torn straight out of the mouths of Jinro and Chum Churum.

“A respectable mid-sized distillery — twenty, thirty billion won minimum, just for the acquisition. Before the marketing. Before the line upgrades. Before any of the other eighty things.”

A billion won. A cup of water thrown at a house fire.

He tipped his head back against the chair and pressed two fingers into his brow.

“Money, money.” He said it to the ceiling, eyes closed, and sighed. “Short in the last life, still short in this one. A returner as broke as me — honestly, it’s an embarrassment to the whole time-traveling community.”

He picked up the glass and drank the rest of the ice water down in one go. The cold slid from throat to stomach, a shiver ran through him, and at last the stampede in his head slowed to a trot.

“Sleep first.”

He closed the Handbook, stood, and left the study. Shower, hair dryer, bed. He had genuinely planned to run through tomorrow’s schedule one more time, but the moment his eyes shut, the day’s weight landed on him all at once, and he went under without a sound.

✦ ✦ ✦

THE NEXT MORNING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s bedroom — oversleeping

Early the next morning, the phone dragged him out of a dream by the ankle.

It buzzed and skittered across the nightstand without pause. He reached for it with his eyes still closed, fingertips grazed the edge, and very nearly swept the whole thing onto the floor. Frowning, blind, he thumbed the answer button.

“…Hello?”

Voice like gravel dragged over felt.

Phone · Ham Eun-jung → Song Ji-yeon

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. Are you up?”

“I am now.”

“So — there’s something I wanted to ask you. Could you look after Mushroom for two days?”

“Mushroom?”

His brain, still not booted, obligingly summoned a picture of the mixed-mushroom platter from his own menu. He peeled his eyes open, one at a time, and studied the ceiling for two full seconds.

“You want me to grow mushrooms for two days? Eun-jung-ah, I can cook — but I haven’t gotten as far as raising crops at home.”

“YAH! Not mushrooms you EAT — the DOG! My dog! His name is Mushroom!”

He sat up, hair standing in several unauthorized directions, and scrubbed a hand down his face. That helped. Somewhat.

“Oh. A dog.”

Right — in the last life, Eun-jung really did keep a little dog. He remembered that much. When it passed, she’d written a post about it — he’d scrolled past it on some forum or other, back when none of these people knew he existed.

“Why do you sound disappointed?”

“I don’t. I just woke up. The brain is still loading.”

From the other end came a short, soft laugh — instantly, fiercely suppressed.

Then her voice dropped a register, went quieter.

“We fly out to Hong Kong this afternoon — it’s two days before we’re back. I was going to leave him with my mom, but she had to run down to Busan of all things, and the pet hotels… I don’t really trust them.”

“I went through everyone I know, and… it seems the only person I can ask is you.”

He’d already thrown the blanket off. Bare feet on the floorboards, cool underfoot; he crossed to the window and pulled the curtains open, and morning poured into the bedroom and stabbed him square in the eyes.

“Sure. Bring him over.” He had nothing scheduled, when it came down to it. Two days of dogsitting was nothing. “Really. It’s two days with a dog — it’s not as if you’re asking me to keep you.”

The line went dead silent.

Several full seconds passed. Then, muffled, as though delivered directly into a pillow:

“…Who needs you to keep me? Learn to keep yourself fed first, Song Ji-yeon.”

He leaned a shoulder against the window frame, grinning at the glass.

“Mm. Well — you could always keep me instead.”

“AISH! Song Ji-yeon!”

That one was a genuine detonation. He laughed out loud — shoulders shaking with it — and the last of the sleep burned clean off.

“Alright, alright. No more teasing. What time will you be here?”

“Half an hour? I’ll head straight out from the dorm.”

“Works. Drive safe — and come straight up.”

He stood at the window for a while after hanging up, letting the morning finish the job of waking him, and did the math on his own absurdity.

Last night, he had been a man staring at a several-hundred-billion-won hole in his financing. This morning, he was a dogsitter.

A life’s swings, sometimes, ran wilder than any candlestick chart.
