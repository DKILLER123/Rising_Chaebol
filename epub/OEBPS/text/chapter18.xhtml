Chapter Eighteen

The Marriage Campaign

FRIDAY EVENING Mapo-gu, Seoul the study, Song family home

Ji-yeon pushed the door the rest of the way and stepped in.

The study was small. A dark walnut desk stood by the window, a few documents fanned across it, a cup of tea still steaming at its corner. The wall behind held a full bookshelf, ordered with military neatness — business and management titles, and more than a few thick volumes on Korean commerce and law, their spines cracked from actual reading.

Song Min-seok sat behind the desk in gold wire-rimmed glasses, paging through a financial report.

He was in his early forties and carried it lightly — in good shape, none of the middle-age drift that collected on most men his age. The brows were the same ones that looked back at Ji-yeon from the mirror, but everything beneath them was steadier: the composure of a man who had been through years of high tide and low and had stopped flinching at either.

“Dad.” Ji-yeon took the chair across the desk.

His father looked up, took off the glasses, and let his gaze rest on his son’s face for a moment. The corner of his mouth lifted a few degrees.

“Mm. Thinner. But you look well.”

“Mom said the same thing.” Ji-yeon smiled. “My weight hasn’t moved.”

“That’s your mother. You stay away for one month, and she’ll spend all thirty days telling the furniture about it.” The report went down; he leaned back with his tea. “The company? Uncle Park came by — he said ONGI’s opening-day numbers were nothing to sneeze at.”

Since Ji-yeon had taken the chairman’s seat, his father had all but stepped out of the picture. He showed up at the office once every three or four days now, the way a landlord visits a building he trusts.

“Seven stores, ₩105 million combined revenue, net profit around ₩1.2 million per store.” The numbers came out clean, with a thread of earned confidence woven through them. “For the hotpot category, that’s a strong opening. It says the model holds. It says the positioning is right.”

His father nodded — no medal, no ceremony; ONGI had walked in the door with a customer base and locations that practically fed themselves. “A decent start. But this trade is full of restaurants that rose fast and fell faster. Anybody can open. Lasting is the skill.”

“I know.” Ji-yeon let a beat pass, then turned the card over. “Dad — once the ONGI model is fully proven and the books level out… what would you think about opening the franchise program?”

The teacup paused — just slightly — on its way back to the desk.

“Seven corporate stores is a small body, Dad. In Seoul, that doesn’t even make a splash. But franchise the model, bring in outside capital and outside hands, and we can lay down scale in months instead of years. Hotpot is the one category built for copying — high standardization, a mature supply chain, the training academy, the operations manuals. The franchisee brings the money and the real estate. Everything else, we run.”

His father didn’t answer right away. He set the teacup down gently, and his fingers tapped the rim — once, twice, three times.

“It’s good thinking.” The words came out slow. “But don’t rush it.”

“Meaning?”

“How long has ONGI been open? Not a week.” The tone stayed level; the eyes didn’t. “The model runs — fine. So tell me this: what is the single biggest difference between a franchise and a store you own yourself?”

Ji-yeon considered. “Control.”

“Exactly.” A small nod. “Your own store — sourcing, kitchen, service — every link of the chain answers to you. A franchisee answers to his own wallet. He put in the money and the lease; what he wants back is return. The day your standards and his margins want two different things, what, exactly, is going to make him listen to you?”

Ji-yeon said nothing.

“I’m not against franchising.” His father’s voice stayed even. “Chains are where this industry ends up, and franchising is how you scale. But only on top of a foundation that can carry the weight.”

He stood, walked to the window, and turned his back to the room, looking down at the small courtyard.

“Your seven stores are still settling in. The supply chain needs another season to prove it doesn’t wobble. The new hires need time to become your people instead of strangers in your uniforms. And the kitchen — consistency in the pot, every pot, every store. Open franchising now, and you’re photocopying a recipe you haven’t finished writing. When the first franchise fails — and one will — it won’t fail alone. It takes the ONGI signboard down with it. And the signboard, Ji-yeon-ah, is the one thing in this business you cannot buy back.”

Ji-yeon listened all the way through without interrupting.

His father turned back from the window. “Slower steps. Make these seven truly profitable first. Blanket the prime blocks of every district in Seoul — all of them — then push outward from Seoul until the whole Peninsula is within reach. Once that foundation is under your feet, franchising will still be there. It isn’t going anywhere.”

“Understood, Dad.” And he did understand.

In truth, he wasn’t in a hurry either. SAVORY F&B was still a company with seven stores to its name — the pot was simply too small. Seven successful openings would never quiet a serious franchisee’s doubts; only scale attracts scale. He knew the old proverb as well as anyone — if you’re in a hurry, take the long way around — and he’d brought the plan to his father precisely so a man with decades of scar tissue could run his fingers over it and find the cracks. Sure enough, the old man’s thinking matched his own to the letter.

Because the road was already mapped: once the seven stores steadied, take the cash and open new corporate stores across Seoul’s other districts — the university neighborhoods first, where the young money and the young appetites lived.

“Good, then.” His father came back to the desk and sat, and had a sip of tea that had long since gone past warm. “Fire in a young man is a fine thing. But business is not the hundred-meter dash — it’s a marathon. The man laughing at the finish line was never the fastest runner on the course. He was the steadiest.”

“I know.” Privately, he agreed with every word.

“Enough shop.” A wave of the hand. “You’re home. Go sit with your mother — she’s been on about you all week.”

He had barely opened his mouth when the door did it for him.

“All right, all right — that’s quite enough, you two!” Kang Seol-ah’s voice arrived before she did. The door swung the rest of the way open, and she stood planted in it, hands on hips, the very portrait of grievance. “The food is on the table going cold while the men of this house hold a summit. Whatever it is, you can say it over dinner!”

His father gave Ji-yeon a long-suffering glance. Ji-yeon had no rebuttal to offer — you’re the one who sent me in to see Dad, madam — but she was his mother, and the appeals process was closed.

The two men traded a smile and filed out of the study.

The dining table was already crowded: tangsuyuk fried crisp and lacquered in its sweet-and-sour glaze, a whole steamed sea bass fanned with scallions, soy-braised beef brisket dark and glossy, garlic broccoli still bright, a bubbling pot of samgyetang at the center, and a whole constellation of little side dishes ringed around it all.

The smell alone was worth the drive across the river. His appetite stood up and saluted.

“Whoa. Mom. All of this?”

“You come home once a month. Once! What did you expect — a cup of ramyeon?” She was already loading his rice bowl like it was her sworn mission. “It’s all your favorites. Sit. Eat while it’s hot.”

The three of them settled in under the warm lamplight, and the room did what warm lamplit rooms do.

His father picked up a piece of the tangsuyuk, chewed twice, nodded once.

“Mm. The pork’s good today.”

“Of course it is. I made it the way you like it.” A proud little laugh — and then, mid-laugh, without a single break in rhythm, the biggest piece in the dish landed on top of Ji-yeon’s rice. “Eat. Look at you. Skin and bones.”

“Mom, I can feed myself—”

“Feed yourself? Left to your own devices, you’d shovel plain rice and call it a meal.” An eye-roll, delivered while her chopsticks never stopped moving. Within a minute, his bowl had become a small mountain with a summit of braised brisket.

He surrendered and applied himself to the mountain.

It had to be said: the woman could cook. The tangsuyuk shattered at the edges and melted inside, the vinegar cutting clean through the sweetness. The sea bass came off the bone at a look, the scallion perfume soaked all the way into the flesh. And the samgyetang was exactly right — the chicken so tender it was practically surrendering, the broth faint with ginseng, sweet underneath with jujube and garlic.

The talk meandered the way family talk does — the company, the weather, a cousin’s kid — until somewhere between the fish and the soup, his mother set down her chopsticks, changed gears, and changed weather.

“By the way. Ji-yeon-ah.” Her voice had gone soft and meaningful in a way that made the fine hair on the back of his neck stand up and salute. “You’ve finished school now. Isn’t it about time you thought about… your personal life?”

His chopsticks stopped in midair. Here it comes. He had known there would be no slipping past this checkpoint.

“Mom, I only just got to Korea. The business isn’t even on its feet yet.” Sidestep. Evade. Regroup.

“A business you can build at fifty. A wife, you cannot.” She had clearly prepared remarks. “Look at you — twenty-two! After the new year, twenty-three! When your father and I were your age, we were already—”

“Mom. Times have changed.” He got in fast, before that sentence could land somewhere unrecoverable. “These days people get married at thirty and their mothers throw parties.”

“That is other people’s families!” The glare arrived on schedule. “We are not other people’s families. You will find a nice girl and date her properly — a year, a year and a half at most — and settle it. I want to hold a grandbaby while my arms are still strong enough to lift one.”

The corner of his mouth twitched. He put some rice in it.

“Say something!” A knuckle rapped the table. “Auntie Jang was over last week. Her daughter just graduated from Seoul National University — lovely girl, good head on her shoulders. Should I arrange something?”

“Mom — really, no.” Both hands came up. “I have a plan. You can leave this entire category to me.”

“A plan. What plan? Say it out loud, this plan.” Arms crossed: the posture of a woman who would be accepting no plan she had not personally audited.

He sighed, laid his chopsticks down across the bowl, and gave her the honest version. “Mom. Right now, I want to get the business standing. And the right person isn’t something a setup dinner manufactures. It happens when it happens.”

“It happens when it happens? Nothing happens to a boy who’s married to his office! Fate doesn’t deliver, Ji-yeon-ah — you have to go out and meet it halfway.” And then the kill shot, delivered with a mother’s unerring aim: “You have never once in your life been good with girls. Not once! If I don’t manage this for you, you will be single until they lower me into the ground.”

A mother’s intelligence network cuts both ways. It was true the boy had been dealt a full hand — a hundred and eighty-five centimeters of son, a face that would never stop traffic but cleaned up very well — and yet in all his years under her roof, she had never once heard so much as a rumor attaching his name to a girl’s. If she hadn’t known for a fact that her son’s heart pointed the ordinary direction, she would have started drawing conclusions of an entirely different architecture.

He had no counterargument. He nodded. “Okay. Okay. I’ll keep my eyes open.”

“That’s all I ask.” Satisfaction, immediate and total. The chopsticks came back up. “And I’ll remember that you said it. Your mother is expecting good news.”

He bowed his head to his rice, and inside his skull he was laughing a very complicated laugh.

Mom, don’t you worry about daughters-in-law. Your son is bound to the Goddess Handbook. The question isn’t whether you’re getting one. It’s whether you can handle the quantity.

Bound already: Ham Eun-jung. Sulli. Still pending: the other five of T-ara, Goo Hara, IU, Victoria… If every one of those bonds actually activated, the mental image would be so dazzling he’d have to look away from his own imagination.

No. Hold on. I’m a serious man with chaebol ambitions. Since when is this a harem storyline? He shut the whole line of thought down like a breaker. Enough. Eat.

The meal wound down to his mother’s commentary and his father’s silent smiling, the way their meals always had. Afterward he helped clear the table, and the three of them reconvened in the living room with the television on — some SBS Wednesday–Thursday drama, half-watched.

Then his mother sat up straight. “Oh — what’s that girl’s name again… Sulli, isn’t it? She’s in dramas now?”

He looked at the screen before he could stop himself. It was To the Beautiful You — a rerun — and there she was in the middle of it: Sulli in the cross-dressing lead role, delicate features and quick, bright eyes holding the frame even under a boy’s school uniform.

“That’s To the Beautiful You. It’s popular right now.” He remembered her telling him the title herself. “And her acting’s always been solid — she was on screens before she was ever on a stage. Child actress.”

“Right? Pretty, and such good manners on her.” Kang Seol-ah gazed at the screen with an auntie’s wholehearted approval. “A girl like that… if only the world worked that way. She’d make someone a lovely daughter-in-law.”

The teacup in Ji-yeon’s hand very nearly ended its career on the floor.

He looked over at his mother with what he hoped was photographic calm. But her eyes had never left the television — the pure, unclouded appreciation of a mother appraising somebody else’s excellent child. She had registered exactly nothing about her own son’s everything.

“Mom. Let’s not daydream.” He kept it light with visible effort. “She’s a star. That is not a weight class our family fights in.”

“A mother can’t even dream now?” One glare, dispensed to her useless son — and then, with the seamless skill of a professional, the topic boomeranged. “Anyway. Hurry up and find someone. And stop being so picky — good enough is good enough.”

He nodded along, quietly exhaling a lungful of relief. So it had been idle sentiment — plus, obviously, an opening to resume the campaign. She didn’t know he knew Sulli. If she had known — if she had truly understood — she’d have had him chasing the girl by tomorrow’s breakfast.

He slid his eyes back to the screen — that dewy, peach-soft face — and privately conceded the point. The little Peach really does own the middle-aged-ajumma demographic. Total market capture. The auntie at the hanwoo restaurant had been exactly the same — doting so hard she could barely bring herself to charge them.

“What are you staring at?” The campaign resumed fire. “Don’t just look — apply yourself! If you actually like her, then go and pursue her. Our family is hardly a bad match, you know. A celebrity bride is well within our means.”

“That’s not — Mom! I was zoning out!” He pried his eyes off the television and put the teacup down with both hands, like it might testify against him. “Can you go three sentences without a marriage campaign?”

Outside, the last blue had drained out of the sky. The streetlights came on and pooled the street in warm gold.

He checked the time and stood. “Mom, Dad — I should head back.”

“So soon?” She rose halfway after him, and didn’t hold him — a mother’s arithmetic: her son had his own life to run now. “Then drive carefully. Text me when you’re home. Not tomorrow. When you’re home.”

“I will.” Coat on, shoes traded, door.

“Oh — one more thing.” She followed him to the entryway, struck by sudden recollection. “If next week is at all possible, come again. I’ll make the soy-braised pork belly you always begged for as a kid.”

He looked back at her. She stood framed in the doorway with the hallway light behind her, a soft gold edge drawn around her hair, her face full of an expectation she wasn’t bothering to hide.

Something warm rose in him all at once — unguarded, unearned, and absolutely welcome.

“Next week. I’ll be here.”

He lifted a hand, let himself out through the gate, slid into the driver’s seat, and started the car. In the mirror, the porch light held on until the corner took it.
