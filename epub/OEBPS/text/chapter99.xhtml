Chapter Ninety-Nine

Arrangements and the Japan Itinerary

FRIDAY MORNING · OCTOBER 2012 Mapo-gu, Seoul SAVORY F&B — the president’s office, third floor

Morning came up thin and bright, the sun cutting through leftover mist and laying itself across the streets.

October in Seoul. The heat that had made the whole city grievance-prone was gone; the air had picked up its first clean edge of chill.

Song Ji-yeon woke on schedule, ran through the morning routine, and pointed the car toward SAVORY F&B.

It was Friday. He had planned, in fact, not to come in at all — but a message from Kim Min-hyun had put an end to that plan, and here he was.

Which only hardened a resolution already taking shape: the man needed an executive assistant. Soon.

Rush-hour Seoul did what rush-hour Seoul does. The traffic lay across every lane like a tin centipede, inching, dorsal plates flashing brake lights. For a city you could misplace inside Los Angeles, it sure put up LA-grade congestion — and a drive that should have cost fifteen minutes quietly helped itself to thirty.

He didn’t fight it. He let the current carry the car and used the time to think.

At the office he went straight up to the third floor, pushed open his own door, hung his coat on the back of the chair, and had barely settled into it when two knocks landed on the doorframe.

“Come in.” He lifted the water glass off the desk.

Kim Min-hyun stepped in carrying a thick black folder, everything about him pressed and precise.

“Sajangnim.” He came to the desk and set the folder down in front of him. “The T-ara matter is fully settled. Yesterday afternoon I signed the artist-transfer agreement with Kim Kwang-soo of CCM Entertainment. T-ara is now an idol group under ShiningYouth. This is the full file — their exclusive management contracts, the copyrights to everything they’ve released since debut, the secondary-distribution rights on the music videos…”

Taking formal delivery of that file was, in the end, the entire reason Song Ji-yeon had driven in on a Friday.

He set the glass down and opened the folder.

Black letters on white pages, and at the tail of every document, the corporate seal and Kim Kwang-soo’s signature, crisp and legible.

There was a lot of it.

CCM ENTERTAINMENT · ARTIST TRANSFER — FILE SUMMARY

Exclusive management contracts — T-ara, six members, full remaining term, transferred to ShiningYouth Entertainment Co., Ltd.

Assignment of the “T-ara” trademark; master and publishing copyrights for all released recordings; music-video and secondary-distribution rights.

Committed overseas engagements, previously executed, transferring with the group: the K-Collection in Okinawa showcase, October 18; the November Japan promotional campaign for the new album, Sexy Love.

Acquisition price — ₩4,000,000,000 (settled in full)

Breach penalty, pre-signed overseas schedules — estimated ₩3,000,000,000

Executed in duplicate. Signature and seal: Kim Kwang-soo, CEO, CCM Entertainment.

Dozens of pages, loose-leaf and overlapping — contracts, trademark assignments, song rights, MV rights, and a small sheaf of overseas commitments already signed and sealed.

Kim Min-hyun nudged his glasses up the bridge of his nose and went on with the briefing.

“Once Kim Kwang-soo verified the amount, he signed faster than we expected — cleaner, too. But… the overseas schedules transferred with the contracts. Those are executed agreements. Walking away from them would run the penalty to somewhere near three billion won.”

Song Ji-yeon had already seen those pages.

October 18. The K-Collection in Okinawa showcase contract. After it, through November, the promotional rounds for the Japan release of Sexy Love.

It was almost funny. The bullying scandal had frozen T-ara’s domestic work solid — and Kim Kwang-soo, of all people, had kept their overseas calendar remarkably well fed. The album promotions alone would route the girls through eight Japanese cities: Nagoya, Sendai, Tokyo, Fukuoka, and the rest.

“Breach? Why would we breach?” He laughed, turning a page. “This is a parting gift from Kim Kwang-soo. We accept it graciously.”

The joke had a floor under it. He had genuinely been puzzling over how one operates an idol group. He had bought one; he had never run so much as a school talent show. A Japan trip with every logistical detail pre-signed was, to him, less a schedule than a guided tour — he intended to ride along, watch how the machine worked, and take notes.

Notes that would matter later, when it came time to mount the China campaign.

Kim Min-hyun, for his part, kept his face professionally still and privately despaired a little.

A gift, Sajangnim. A gift. That was four billion won of real money.

What he didn’t say out loud: if T-ara hadn’t been carrying so many pre-signed overseas dates, he could have pressed the price down toward two billion. It was exactly those dates Kim Kwang-soo had used to hold the line at four. Run the dates properly, though, and the premium earned itself back — and then some.

Assuming they could be run at all.

He let out a quiet breath and delivered the warning that was his actual job.

“Sajangnim, one thing I have to put in front of you. T-ara now belongs to ShiningYouth — which means every activity from October through November lands on ShiningYouth to deliver. And ShiningYouth, today, is a shell. It has no capacity to carry a Japan run.”

“That part I’m not worried about.” Song Ji-yeon waved it off. “We have ten-plus days until Okinawa — enough time to raise the company’s basic frame. And these are contracted events. There’s nothing for us to build. T-ara shows up on time with a couple of managers, performs, and comes home. The promoters run everything else.”

He didn’t know how to find T-ara work — that was true. But he had ordinary common sense, and ordinary common sense said a signed, scheduled event is a turnkey operation: the venue, the staging, the settlement — all of it belonged to the organizer. His ignorance lay elsewhere — in how you booked a girl group onto tours and variety shows in China. He had no showbiz network over there to speak of.

So the Japan trip would do double duty: a field trip. Learn the workflow here so the China campaign could be run properly later.

Kim Min-hyun nodded and took out his memo pad.

“Ne, Sajangnim. For liaising with the overseas promoters — should Legal second one or two people across, help hold the line?”

The organizers’ contact sheets were already sitting in the folder. ShiningYouth would have to open fresh conversations with all of them — those contracts bore Kim Kwang-soo’s signature, and with the group’s ownership changed hands, the paper would have to be re-executed under the new entity.

Song Ji-yeon leaned back and let his fingertips drum the desktop.

Tak. Tak. Tak.

He ran the whole position through his head. And the more of it he ran, the more outrageous the audit came out.

“ShiningYouth,” as of this morning, did not strictly speaking qualify as a company.

It was a shell. A stage set with a letterhead. The name rang impressively in the ear; the balance sheet behind it was a punchline. Take inventory: himself, the wallet behind the curtain. Six idols, newly purchased. And — fine — the two fangirls from SAVORY’s front desk, who counted only on a technicality and only if you squinted.

That was the entire organization.

No management team. No PR department. No cleaning lady. No security guard. Not even a real address — the company had nowhere to put a desk.

If anyone ran a registry search on ShiningYouth today, they would file it under boiler-room scam and move on.

So yes — delivering those contracts would genuinely require one or two legal professionals. Except SAVORY’s legal department, if he remembered right, was four people — Kim Min-hyun included. Carve one or two out of that, and what happened to the restaurant company’s own paper?

He looked up and waved the concern away before Kim Min-hyun could volunteer.

“Leave Legal where it is. You’re four people, total — I’m not hollowing out SAVORY to staff the new company. You keep the dining-side contracts moving. The entertainment side, I’ll work out.”

“Sajangnim, don’t worry on that account. SAVORY’s paper is procurement agreements — I can hold those alone with one hand. One or two of ours can absolutely carry the entertainment matters… and the Japanese promoters won’t be difficult. It’s re-papering executed agreements — a day, maybe two.”

Kim Min-hyun said it would be fine, so Song Ji-yeon stopped standing on ceremony. He slid the overseas-activity files out of the folder and handed them across.

“Then I’ll trouble Director Kim — put one or two of the international-law people on making contact.”

“Understood, Sajangnim. I’ll see to it now.”

Kim Min-hyun gathered the files, bowed, and withdrew.

The door had barely clicked shut when the phone came out of his pocket. He found Park Seon-young’s number and dialed.

One ring — no, not even one.

Phone · Park Seon-young → Song Ji-yeon

“Sajangnim!”

“Seon-young. My office. Now.”

“Understood! I’m already moving!”

Three minutes later, the knock came, and Park Seon-young pushed through it still catching his breath, sweat beaded at the hairline — a man who had clearly treated the stairwell as a sprint event.

He planted himself in front of the desk, hands flat against his trouser seams, spine vertical, his level of deference calibrated to the millimeter. Nothing to correct. Nothing to add.

Song Ji-yeon nodded at the chair opposite.

“Sit.”

Park perched on it — half of him on the seat, the other half still at attention — and skipped the throat-clearing entirely.

“You handled ShiningYouth from the very first form. The registration, the filings — all of it ran through your hands.”

He looked at him steadily.

“I’m handing you the company. Full authority, top to bottom. From today, you’re its representative director.”

Park Seon-young stopped working.

His eyes — never his best feature — went as wide as they physically could. His mouth hung a few millimeters open. Somewhere behind his forehead, an aging desktop computer made the sound a desktop computer makes when it gives up.

Full authority. To me.

He ran the concept into the ground and it still wouldn’t fit. Park Seon-young — until this morning, a planning-department director. Store operations, new-site launches, quarterly menus. And now, one sentence from the Sajangnim, and an entire entertainment company sat in his lap.

Director to representative director was not a step. It was the carp going over the waterfall and coming out a dragon — one stroke, a different animal. Directors draw salaries. Representative directors hold equity — options — and if the company flies, the class you were born in stops being the ceiling.

In his chest, one word assembled itself at parade volume.

LOYALTY.

“Sajangnim!” He came off the chair like it had bitten him, every soft line of his face trembling with the rest of him, flushed to the ears, eyes gone bright and wet at the rims.

“You— you mean it? ShiningYouth — for me to run?”

“Why — can’t you? Say the word and I’ll find someone who can.”

“I can! I absolutely, definitely can!”

He was waving both hands fast enough to blur, visibly one molecule away from dropping to the floor in a full formal kowtow.

“Sajangnim, rest absolutely assured — this is a second life you’ve handed me! From today: point east and I will not walk west! Send me out to beg and I’ll come back having built you a multinational out of begging! I, Park Seon-young, am your man in this life and your ghost in the next—”

“Stop. Stop.”

Goosebumps had staged a full deployment down both arms. Song Ji-yeon cut the broadcast before it could reach verse two.

“I don’t collect ghosts. I need a man who gets things done. Since you’ve accepted the job — first assignment.”

Park snapped upright, a soldier receiving orders, and produced from inside his jacket a small notebook and a pen, held at chest height, cap already off.

“Task one. Scout me a building in Seoul.” The instructions came out in clean working order. “Don’t buy — rent. Location first, facade second. The place has to look like a company, not an apology.”

The pen flew across the page.

“Understood — Seoul. Location, grandeur. No apologies.”

“Task two. Build the team. Staff the company.” A knuckle rapped the desk for emphasis. “HR, admin, legal — the standard skeleton, no discussion needed. What actually matters is PR and the artist-management team. You know exactly what the media weather on this Peninsula has been like all year. The PR people I want are fighters — hard hands, comment control, cleanup, counter-narrative. Then vocal coaches, dance coaches, cleaning, security. All of it. Recruit until the org chart is full.”

He watched Park write it all down, and then handed him the bottom line.

“Fastest possible timeline. The whole frame, standing. Once the site’s signed, I’ll wire the startup capital into ShiningYouth’s corporate account.”

“That money is the company’s runway for year one.”

Park closed the notebook, and the look in his eyes had gone past enthusiasm into something with a temperature.

The Sajangnim had already sunk four billion into ShiningYouth — and was now preparing a second injection on top. Nobody does that on a whim. The commitment was structural. Which meant the ladder he was standing on was real — and it went all the way to a floor he’d never touched.

There was a fire in his chest, and it wasn’t the promotion. It was the stage.

“Sajangnim, put your mind at ease!” He thumped his own chest like a warranty. “Site locked within three days! Full staff within two weeks! Mission guaranteed — I will not let you down!”

“Enough slogans. Go do the work.”

A ninety-degree bow, textbook depth — and then he was out the door at a dead sprint, even the door-closing radiating the energy of a man mainlining espresso.

✦ ✦ ✦

The office settled back into quiet. Song Ji-yeon leaned into the chair and rolled his neck once, slowly.

The frame was Park Seon-young’s problem now. The money was still his.

An entertainment company’s first year was a bottomless pit with excellent manners — it burned capital from the moment it opened its eyes, and it did not send progress reports.

No money, no movement. So — money.

He nudged the mouse awake and opened the trading terminal. Password, enter. The screen refreshed, and red poured into it edge to edge.

Samjin Group.

Ever since he’d gone in heavy off the Stock Forecast Card — the one that had reached him through Eun-jung — the chart had stopped behaving like a stock and started behaving like fiction. Four straight sessions of new highs. No pullback worth the name. A stock that had apparently decided gravity applied to other stocks.

He opened the retail board, where the trapped and the euphoric fed the scroll together at full volume.

PAXNET · SAMJIN GROUP (005630) — REAL-TIME BOARD

1did Samjin mainline gunpowder?? FOUR straight green days—

2it goes again today. CHARGE. my retirement fund is going in.

3fam, who else is fully cooked. i can’t feel my face. all in today.

4LMAOOO 20× leverage. one more week of this and i never work again.

He read the euphoria, and the corner of his mouth moved. There was no smile behind it.

In the capital’s casino, the moment every last retail trader is certain they can win is the moment the house lifts the knife.

Today was day five. The stock’s last carnival.

He kept his eyes on the minute line hopping across the screen, and every hop of the red digits added another eight-figure sum to the account.

SAMJIN GROUP (KRX 005630) · DAY 5 · MID-MORNING

Position₩10.0 billion notional — ₩5.0B principal, 2× leverage

Cumulative gain since entry+40%

Card’s final leg, into today’s close+5% more (≈ +45.2% total)

Shape beyond the windowwaterfall — gap-down cascade, no bid

The edgethe cliff is a known cliff; the crowd sees a staircase

The forecast’s horizon ended with one final five percent leg before the close. After that, the shape he’d been shown did what that shape always does — goes over the falls, and buries everyone who bought the top with it.

Knowing the hole cards before the deal even starts. That kind of asymmetry doesn’t leave much room for suspense — only for timing.

“No rush. Let it run hot a little longer.”

It was barely past ten. He had never been a last-bite man — but he also wasn’t a man who let money walk out the door on principle.

The plan: by three o’clock this afternoon, every Samjin share he held would be listed and gone. A week of gains this unhinged meant a crowd of buyers permanently camped under the chart, hands raised. Plenty of retail to catch every block he dropped.

When the dust settled, the trading account would be sitting close to ten billion won.

Which looked like a lot, and in most weeks would be.

But the plan said: carve out a full five billion. Every won of it into ShiningYouth’s corporate account — the operating budget for the year. Which would leave the trading account back down to five billion.

Add the three-odd billion the peach’s rebate cards had churned out — still parked in his bank, still doing nothing — and there it was: a month and change of near-constant motion, and the war chest had, essentially, not moved.

Somewhere between impressive and insulting. The wealth had grown fast; the burn rate had simply grown faster. However much came in, the operating sensation stayed hand-to-mouth.

He let his eyes drift across the screen while the next moves assembled themselves.

Five billion into ShiningYouth. Let’s be honest about what that meant: not one won of it coming back this year.

The six crowns were his now — but on the Peninsula, their goodwill with the general public hadn’t merely dipped. The scandal had taken it apart, beam by beam. Domestic music shows, mall gigs, festival slots as a recovery plan? That wasn’t strategy. That was setting money on fire in public, politely.

There was exactly one way out of the box: the Chinese market.

Every agency on this Peninsula was already circling that market — and he, of all people, knew precisely how fat it was about to get. A slab of prime, well-marbled market; one honest bite of it would keep six idols walking sideways through this industry for a decade.

Reality, of course, was built entirely of bones.

The girls’ Chinese — all six of them, pooled — amounted to one complete “nǐ hǎo” and one complete “xiè xie.” Both mangled.

You cannot walk into that market as a beautiful mute who can only smile.

A China push, he’d always figured, was a sincerity play. You meet the fans halfway; the fans meet you the rest of the way. Keep the language broken forever, and whatever money you make over there is borrowed, not earned — it leaves the moment the novelty does.

And he had every intention of building T-ara into the number-one girl group in China. Which meant corner-cutting on the one skill that carried the whole plan was not on the menu.

“Guess I’m hiring Chinese teachers.” He rubbed his jaw.

The syllabus was already drafted in his head. The moment the new office finished renovation, first order of business: the girls go into the practice room, and the door locks behind them. No other schedules. Eight hours a day of intensive Chinese, drilled up from survival phrases, the complete devil’s course — nothing on the dinner plate until the day’s lines are memorized.

Want to earn in that market but won’t sweat the one gate that guards it? What did they think fans were — ATMs with no memory?

Pass the gate. Stand on that stage and talk to the fans in their own words, joke with them in their own language — and only then does that vast market actually hold. Everything before that is tourism.

On paper, the early spend looked like a pit that never paid: building rent, payroll, vocal and dance and Chinese instructors, six idols’ living costs — all of it leaning on the five billion like a cathedral on five columns.

But necessary. Absolutely, structurally necessary.

Survive the growing pains. Forge them into the hottest K-pop girl group in that market. Book the tours that sell out, land the endorsements with national reach — and the five billion stops being money. It becomes a rounding error, repaid many times over, in a single season.

“For now — let them enjoy a wholesome stretch of hard days.”

A soft laugh, and the thought reeled itself back in. His eyes returned to the screen, and the red digits went on dancing.

SAME HOUR Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Park Ji-yeon’s flat, second floor — the little dragon’s den

Across the river and down in Cheongdam, the six of them — altogether innocent of the syllabus being drafted in Mapo — were piled into the little dragon’s living room, where an entirely different operation was opening its brief.
