Chapter Fifty-Six

The Weekly Meeting

SUNDAY NIGHT Apgujeong-dong, Seoul PIEN PLUS, curbside

The car ran steady on through Seoul’s late-night streets, toward Apgujeong.

Eun-jung kept her face to the window, watching the streetlights drag themselves past and stretch long shadows into the dark. Once — only once — her eyes slid sideways, to the driver’s seat. The profile there was quiet and clean-cut under the passing light, eyes forward, hands easy on the wheel.

She caught herself wishing, absurdly, that the road were a little longer.

Apgujeong, regrettably, arrived anyway.

The car turned onto a hushed side street and rolled to a stop in front of the PIEN PLUS building. Engine off, parking brake on, and he turned to the passenger seat.

“Here we are.”

“Mm…”

The seatbelt came off. She didn’t move. For a few seconds she looked down at her hands in her lap, choosing nothing — and then, quietly:

“Thank you. Really. For today.”

A pause. She seemed to be shopping for better words, and in the end simply gave up and smiled.

“For going around the flats with Ji-yeon. And for letting me rest at your place.”

“I told you — it was nothing.” He smiled back, and his voice was mild. “Go up and rest properly. You’ve been running all day.”

“Mm. I know.”

Her hand found the door, and stopped. She turned back to him once more.

“You rest early too. Good night.”

He lifted a hand and waved.

“Good night.”

She pushed the door open and stepped out into the early-autumn wind. He didn’t pull away. He sat where he was, watching her cross to the lobby and pass inside — watching until her figure was swallowed up by the corridor light — and only then drew his eyes back, started the engine, and turned the car for Cheongdam.

SUNDAY · 10 P.M. Cheongdam-dong, Seoul home again

Home. Jacket off, tossed over the sofa arm, straight into the bathroom.

The water came down hot, and under it his mind ran tomorrow on a loop. Monday, at the open: sell Samsung, all of it. Stock Forecast Card onto the week’s biggest gainer. All in. Friday, at the close: out. One card covers a week. Two cards, two weeks.

And when the cards ran dry — well. He knew exactly whose goddess ability he’d be farming for the next batch.

He wiped the water off his face and grinned at the tile, entirely aware that a man planning to keep lightly colliding with a woman who liked him — purely for the dividend — was operating at a certain moral discount. Nothing to be done about it. Capital was tight, and hers was the only wool within reach.

Out of the shower, into sleepwear, and the bedside phone said ten o’clock. Tomorrow held the weekly meeting and the Samsung business both, and the mood for phone games died on the spot.

MONDAY · 8 A.M. Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the flat, wrong side of an empty pillow

Monday, eight sharp, and his eyes opened on schedule. His hand was already crawling along the edge of the pillow, hunting — and got halfway through the search before the memo loaded: Mushroom had gone home with Eun-jung last night.

The corner of his mouth twitched, at a loss for its own opinion. He actually missed it. The wake-up service. The furry alarm clock that opened every morning by walking on his chest.

Did he have… masochistic tendencies?

He shook the nonsense out and rolled over. Phone off the nightstand, a check of the hour, up and at the sink.

He had a full day. Nine o’clock, the weekly meeting — two hours minimum, by all precedent — with the Samsung sale and the next purchase to be executed somewhere in the seams of it.

Half an hour later: a plain white shirt, a casual suit jacket thrown on over it, and a glass of milk standing in the kitchen.

Glug, glug.

Two swallows, done. Keys, out the door, down the elevator to the basement level, and the car pointed for SAVORY F&B.

The morning run north out of Gangnam was all September light, the sun coming down through the ginkgoes and laying dapples all over the road. One hand on the wheel, he nudged the air conditioning a degree cooler with the other.

The car pulled up before an off-white office building, slid into the reserved spot, and he stepped out with the document folder off the passenger seat.

SAVORY F&B kept a modest footprint — a touch over three hundred pyeong, three floors. The lobby was small: one white desk, one computer, the wordmark on the wall behind it in clean, unornamented lettering. Past the desk sat the logistics and training department, generally inhabited only at quarterly training sessions. Most of the company lived up on the second and third floors.

“Sajangnim, good morning.”

The receptionist rose as he came through the door.

“Morning. The department heads all in?”

He didn’t keep an assistant — so the front desk was where a man went for facts.

She was twenty-one or twenty-two, young and pretty, and no one in the building had needed an explanation for why a certain planning director materialized at that desk whenever work went quiet.

“Ne, Sajangnim. All six department heads are in — they’re waiting for you in the conference room.”

He nodded and went straight for the stairs to the third floor. After the body enhancement, a flight of stairs wasn’t a problem in any technical sense. It annoyed him anyway, on principle. A bona fide regressor — and his company didn’t have an elevator. Two months into his second life, and this was the empire. The protagonists of every regressor novel he’d ever binged would have struck him from the register on sight.

He pushed into the conference room to find both sides of the long table full. The six department heads rose and bowed as one.

“Sajangnim.”

He nodded, satisfied, and dropped into the chair at the head of the table.

“Sit, sit — all of you. What’s with the standing? You’re not tired, and I am. None of this ceremony from now on. Not my thing.”

“Ne, Sajangnim — it won’t happen again!” Six voices, in near-perfect unison.

Park Seon-young, settling back into his chair, kept his lips to himself. If that grin had left your face even once since you walked in, Sajangnim, I might have believed you.

Flattery was the one arrow that never failed to pierce. Every adult in this room knew the rule by heart, and every promise of never-again came pre-expired. Come next Monday, all six would be on their feet and bowing exactly like this. Because the boss — visibly, measurably — drank it in.

“Everyone’s here?”

He was in a fine mood. Wanting to be a chaebol — wasn’t this the exact moment the wanting had been for? He flipped open the stack of documents in front of him and ran his eyes once around the table.

“Then let’s begin.”

Park Seon-young went first. Notebook open, one throat-clearing for form’s sake.

“Sajangnim — the 312 Cheongdam-dong storefront. Final contract is executed with the owner, Lim Yoon-ah-ssi.”

He drew a copy of the agreement from his folder and slid it across the table.

“Five-year lease. ₩10 million monthly, ₩400 million key deposit, and the first year’s rent paid up front, in full. The previous tenant, Old Chen, moved out of the space yesterday.”

Song Ji-yeon picked the contract up and ran down the key clauses; satisfied, he nodded. Yoon-ah’s side had moved fast — the letter of intent only days old, and the full paperwork already walked through to execution today.

“The design-and-build team for the new store is lined up as well,” Park Seon-young went on. “Our old partners — they did the fit-outs on all seven ONGI stores.”

“Good.” He set the contract down and looked over at his planning director. “Stay on top of the build. Keep hunting storefronts in Seoul — the numbers work, we sign.”

“Ne, Sajangnim.”

Park Seon-young took one careful glance at him, registered no further orders pending, and settled back down with his heart at ease.

“Finance. Report the company’s capital position — what can actually go into ONGI’s expansion.”

Kang Jin-ho nodded, stood, and produced a single sheet.

“Ne, Sajangnim. As of today—”
