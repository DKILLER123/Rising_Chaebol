Chapter One Hundred Nine

The Overthinking Han So-hee

TUESDAY · EARLY AFTERNOON ONGI Cheongdam — second floor The VIP corridor, immediately after the naming of names

“Sajangnim — was there anything else?”

“Nothing else.”

“Then I’ll get back to work.”

Han So-hee breathed a syllable of relief, risked one glance at his face, and turned to make her escape at a brisk, professionally calibrated pace.

She was, by self-assessment, an ordinary employee — and ordinary employees do not linger in the gravitational field of the company’s Sajangnim.

The pressure was considerable.

A NOTE ON THE OFFICE · THE CHAIN OF COMMAND

On this Peninsula, the hierarchy between senior and junior in the same workplace is strict; the hierarchy between ranks above and below is stricter still. Greet a superior: ninety degrees, held. Hand anything over: two hands, both of them. A superior is speaking: you are not. An assigned task: completed to the letter, then reported.

Comparisons to a feudal dynasty are common, and not refuted with much energy. Can’t live inside it? The exits are clearly labeled — resign yourself, or be resigned.

Han So-hee had fought hard for a job this clean, with wages this decent. Lingering near the Sajangnim, risking exposure of her actual personality, was not on the survival plan.

Song Ji-yeon watched her go — the brisk shoulders, the flight trajectory — rubbed his nose, and gave the empty corridor a wry little smile.

Am I that scary?

He’d genuinely meant to ask how she’d ended up at ONGI. But since the universe had now delivered her into arm’s reach twice in one afternoon, he had no intention of letting the matter stay un-asked.

Meat this tender does not walk past a predator’s mouth twice.

✦ ✦ ✦

In his last life he had watched the drama My Name for Han So-hee, and that was the entire depth of his research: a face he liked, an actress he noted. The rest he’d absorbed as stray gossip — parents divorced, raised by her grandmother, landed in Seoul after high school with thirty thousand won to her name, broke in as a music-video model.

A hard-luck case. …Why does this dossier feel familiar? Sulli. Hara. Her. Three girls the world shortchanged at the starting line — if the three of them ever ended up in one room, they’d be friends inside of ten minutes.

He shook his head and let the smile go crooked.

She wouldn’t give him an opening to ask? Fine. He’d simply go and find out.

SAVORY’s Logistics & Training division was, functionally, the company’s personnel department: every employee cleared a background check on the way in, and Song Ji-yeon was one phone call away from all of it.

✦ ✦ ✦

MINUTES LATER Cheongdam-dong — the kerbside The black Mercedes, engine off, phone up

He walked out the front, stood at the kerb, and dialed Logistics & Training direct.

Lee Ji-hoon was, technically, thirty meters away inside the store. But Song Ji-yeon would have bet the car that the man had no idea who Lee So-hee was — the division head carried twenty-some direct reports and the company ran to hundreds of staff; a man who cannot remember one tray-carrier’s file is a man who remembers none of them. Asking him would have been pure latency.

The line picked up on the first ring.

Phone · SAVORY Logistics & Training — duty desk → Song Ji-yeon

“Sajangnim — good afternoon!”

“I need a file pulled.” He kept it flat and immediate. “A Cheongdam store employee. Lee So-hee. Her full onboarding packet — to my inbox, inside five minutes.”

“Yes, Sajangnim — on it this second!”

The voice at the other end did not breathe again until the call ended. Duty-desk instincts: when the Sajangnim bypasses every layer to call you personally, the five minutes are not a suggestion.

He hung up, dropped into the Mercedes at the kerb, opened his mail on the phone, and waited.

A NOTE ON THE CLASS · THE CHAEBOL’S CONCIERGE

The privilege of the chaebol class is not the money — it is the compression. Paperwork that eats an ordinary applicant’s month resolves, at that altitude, in the length of one phone call.

This is the actual reason otherwise comfortable people chase rank the way they do. Nobody worships a number. Everybody worships what the number can reach.

Ding.

Under three minutes. He opened the attachment: Lee So-hee’s employment application, scanned and filed.

The photo at the top was a bare face, photographed like a passport and defiant anyway — cool eyes, that distance that had no business belonging to a nineteen-year-old.

Read once, then read again. Some files stay quiet about the loudest things.

Song Ji-yeon leaned back into the seat and drummed two fingers on the wheel, slow.

As advertised, then. The stray gossip of a future internet, now cross-examined by a personnel file: the “most beautiful homewrecker,” the controversy empress of a later decade — and underneath the headline, this. A girl from Ulsan forwarding her wages to a grandmother’s pharmacy.

By the last page, the decision had finished forming. This was the window — the one and only window. A girl this face belonged to would not be carrying trays for long; the modeling world would find her the way water finds a crack, and after the first paycheck, signing her would stop being an errand and start being an auction.

So: now.

He got out of the car and walked back into ONGI.

✦ ✦ ✦

TUESDAY · MID-AFTERNOON ONGI Cheongdam — ground floor, then up A full house, and one face missing from it

He came through the doors in time to catch Lim Yoon-ah and Kim Tae-yeon descending the solid-wood staircase, lunch completed — masks and caps back on, but the lightness in their steps testified louder than any review site.

Tae-yeon clocked him first. Her eyes went on over the mask like headlights.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi — your hotpot is a crime!” She crossed the floor to him at a trot, enthusiasm outrunning volume, that soft voice somehow landing with force. “The signature broth — incredible. And the meat was so fresh I felt guilty. I haven’t eaten like that in months!”

The commander of the nation’s biggest girl group, glowing over a lunch table like a kid let loose in a candy shop — Song Ji-yeon laughed out loud before he could help it.

“If it pleases Tae-yeon-ssi, the honor is ONGI’s. Anytime you can face the scale afterward, the door’s open.”

“Oh, we’re coming back. Next time I’m bringing Pani, and Sunny, and the whole of Girls’ Generation!”

She was already nodding through it, visibly assembling the reservation in her head.

Next to that open flame, Lim Yoon-ah kept her pilot light on low — two sentences of even, polite agreement, exactly.

“Ne. It was genuinely good.”

Then, without appearing to move at all, she pinched the hem of Tae-yeon’s sleeve — tone it down, we’re in public — the choreography of an unnie who has managed this exact brightness for five years.

Song Ji-yeon watched the exchange and grinned. Yoon-ah really was the steady one.

…Hold on. Wasn’t the fandom consensus that Tae-yeon is the textbook introvert — the I-est I in the industry? That woman upstairs just declared a nine-member team dinner at parade volume. She doesn’t read introvert. She reads recharge complete.

The three of them stood by the ground-floor aisle and traded a few easy lines about upcoming branch openings.

“We won’t keep you any longer then, Song Ji-yeon-ssi. Thank you for today’s hospitality.” Lim Yoon-ah dipped a small, punctilious bow.

“My pleasure. Watch yourselves out there — I’ll walk you to the car.”

He personally convoyed the two idols out the doors and stood by until the van folded them into traffic. Only then did he turn back inside.

He re-entered the store and swept the ground floor once, corner to corner. Full house — every table out, servers threading the aisles with trays, the whole machine at honest capacity.

He looked for one face in particular and did not find it.

No Han So-hee on the floor. He didn’t panic; the ONGI shift system had answers. He took the stairs to the second floor.

✦ ✦ ✦

CONTINUED ONGI Cheongdam — second floor Room 0012, being reset

VIP rooms, the whole corridor. He rounded the stair corner — and there she was.

Room 0012. The room the idols had just vacated. Lee So-hee, alone, restoring it to factory condition.

Every ONGI private room keeps a service bell on the table; ring it and the assigned floor staff answer within a breath — refills, water, napkins. When the meal ends, the room resets. The second-floor corridors run a small crew of exactly such staff. Han So-hee was one of them.

Dark ONGI uniform, long hair pinned up at the nape — and even standing still over a table of empty dishes, the keep-out aura came off her like cold off ice.

He walked the corridor’s full length without slowing.

At the footsteps she turned, saw the Sajangnim, and straightened into a textbook ninety-degree bow.

“Sajangnim.”

He stopped in front of her and looked her over, unhurried.

Han So-hee, for her part, was quietly running out of theories. This was sighting number three today — which was at least two past normal. Was there something wrong with me? Did somebody report something? Why does the Sajangnim keep… orbiting?

“Lee So-hee.” He didn’t dress it up.

“Ne, Sajangnim — your instructions?” Head down, respect layered on respect.

No preamble, no cushion, no run-up. The question arrived bare.

“Do you want to be an artist?”

…What?

Her head came up fast — and the joy that hit her eyes died before it could organize, replaced on arrival by a full perimeter of caution.

She was not a child. There is no such thing as uncomplicated kindness — a lesson her own mother had taught her, in the negative. And a stranger with a handful of shared silences was several tax brackets below family on the trust ledger.

Surprise first, walls second. She had stopped extending the benefit of the doubt to anyone, because life had graded that course for her personally, repeatedly, in red ink.

“Sajangnim… what do you mean by that?”

“I mean it plainly.” His voice kept its unhurried middle register. “I own a new agency — ShiningYouth Entertainment. Your profile is what I’m in the market for. I’d sign you as a talent. As an actress.”

The words landed somewhere behind her ears and rang.

A windfall, dropped from the sky?

In Seoul, the sky drops no rice cakes. Only traps — the kind that cost everything to spring.

She kept her face still and audited the man in front of her instead.

Young. Handsome, in the clean-cut way that reads as expensive. In a position of power at an age where most men were still renting their suits. He had stared at her at the ribbon-cutting. Asked her name in the corridor. Now this — appearing at her section, out of nowhere, offering to sign her.

So that was the pattern. The question she’d been turning over all afternoon — why is the Sajangnim so interested in me? — had just answered itself.

And the answer made her faintly sick.

Her lashes dropped; the cold went into storage behind them.

Sponsorship. Gilded cages. “Special clauses.” The vocabulary of the last two months, heard through restaurant kitchen walls and over bar counters, in the voices of men who thought volume equaled persuasion. She was nineteen and already fluent.

Divorced parents. A mother who never did a mother’s work and left with debt instead. A grandmother who had raised her on a pension and pride. She knew the exchange rate of the world’s kindness, and she knew the only asset she carried on the books: this face.

And she remembered her grandmother.

MEMORY · ULSAN — THE OLD RENTED ROOM

The ancient television with its blizzard of static, and her grandmother planted in front of it for company, watching the beautiful people flicker in and out of the snow.

“If only our So-hee could be on TV too…”

Over and over, like a prayer she never expected to be answered — a wish so old it had worn a groove in the evening.

Television. Acting. Her grandmother’s wish — and her own, buried so deep and so long it had sediment into bedrock.

And what did she have to dig it out with?

Nothing. No connections, no degree, no training. Every résumé she’d fired into this city had sunk without a ripple; every agency she’d walked into had turned her around at the door — and the ones that hadn’t turned her around had offered contracts whose supplementary clauses made her skin crawl.

Men exactly like this one, radiating exactly this much ease.

She had taken this tray job precisely so that she would never have to say yes to one of those. And now look — the trap had come to her, wearing a company’s uniform, in her own workplace.

Her fingers whitened around the edge of the service tablet.

So run the numbers, then. If the Sajangnim’s intentions were exactly what they smelled like — if the price of the signature was a gilded cage—

A small, dead-edged laugh, aimed at herself.

Fine. If it couldn’t be dodged forever, then between him and the pot-bellied alternatives lining this industry’s corridors, the arithmetic was almost insulting in its clarity. Better the devil with the clean jawline than the ones with the wedding rings.

For the television. For the sentence her grandmother had worn a groove into the evening with. For the one chance in this life of flipping the table over — she’d pay it.

A caged bird, then. Still a step up from a corridor, forever, with a tray.

She drew a full breath, and when her eyes came back up, the audit was over and only the bet remained, buried where he couldn’t read it.

“Sajangnim — you’re joking, aren’t you?” Politeness up front, distance filed underneath. “I’m a high-school dropout who carries trays. I’ve never spoken a single line of dialogue in my life. What about me says actress to you?”

The wariness in her eyes almost made him laugh.

Anyone else on this Peninsula, hearing an offer like his, would already be face-down in a full bow. But Han So-hee wasn’t anyone else. She came with spine.

Nineteen years old and armored like a hedgehog — every quill up, permanently, on principle, ready to go to war with a world that had never once fought fair.

He had no way of knowing that in the preceding ten seconds, the girl in front of him had lived through an entire marriage, a betrayal, and a bankruptcy.

“Your face is the entire argument.” He said it straight, without decoration. “ONGI doesn’t need another tray carried. My agency needs a face people remember on sight — one that arrives with its own headlines. You didn’t pick that face. But the camera is going to. You were built for the lens, Lee So-hee — the only question in this building is which side of it you stand on.”

The words went through her armor at a seam she hadn’t checked.

So it is the face. Of course it’s the face. Everything in her life that had ever arrived unearned — the good and the bad alike — had come through exactly that door.

She closed her eyes. Opened them. What was left in them was a person walking into water over her head, on purpose, with her shoes on.

“All right. I accept.”

“Good. Go change — I’m taking you to the new office to sign.”

“But my shift doesn’t end for another two hours…”

“I’ll square it with the store manager myself. Today’s pay counts in full — don’t worry about the clock.”

She bowed and went — fast, before her nerve could file an objection — toward the staff changing room.

Her steps came back just slightly unglued from the floor, her heart running well ahead of her feet.

In the changing room she shed the dark ONGI uniform and put back on the clothes she’d arrived in that morning — a denim jacket washed nearly white, black trousers cut for someone else’s body, canvas shoes older than her Seoul address.

The mirror returned a pale face above the collar — and features that had no business, statistically or morally, belonging to any of it.

1st LOOK · STREET CAST “THE CIVILIAN EDIT”

No stylist, no budget, no retouching — and the camera would still find her first.

Outerwear — a blue denim jacket washed to the color of old sky, cuffs fraying at the thread

Top — whatever plain thing was warm and clean that morning

Bottom — black trousers, one size wrong, hanging on by conviction

Footwear — white canvas sneakers, washed-out, soles worn to the shape of her walks

Hair — long and dark, let down out of the shift knot, no thought given to it

The total cost of the outfit would not cover a magazine’s lunch — and the face above it out-performs every editorial in the rack

A casting director would call this photograph “found.” The industry word for what it found is face.

She looked at the mirror’s version of herself and said it quietly, so only the glass could hear.

Lee So-hee — from today on, no looking back. Even if what’s ahead is a pit of fire, you jump in smiling.

Three minutes later she walked back out to the corridor. Song Ji-yeon was exactly where she’d left him.

He took in the civvies — the faded denim, the wrong-sized trousers — and something in the combination landed on him sideways: the cool distance from the corridor now layered under a thin, defenseless frailty, as if the uniform had been the armor all along.

“Let’s go.” No ceremony. He turned and led.

Han So-hee fell in behind him like a wary shadow — close enough to follow, far enough to run.

They slipped out the side door, under the main hall’s radar.

The black Mercedes waited at the kerb, quiet as furniture. He opened the passenger door and stood off it.

She looked at the car — the single most expensive object she had ever been invited inside — bit her lip once, and got in.

The cabin held a faint, unfamiliar cologne of something clean and expensive, and she sat very still inside it, like a guest in a stranger’s religion.

The car pulled out and folded into Cheongdam’s glittering traffic.

“Hungry?” — out of nowhere, mildly.

Her spine tightened on reflex.

“No, thank you… Sajangnim.”

“At the company, ‘Sajang’ is fine. Off the clock — ‘oppa’ will do.”

A pause exactly one heartbeat long.

“Ne — oppa.”
