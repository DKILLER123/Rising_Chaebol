Chapter One Hundred Two

I’m the Eldest Here

FRIDAY · PAST SEVEN Gwacheon, Gyeonggi-do Seoul Grand Park — the campsite by the stream

By the time the two of them came down the little path to the stream, the last daylight was gone for good.

The folding tables and stools had long since been laid out in tidy ranks — the work of five efficient hands. Marinated hanwoo, thick-cut samgyeopsal, chicken wings already glazed gold before they’d ever met a flame, skewers of vegetables in a proud row: a full table, loaded to the edges, arranged with the gravity of a state banquet. The sight alone could make a bishop ambitious.

Park Hyo-min was crouched at the charcoal brazier, a lighting torch in one hand, playing flamethrower over the coals.

Blue flame spat from the nozzle and licked along the black charcoal. Within a minute the whole bed had caught, glowing orange-red, throwing off a crisp, busy crackling like small applause.

“Wow — you two have perfect timing!” Hyo-min spotted them coming and rose, dusting her palms off against each other, grinning. “Coals just came ready. We were only waiting on the two heroes of the day.”

Park Ji-yeon lifted a full tray of hanwoo out of the cooler and set it down with a thump of ceremony.

“Sit, sit, SIT! I’m dying here! HOW does pitching tents take an entire evening?!”

They’d rolled into the campground a little past six, and between the tents and the furniture and the general parliament of opinions, it was now half past seven.

Which — for this particular collection of women — counted as an early dinner. There had been seasons when schedules ran so dense that skipping meals entirely had been routine hygiene, a small tax paid for looking right under stage light. But the last two quiet months had re-civilized them: three meals a day, on the clock, like ordinary people. Suddenly eating at seven-thirty felt like a border crossing into famine.

The corner of his mouth crooked. He looked over at the dragon.

“Funny. You skip dinner entirely on a normal day and nothing happens to you.”

He knew this case file by heart. Park Ji-yeon, absorbed in ranked matches, forgetting meals for a full day was not an anomaly — it was her resting state.

Back then: nothing, apparently.

Now: at death’s door.

Women. Fickle to the last. Every single one of them.

Ji-yeon’s lower lip came out.

“Oppa. We are OUT. Out-of-doors. The entire point of going outside is eating and drinking! If I’m not out here for the food, what am I out here for? Was staying home and gaming not good enough for somebody?”

He opened his mouth, found no counterargument anywhere in the building, and sat down in surrender.

She was right, and he knew it in his bones — twenty-two years of a restaurant family will teach you this much about human nature: at its root, tourism is a food-service category.

✦ ✦ ✦

Seven people folded themselves around the small table. The brazier went in the center, the grill rack bridged across the glowing coals, and the wire mesh over it began its slow climb toward scorching.

“Me! Me — I’ll do it!”

Ji-yeon seized the tongs like a relay baton and began laying down slices of samgyeopsal, fat and lean layered in perfect strata, with the solemnity of a first-grader writing her own name.

“Tsss—”

Meat met the hot wire and answered instantly — that savage, mouth-watering hiss — and white smoke went up carrying the smell of dinner.

Jeon Bo-ram’s eyes had glued themselves to the grate and were not accepting other assignments. She held her little bowl in both hands, elbows in, spine straight — a soldier at parade rest. Her stomach was issuing continuous commentary, and her one terror in the world was that her hands would be slow to the first piece of meat.

Lee Qri, seated beside her, was watching this small hamster guard her food supply and finding it very hard to breathe quietly.

She took up her own tongs with elaborate, glacial patience. She waited — truly waited — until a slice had gone gold on both faces and was bubbling at the edges, then speared the single most perfect piece on the entire grate. She did not put it in her own bowl. She lifted it and dangled it, slowly, right in front of Bo-ram’s nose.

“Bo-ram-ah. Look at this one. How’s the doneness — good?”

Bo-ram’s eyes tracked the meat the way a cat tracks a feather on a string. Her bowl thrust forward on pure reflex.

“Give — give it here! GIVE!”

Qri turned her wrist, and the slice landed, neat as a court seal, in Park Ji-yeon’s bowl.

“Ji-yeon worked the grill. Growing girls eat first.”

Ji-yeon beamed, stole a glance at the desperately craving unnie beside her, and with a face of total calm picked the slice up and extended it toward Bo-ram — a peace offering, an apology, a benediction.

Bo-ram’s whole face lit with gratitude. Her bowl came out to meet it—

— and the hand retracted. Blew on the meat twice. Placed it in her own mouth. Chewed. Narrowed her eyes in bliss.

“Mm. Melt-in-your-mouth. Perfect.”

“YAH! LEE QRI! PARK JI-YEON~!!” Bo-ram was on her feet, bouncing. They had done this on purpose. She knew it in her bones. This was a conspiracy!

“What?” Qri’s face was a portrait of innocence framed for a gallery. “I was honoring the hierarchy. Ji-yeon’s the baby of this group — the baby is growing. Someone has to look after the children.”

The double-cross having succeeded to specification, Ji-yeon joined the laughing, entirely at peace with the universe.

“Qri-unnie speaks wisdom. I am growing — I need it more. So Bo-ram-unnie, you just wait for the next batch, okay?”

Bo-ram’s mouth pulled sideways. She stared down at her empty bowl and muttered — quietly, mostly to the bowl —

“…I’m growing too, for your information.”

It was quiet. It was not, however, quiet — seven people seated elbow to elbow at one small table constitute a security perimeter, and her sentence cleared it in full, word for word, on the first pass.

Pfft. The sound came from six directions at once.

Park So-yeon had laughed herself sideways, one arm wrapped around her own ribs.

“Unnie. Sweetheart. You’re about to be thirty. Growing still?”

Bo-ram was T-ara’s eldest — born in ’86, twenty-seven this year. Age was, and had always been, the one hill she could never quite take.

“About-to-be — YAH! I am twenty-seven! Twenty-seven! Young! Spring chicken!”

It was not the first time So-yeon had gone fishing in that particular pond, and it would not be the last time the pond lost.

Ji-yeon, for her part, had progressed to slapping her own thigh, laughing in one continuous burst, absolutely unable to stop.

“Hahaha — unnie, it’s fine! It’s FINE! Our secret dies at this table. As long as the five of us never say anything, nobody out there will ever find out you’re flirting with thirty. You look like the maknae of this group anyway.”

“YAH, PARK JI-YEON! I’M THE ELDEST HERE!!”

Oh, she caught the subtext. She caught all of it. That last line had nothing to do with the calendar and everything to do with altitude.

Aigoo. Fine. Age was not the true wall. Height was the wall.

“Hahaha — okay, unnie~.”

Ji-yeon let the vowel trail out long and insolent, and delivered the whole reply with a sidelong look that was one letter short of a written provocation.

Bo-ram’s temper arrived at its stop and found her already waiting, fists curled and tight.

She wanted — wanted — to go discipline the little dragon on the spot.

But memory, ever the killjoy, played its compilation: the highlights of every previous engagement with the dragon, each ending in uniform, humiliating defeat. Strategically, morally, physically. In front of witnesses.

…Better to let it go.

Endure it. For now.

Besides — the Sajangnim was sitting right there.

This could be framed as generosity. As face-preservation. The maknae shouldn’t be throttled in front of the future CEO of the company — it would reflect poorly on her upbringing.

Yes. That was the reasoning. That was exactly the reasoning.

It wasn’t that she couldn’t beat the little dragon. It was that she was protecting the dragon’s dignity in front of management. Honestly — an unnie this magnanimous was a blessing the dragon flatly did not deserve.

She had fully persuaded herself. The lingering sulk, however, declined to be persuaded, so she converted it directly into appetite: took up her own tongs, fixed her eyes on the grate, and began laying meat with the focus of a woman single-handedly defending a supply line. Self-sufficiency. A lifestyle.

He watched the whole exchange and laughed out loud.

So the resident Bo-ram-hazing wasn’t a variety-editing artifact after all. T-ara ran this software at home, at full build, all year round.

He spared the puffed-up eldest a glance of something close to pity. Of the five others, only Hyo-min and Eun-jung were confirmed non-combatants — Eun-jung because her heart ran too big to hunt anything, and Hyo-min because underneath the volume she was soft as proofing dough. The remaining three treated the hazing of their eldest as standing team-building: one jab minimum per opportunity, in any weather, no holidays observed.

And looking at her there — puffed up, betrayed, heroically grilling her own dinner — he honestly couldn’t stop the laugh.

He picked up his tongs, lifted the single best-cooked slice on the grate — premium hanwoo, edge-caramelized, still hissing — and set it down, steady and precise, in Jeon Bo-ram’s bowl.

“All right, that’s enough bickering. Eat this one.”

Bo-ram blinked. Looked down. The hanwoo sat there, radiating aroma and justice.

Her big eyes went floodlit.

“Thank you, Song Ji-yeon-ssi! You’re the best after all!”

Heat was a formality she skipped — two quick blows, straight into her mouth, and her next sentence arrived pre-chewed.

Watching her eat, cheeks rounded out like a hamster banking food for winter, Qri lost the war with herself and handed her a napkin.

“Bo-ram-unnie. Slow down. Anyone watching would think the old company starved you — not one square meal in years.”

Ji-yeon turned the meat on the grate, brisk and capable, and piled on from the side.

“So heartless, unnie. Oppa gives you ONE piece of meat and he’s ‘the best.’ I used to split my snacks with you — actual snacks, for years — and I never once got a thank-you… don’t tell me it’s a face thing? Friends forgotten the moment a handsome man shows up? Do you have a crush on our Sajangnim~?”

Bo-ram had only just gotten the meat swallowed when the question detonated. She opened her mouth for an immediate, vigorous rebuttal — and the meat, filing a late objection, closed her throat instead.

“Kgh— kghk—!”

Three full coughs. Then, in strict sequence: a guilty glance at Song Ji-yeon, then a blush arriving with no interval in between, then — in the smallest voice the meadow had ever hosted —

“Yah, Park Ji-yeon — what are you even SAYING?! Song Ji-yeon-ssi and I are completely innocent.”

Ji-yeon’s eyes narrowed into two satisfied slits.

“…Then why is your face that red, unnie?”

Bo-ram’s skin ran pale by default. In the low firelight, lit warmly from below, it had now gone the exact red of a boiled shrimp — collarbone to ears, no patches spared.

Panic arrived. A hand flew up to cover the evidence.

“I— just now— I choked. On the meat. Yes. The choking. That’s the red. Choke-red.”

Uncovered, it might have passed for firelight. Covered, it became a confession with a signature on it — the kind of denial that arrives carrying its own notarized proof.

By this point, both Ham Eun-jung and Lee Qri had quietly completed their own readings.

Qri’s eyes stayed on the crimson eldest, narrowing into something patient and forensic.

Her working theory had run, until tonight, to a group total of exactly one: Eun-jung. The evidence there was circumstantial but voluminous. But the model was wobbling. Ji-yeon chased the man around calling oppa, day in and day out, and had never once filed a complaint about the hugs that resulted. Eun-jung was, if anything, the more direct case — the atmosphere around those two had long since stopped pretending to be ordinary. And now here was Bo-ram-unnie, combusting over one joke and one piece of hanwoo.

…Don’t tell me all three of them have something going on with this man.

Her gaze slid sideways to the individual under investigation, and settled in, customs-officer patient, looking for the tells.

Eun-jung, for her part, was mildly startled by Bo-ram-unnie’s reaction — and then, a breath later, not startled at all. Oppa was, by any honest measure, an exceptional man. A girl developing feelings over him required no investigation; it required only a pulse.

Besides — her own file was sealed. Nothing had been announced, nothing confirmed. As far as her members knew, Song Ji-yeon remained exactly what the market said he was: a diamond, single, freely circulating.

The subject of two separate investigations, meanwhile, was aware of neither. Wedged happily in the middle of the bickering, he was busy laughing. Ji-yeon’s teasing had bounced off him without leaving a mark.

He and Jeon Bo-ram, honestly speaking, were not close. If he had ever set out to farm T-ara’s bonds on purpose, the campaign map was obvious — start from the dragon, whose golden card had already rung the bell — not from an unbound Bo-ram who had never once pinged the Handbook’s radar.

So: a woman teased about a man, turning red. A strong reactor, nothing more. A high-amplitude blush.

If she actually liked me, the Goddess Handbook would have rung the bell weeks ago.

He shook his head, reached over, and relieved Ji-yeon of the tongs, shooing the dragon off the grill station with one arm.

“That’s enough, Ji-yeon — go eat. I’ll take the meat from here. Apparently meat can’t shut your mouth for you.”

“Yah, oppa! Now you’re protecting her!” The pout deployed at full depth. She and Qri-unnie had been in mid-flow — a once-in-a-camping-trip comedic run — and here came the boss, breaking up the show right at the punchline. She still had at least two jabs loaded.

Not that she read anything strange into Bo-ram-unnie’s blush. The unnie was the shy type; teasing a shy woman until she ripened was cause and effect, chapter and verse.

So she banked the interruption and fired one more shot on her way off the station.

Bo-ram went red all over again — and this time simply lowered her head and studied her own bowl like it owed her an apology.

“No. Nothing so noble.” He was already arranging the meat with a professional’s economy. “I just did the math. One more monologue and everything on this grate turns to charcoal.”

Charcoal fires run hot and temperamental — fat drips, flames surge and sink, and the person holding the tongs has to actually watch. A distracted novice narrating a comedy routine was not going to catch the flip window. Better his hands.

Otherwise, on current trends, somebody here would be making close personal acquaintance with the campsite restrooms around two in the morning, and he already knew which somebody the group vote would name.

✦ ✦ ✦

With him on the tongs, the grill’s throughput roughly doubled.

And an outdoor barbecue has its sacraments. Hyo-min fished chilled beer cans out of the cooler — crack went the first tab — and poured a cup for everyone around the table.

“To our great escape from the sea of suffering — and to Song Ji-yeon-ssi, formally installed as our Sajangnim! Geonbae!”

“Geonbae!”

The clinks rang out crisp and glass-clean across the quiet camp.

It stayed at the level of ceremony — sips, not rounds. This was a campsite, not a living room; what they were drinking was the leisure, not the liters.

Friday night, thin crowds by design, and after dark the enormous campground emptied out to a hush. Their site was the only one burning warm yellow light for a hundred meters — charcoal murmuring, sparks drifting up now and then, and the laughter of six women threaded through all of it. It looked, from any distance, like the warmest coordinate in the county.

The smoke went up heavy and sweet, and everyone ate like the food was going to be outlawed.

Though for all Ji-yeon’s famine talk, the actual consumption by the women ran modest. Idol figure management is not a habit — it is firmware, flashed at the factory, running in the background on every plate. Calories counted themselves.

Which meant the majority of the provisions, by weight, found their final resting place in the boss’s stomach. The ledger of the evening would show one line item: Song Ji-yeon, catering-grade intake.

Full-bellied and battle-satisfied, the table wound down. Ji-yeon had achieved a state of total collapse in her folding chair — no image management remaining anywhere in her body — patting her stomach and burping with the civic confidence of a retired fisherman.

“All right. Stop melting out of the chair. Up — walk it off a little, and help me get this table cleared.”

He stood and began stacking the empty plates.

Eun-jung came over quickly and lifted the greasy stack out of his hands before it could become his problem.

“I’ve got those. You stood at the fire half the evening — go sit somewhere and recover. Hyo-min, come give me a hand.”

Camp dinners run on disposables — bag it, bin it, done. Light work.

So he complied, carried a camp chair out to an open patch of lawn, and let the mountain wind have him for a while. Idle minutes. A rare currency.

Hyo-min gathered up an armload of plates, and on her way past Eun-jung, delivered one deliberate little shoulder-bump — eyes flicking, mid-collision, toward the man on the lawn — and a whisper pitched for an audience of exactly one.

“Unnie. Aren’t you going to go keep Song Ji-yeon-ssi company? I’ve got this covered.”

Eun-jung’s ears went warm on schedule. The soft scold arrived half a beat late.

“Stop talking nonsense. Work.”

Hyo-min froze mid-step, plates balanced, world briefly tilting.

…Hadn’t those two been circling each other since forever? What happened? What is THIS now? Did the dragon lie to me?

No. Impossible. The dragon exaggerated — certainly, hourly — but lied, never.

Eun-jung was not waiting around for the answer to settle. She hefted the garbage bag and made an early, discreet, high-speed exit toward the bins — genuinely afraid of what question might come out of that mouth next, and in what volume.

The wreckage cleared, the six of them spread a giant ground sheet over a flat stretch of lawn well away from the stream.

No neon out here, no light-kill from the city — the sky ran clean and enormous, scattered with stars like a fistful of loose diamonds thrown across black velvet.

So they gave up on shoes entirely, flopped down where they pleased, heads this way and that, and let the sky have all of them.
