Chapter Sixty

Talk About a Freebie Bonanza

MONDAY NIGHT Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Cheongdam Daewoo Royal County — Unit 201 · the dinner table

“Eating is fine. But eating only is boring.”

Park Ji-yeon drained her champagne, and her eyes went around the table with a fresh and distinctly alarming sparkle.

“Let’s play a game!”

“A game.” Park So-yeon looked at her the way one looks at a known recidivist. “You lose worst every single time we play.”

The record did support this. Park Ji-yeon was that rarest of competitors: allergic to winning, addicted to playing. Every gathering, she would invent some exotic new contest — and every time, the executioner’s hood ended up on her own head. The woman was a walking game black hole. Gravity, event horizon, the works.

“That’s just bad luck!! Tonight my luck is at its absolute peak — it’s my housewarming! King’s Game. Come on!”

Bo-ram and Hyo-min were in before she finished the sentence. Qri made no commitment either way and sipped her champagne like a woman attending the theater. Song Ji-yeon had no objection — he wasn’t much of a drinker by habit, but he held his liquor well enough.

Eun-jung, though, was visibly torn.

“But we don’t have any cards. How do we play the King’s Game without cards?”

Ji-yeon burst out laughing — a laugh carrying far too much delight, eyes shining with pure premeditation.

She shot up from her chair, crossed to the kitchen, opened the refrigerator, and came back cradling a plastic bag with a six-pack of beer in it. Onto the table it went. She reached in around the cans — and produced a brand-new deck of playing cards.

“Ta-da! There. Now we have cards.”

Song Ji-yeon laughed too. Premeditated. Fully, gloriously premeditated.

Ji-yeon cracked the seal, fished out six number cards — one through six — plus the big joker for the crown, shuffled them into a face-down row, and dealt.

“One card each! Whoever draws the joker — is the King!”

The first round’s luck ran exactly to script: the joker landed in the housewarming girl’s hand.

“Hahaha! See?! Tonight I reign!” She brandished the joker, cleared her throat with enormous ceremony, and delivered the royal decree in a voice two sizes too grand. “The King commands… Number Two and Number Five — a lovers’ toast! Arms hooked, glasses drained!”

Every head at the table bent to its own card.

Privately, Ji-yeon was already kicking herself. The crown had come home, yes — but nobody could see anyone else’s numbers, which meant she couldn’t aim the drinking at oppa and unnie with any accuracy. A design flaw in her own kingdom. Still — the game had many rounds. Sooner or later, the two of them would drink. She had all night.

Song Ji-yeon checked his card. A two of hearts.

“Who’s Two and Five? Show of hands!” Ji-yeon drummed the table.

“Five.” Lee Qri raised a hand, expression perfectly level.

“Two.” He raised his.

“Tch.” Ji-yeon’s disappointment was loud enough to have booked its own chair. She’d wanted oppa-plus-unnie — but oppa-plus-Qri-unnie was, at minimum, partial credit.

Song Ji-yeon took it in stride. He picked up the soju, filled his own cup, then filled Qri’s.

“Qri-ssi — please.”

She looked at him a moment, smiled, and rose with her glass. Her arm threaded through his with an elegance that belonged on a dance floor, and just like that the distance between them folded to nothing.

Her perfume arrived with it — cool, woody, quiet. Nothing sweet about it. A forest after rain.

She tipped her head back and drained the soju in one clean motion. He matched her, and both glasses came down empty.

“Good!” Ji-yeon applauded the wrong outcome with her whole heart.

The game rolled on. Round two crowned Park Hyo-min.

Those famous fox eyes made a slow, gleaming circuit of the table, and her smile arrived before her verdict did.

“The King commands… Number Three — feed a piece of fried chicken… to your favorite man at this table.”

The table contained exactly one man. The command had been custom-tailored, and everyone at the table knew for whom.

Eun-jung’s heart climbed into her throat and stayed there. She looked down at the chopsticks in her hand. A four.

“…I’m Three.”

A small, soft voice, wavering. Jeon Bo-ram, hand half-raised, that baby face carved entirely out of helplessness.

Ji-yeon nearly went over backward off her chair.

“Bo-ram-unnie! Quick! Feed our Song Ji-yeon-oppa his chicken! Hurry, hurry!”

Bo-ram’s face went scarlet in one smooth wave, ear to ear. She picked up a piece of chicken, stood, and crossed to him with her hand trembling faintly at the end of her arm.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi…”

He looked up into the face of a woman one breath from actual tears, found it somewhere between funny and heartbreaking, and solved the problem himself — leaning in, opening his mouth, and taking the chicken cleanly off her chopsticks.

“Thank you. Delicious.” Two chews, and a smile as mild as morning. “Really.”

Bo-ram looked like a woman pardoned mid-execution. She made it back to her seat at a dead sprint.

Song Ji-yeon surveyed the table, amused. Was this actually a drinking game? Because from where he sat, it was starting to feel like a freebie bonanza with a table wrapped around it — and himself as the sole beneficiary.

Round three, and the crown landed on him.

Every eye at the table converged. Park Ji-yeon’s most of all — she was winking at maximum voltage, projecting a full sentence through her eyeballs: oppa. You know what to do. Make unnie drink.

Song Ji-yeon, who did not in fact know what to do, looked at his joker and considered the kingdom.

“The King commands… Number One and Number Six — each of you, say one thing you admire about the other.”

“One!” Ji-yeon’s hand shot up instantly.

“Six.” So-yeon raised hers, unhurried.

Park Ji-yeon’s mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.

She turned to the King with the nakedest, most wounded puppy stare in her arsenal. oppa. Why won’t you read my heart.

He raised an eyebrow back at her, the picture of what-now. He genuinely could not decode the semaphore she was broadcasting — as far as he could tell, the girl had been blinking at him for medical reasons.

Ji-yeon arrived, privately, at a grim conclusion: this was the worst supporting cast she had ever been handed. Not one of them could run a play.

She and So-yeon traded one long, mutually resigned look — and then answered in perfect unison:

“Ji-yeon-ah: pretty — and legs that go on for days.”

“So-yeon-unnie: a voice like an angel — and she mothers the whole team.”

✦ ✦ ✦

Round upon round upon round, and the flush crept onto every face at the table.

Ji-yeon’s voice climbed steadily in volume until she was dragging Bo-ram into rock-paper-scissors for stakes no one had defined. Hyo-min and Qri had discovered a shared research interest in this season’s new handbags and were conducting a seminar. Eun-jung and So-yeon ate fried chicken with the quiet diligence of women who intended to stay sober — and it showed.

In other words: every soul at that table was bright-eyed except the one who had demanded the game in the first place. Watching the drunk maknae entangle Bo-ram in yet another doomed match, Song Ji-yeon finally understood the leader’s earlier verdict, delivered with such weary confidence. The girl’s luck was genuinely, cosmically bad — and her tolerance for alcohol played in a lower division still.

He was mid-observation when his shoulder was tapped.

He turned. Park So-yeon had materialized behind his chair, glass in hand, eyes clear.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. That matter of yours — buying T-ara. Where does it actually stand?”

He thought of Min-hyun’s report that morning, and gave the question the serious answer it deserved.

“Our legal department is already in contact with CCM Entertainment. I’d expect Kim Kwang-soo to sit down with you about the contract transfer next week.”

“And after that?”

“After that—” a nod — “I’ve had a new entertainment company registered. Dedicated to running the six of you, and nothing else. Early on, we warm the engines — multi-artist showcase concerts, online events, that ladder. And when the timing’s right, I’ll have a single custom-built for you. Cut for six voices. In Chinese.”

He paused, and let the apology into his voice.

“As for the market here at home — right now, I don’t have the right doors. But that’s temporary. Give me a year. Two at the outside. And I will hand you back everything that belongs to you.”

He said it plainly — no flourishes, no poetry — and it was exactly the plainness that made it land. Every still-sober girl at that table heard him. Not one of them was left unmoved.

“Good.” So-yeon lifted her glass. “Then on behalf of T-ara — thank you. In advance.”

“Yah!! How are you drinking without me — I want some too!!”

Ji-yeon’s head had long since gone to fog. She had registered precisely none of the business — only the dim visual of So-yeon-unnie toasting oppa — and she lurched toward the table with her glass out and her horizon tilting.

Eun-jung caught the swaying girl by the waist, reeled her in, and seated her firmly on her own lap.

“Ji-yeon-ah, be good. Your So-yeon-unnie is talking business with Song Ji-yeon-ssi.”

He watched the pair of them — the lap, the sway, the small pat Eun-jung gave the drunk girl’s back — and smiled. He raised his glass and touched it to So-yeon’s, light as a leaf.

“No thanks needed. We’re partners.”

The night kept deepening. The food on the table sank to wreckage; the bottles followed one another into retirement. And in the end, Park Ji-yeon never did escape the family curse — she went down face-first onto the table, mumbling something with no consonants in it, and was gone to the world.

Bo-ram had drunk her small face to a glowing pink and lay against Qri’s shoulder, eyelids at half-mast and losing altitude.

“That’s me done.” Hyo-min stood and shook her head slowly, like a snow globe. “Pictorial shoot tomorrow.”

“I’ll walk you down.” So-yeon rose with her.

“We should go too.” Qri gathered Bo-ram up with practiced arms.

“Get home safe.” Song Ji-yeon saw them all to the door.

And then the living room was down to three: him, the maknae sleeping like the dead, and Eun-jung, cheeks carrying a faint, warm flush.

“Ji-yeon, she…” Eun-jung looked at the girl sprawled across the table, helpless. “She always drinks like this?”

“Let her sleep.” He moved to lift the girl onto the sofa.

He’d barely touched her arm when she whimpered, burrowed, and mumbled from the far side of consciousness:

“Drink… Eun-jung-unnie… Song Ji-yeon-oppa, drink…”

Torn between laughter and surrender, he looked down at her. This little dragon. Unconscious — fully, certifiably unconscious — and still taking attendance on the drinking. What was a man even supposed to say to that.

The two of them managed the sofa transfer together — one under the shoulders, one under the knees — and a thin blanket went over her, tucked with more care than either of them put into words.

Done, they ended up standing in the middle of the living room. The room had gone very quiet, the way rooms do when a party has just left them.

“So… I should get going.” A glance at the time. Nearly eleven.

“I’ll walk you down.”

As if she’d decided something. She followed him to the entryway.

The porch light was dim and yellow. He changed his shoes, turned — and there she was, standing in the warm half-dark.

She’d had a little to drink too. Those eyes of hers, always so bright, had gone soft at the edges — glassed over, just barely, like the first mist rising off a lake. Looking at him. And looking, frankly… dangerous.

The elevator hummed them down to the first floor.

And stopped, at last, in front of Song Ji-yeon’s door.
