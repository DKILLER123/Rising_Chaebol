Chapter Twenty-Three

The Contact Reward

SATURDAY, SEPTEMBER 8 · NIGHT ICC Jeju — the east gate the nine-seater, door open

Song Ji-yeon looked into the van, at the beautiful chaos crammed inside it, and hesitated with one foot on the step.

The Starex seated nine. The middle row was occupied by Eun-jung and Park Ji-yeon, still mid-battle; in the rows behind them, several exquisite faces drifted in and out of the shadows. The air inside was light perfume layered over stage makeup, and it carried the distinct atmospheric pressure of a web with six very beautiful spiders in it and one fly doing the math on his odds.

“Wouldn’t I be — cramping things? I could take the—”

He pointed at the passenger seat up front. Three women made a full drama cast, and there were six in this vehicle. He had genuine concerns about leaving it alive.

“The front seat’s buried under support boxes and luggage. You’ll sit in the back.”

That was the manager, from the driver’s seat, twisting around to deliver a long look — the look of a man auditing exactly what manner of man Eun-jung would run out of a venue to meet.

Song Ji-yeon laughed the laugh of the doomed and climbed in.

Eun-jung, having finally managed to wedge Ji-yeon into the far seat, turned around and patted the empty spot beside her.

“Here. Sit.”

Her voice was about the size of a mosquito’s. Under the cabin’s warm yellow dome light, the flush that still hadn’t left her face was doing something deeply unfair to the entire vehicle.

He nodded and sat, obediently.

The instant he was down, the van went silent.

Five pairs of glittering eyes swung over and pinned him in place.

Two lifetimes had given him a hide thick enough to stop small arms fire, and even he briefly experienced himself as an exhibit in a glass case. He was no longer entirely sure he had boarded a van. It felt considerably more like the final audition round of a national casting program.

“Annyeonghaseyo, oppa~ I’m T-ara’s Ji-yeon!”

Ji-yeon broke the silence first, head popping out from behind Eun-jung’s shoulder, smiling like a small fox in possession of stolen goods, one playful wink delivered through the famous smoky eye makeup.

“Annyeonghaseyo. Ji-yeon-ssi.”

He returned the greeting politely while conducting a private internal review. Why was this little dragon acting like they went way back? They had never met. He was almost certain they had never met.

He never got the question resolved, because a sweet voice floated over the seatback.

“Annyeonghaseyo, I’m T-ara’s Bo-ram. Song Ji-yeon-ssi — your light board was very, very bright tonight. We could see it from the stage.”

Bo-ram had folded herself over the seatback, a baby face packed with curiosity, pushing her black-rimmed glasses up her nose, shoulders rounded inward — presenting, all told, as more maknae than the actual maknae.

He rubbed his nose, embarrassed, and glanced sideways at Eun-jung, who was studying her own knees with great scholarship. Ah. His file had circulated. The five of them knew exactly who he was — and the question of how they knew was one he would be taking up with a certain someone at a later date.

“Annyeonghaseyo, I’m So-yeon. Thank you for coming all the way to Jeju for us tonight.”

The leader’s register was steadier, with a half-bow delivered from the seat. Her gaze made one quiet circuit — and landed on his jacket, currently wrapped around Eun-jung’s shoulders. The corner of her mouth rose by exactly one meaningful degree, and she reached up to tuck a strand of hair behind her ear.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi is quite the attentive man, isn’t he?”

He had opened his mouth to demur in the standard fashion when the last two leaned in from the back.

“Annyeonghaseyo. I’m Hyo-min.”

The signature fox eyes ran him top to bottom, one eyebrow climbing — an appraisal conducted somewhere behind her eyes, verdict: the man was not bad at all.

“I’m Qri.”

Qri, the quietest, sat all the way in the corner and gave him one small nod. But that face — one of the great faces of the Peninsula, full stop — gave off a cool, titled, don’t-embarrass-yourself sort of radiance even in a dim van.

He worked through the introductions one at a time, gracious at surface level, hurricane inside.

So the forums were right. The all-visual group. On a screen, the six of them had been pretty, the way all top girl groups are pretty. Seated among them at arm’s length, the effect compounded like interest. Eun-jung — warm jade over steel, sweetness with a spine in it. Ji-yeon — aggression polished into precision. So-yeon — the dignified grande dame. Bo-ram — small, soft, roundable. Hyo-min — allure, wholesale. Qri — elegance all the way down to the marrow. Six faces, six completely separate kinds of beauty, and not one of them stamped out of the Peninsula’s idol assembly line.

In his old life he had sat through plenty of fourth- and fifth-generation stages, and the only face from any of them that had ever stuck in his memory was Bae Joo-hyun’s. The rest had arrived interchangeable and left the same way — zero hooks, nothing to hold.

A group with visuals at this level was a royal flush in any era of the industry.

But one thing puzzled him. Why — Eun-jung excepted — were they all looking at him like that? That blend of teasing, gossip-hunger, and something that could only be described as prospective mother-in-law inspecting the boyfriend. What was that?

Eun-jung-ssi. What, precisely, have you been telling these people about me?

Park Ji-yeon in particular looked one stamp and one ink pad away from engraving I FIND YOU VERY INTERESTING directly onto her own forehead.

He leaned sideways, voice dropped to a murmur near Eun-jung’s ear.

“Eun-jung. Have you been telling them I’m terrible?”

The breath of it tickled her neck. She shrank half a size, fingers strangling the hem of the jacket.

“I have not.”

Head down, ears pink. These teammates of hers had no decorum whatsoever. They were only friends — nothing declared, nothing decided — and now she was strongly considering living out the rest of her life in this oversized men’s jacket, face-first.

Ji-yeon observed the two of them, produced a small heh-heh, and tugged Eun-jung’s sleeve.

“Eun-jung-unnie. Why is your face red?”

“Yah, Park Ji-yeon — mouth. Shut.”

Eun-jung’s head came around like a cat whose tail had been trodden on.

He watched the show from the middle of it. These girls were a great deal tougher than he had been worrying they’d be — and a great deal louder.

He settled back into the seat and felt the van pull away, Jeju’s night sliding backward past the glass. Somewhere beside him, Ji-yeon tucked in her chin, stuck out her tongue in a display of perfect innocence, and — for once — actually shut up.

The Starex ran smooth and fast through the dark, tires hissing one clear, even note on the asphalt, the only sound in the night. The cabin’s warm light floated mirrored on the windows, with the wind and the black sea sealed outside.

✦ ✦ ✦

LATER THAT NIGHT Jeju International Airport the underground lot — and the last flight to Gimpo

The van glided down into the airport’s underground parking garage, and the noise level in the cabin collapsed on arrival. Tiredness climbed back onto every face, like a tide that had only been waiting for permission.

So-yeon was out first, off to square the logistics with the manager. The other five began the slow archaeology of their bags.

Eun-jung took off his jacket, folded it with care — sleeves in, corners square — and held it out.

“Thank you.”

Head down. Voice at a whisper’s volume.

He took it. It came back still warm from her.

“You’re welcome.”

He looked at her reddened ears, and left it there.

Ji-yeon’s head appeared over the backseat, winking at him.

“Oppa. Next time I come to your restaurant, ‘anytime’ had better still mean anytime.”

Somewhere between the venue and the parking garage she had extracted and assembled his complete file, and her curiosity about this man — this man who treated Eun-jung-unnie so differently from the rest of the planet — had only grown. A tiny demon was awake and stretching in her chest. She had already thought of something fun.

“Anytime. I mean it.”

He smiled it away. One dinner. For a man in his position, a rounding error.

The group crossed the terminal toward the gate. The late-night airport was nearly empty; the hollow hall carried nothing but their footsteps and the low roll of luggage wheels.

The manager walked point, strides long — a man trying to outpace the humiliation of the venue behind him. The six girls came after in a line, caps and masks on, heads down, expressions unreadable. Song Ji-yeon brought up the rear at a courteous remove: close enough to belong, far enough not to crowd.

The flight was a red-eye, economy half-empty, and to keep a low profile the manager had scattered their seats across the cabin. By some quiet conspiracy of the seat map, Song Ji-yeon’s and Eun-jung’s were side by side. Window side.

“Eun-jung-unnie, I want to sit with oppa. Swap with me.”

Ji-yeon was leaning on their headrests, wearing the grin of a known troublemaker.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“You have your own seat.”

“A seat is a seat. What’s wrong with mine?”

The two of them were off. The other four didn’t even look up. This was weather, not news: everyone knew Ji-yeon had no actual designs on the seat between them — she simply enjoyed winding her unnie up and watching her go. It was, at this point, one of the group’s daily sacraments.

But Eun-jung was no good-natured Bo-ram, and in the end Ji-yeon bowed before the iron fist and did not press her luck twice.

Having restored order, Eun-jung sat back down and said, in a low voice, for him only:

“Ji-yeon’s a live wire. Don’t mind her.”

“Mind what? If a pretty girl wants the seat next to mine, I’m the one profiting.”

Eun-jung went still. The corners of her mouth dropped flat — meaning: oh, I see. I’ve cost you your good fortune.

She turned her face to the window and stopped speaking to him.

He registered the miss roughly a second after landing it. Somewhere inside, he sighed. Rooms with this many women in them were, logistically, a nightmare.

He didn’t explain, though. They were friends — only friends — and over-explaining was how a man ended up on the back foot with nothing left in his hands. The current arrangement suited him fine.

The takeoff bumped and swayed, and Eun-jung’s hand found the armrest without asking her permission, knuckles paling one by one.

He glanced over. Said nothing.

The plane leveled into cruise, the cabin lights dimmed, and the rows around them settled into long, even breathing.

Eun-jung leaned her head back, tipped sideways. Apparently asleep.

But her brows stayed faintly drawn, and in the low light her lashes kept shivering.

He knew she wasn’t asleep.

What tonight had done to them was not the kind of thing a body metabolized in an hour. That black sea. The sensation of being lowered into it, carefully and by consensus, by the entire world at once.

He sighed, dug the eye mask and neck pillow out of his backpack, and — not using them himself — tapped her arm, lightly.

“Here. You’ll sleep better with these.”

She stiffened. Her eyes opened slowly, and looked at the things in his hand for a long, uncomprehending moment.

What was this — a stepstool? A way down for her? Fine, then. The seat business was provisionally forgiven.

She hesitated, then accepted.

He had no access whatsoever to any of this. The moment she took the mask and pillow, he tipped his own seat back and closed his eyes.

She put the mask on, adjusted the pillow, and settled.

The cabin went quiet.

Some uncountable while later, weight arrived on his shoulder.

He turned his head. At some point in the dark, Eun-jung’s head had come to rest there. Her breathing ran even; a small, tranquil smile had settled at the corner of her mouth. Genuinely asleep, this time.

She was still in her stage costume, and through the thin fabric he could feel the warmth of her, and a faint scent under it — shampoo, and the honest salt of a long day.

His body went rigid for exactly one second.

He wanted to move. Moving her might wake her.

He looked at the sleeping face under the mask — lips soft, slightly parted, every trace of the stubborn hold-the-line girl gone from it. In this light she looked breakable, like something you would carry with both hands.

In the end, he didn’t move.

He shifted a few degrees instead, found an angle that let her lean steadier, and let the night go by.

Her breath reached his collarbone through his shirt, warm, even, one wave after another. It tickled. His thoughts, however, were nowhere in the neighborhood of softness and warmth — because into the middle of it dropped the Handbook’s notification chime, and his eyelids jumped, hard, twice.

Goddess Handbook — Contact

EventContact with Ham Eun-jung

Goddess AbilityTriggered — successful

Reward IssuedStock Forecast Card ×1

A Stock Forecast Card — genuinely good merchandise, whatever else you could say about the system. One card, one stock, a full week’s forecast: the rise, and the moment to sell.

And his brow was knotting tighter the whole time he thought it.

Something didn’t fit.

He had already had close contact with Choi Sulli — contact of certified quality. At the barbecue restaurant he had patted her hair, at length, with technique. Measured on any axis you liked — intimacy, duration, sheer surface area in proximity — that outranked a sleeping head resting on a shoulder.

And the Handbook had sat there like a ten-year-old desktop with the fan on: nothing. Not a chime. Not a syllable. Let alone a contact reward.

He eased back a little, careful to keep his shoulder still, not to jolt her awake.

In his mind, he called up the handbook — the two-page one only he could see.

The system had always run cold and proud. Beyond the two small cards bearing Eun-jung’s and Sulli’s portraits, the thing had never volunteered one word more than it absolutely had to.

Two lifetimes of practice had made him a rigorous man. He laid the two samples side by side in his head and ran the comparison every direction it would go — and thought, and thought, and got nowhere at all.

“Still nothing. Would it kill this rattletrap to ship with a manual?”

He gave up and grumbled it into the dark, silently, thoroughly annoyed.

Mid-sulk, the weight on his shoulder lightened.

Eun-jung stirred, muzzy. Either the pillow had turned out harder than advertised, or that one instant of tension in his shoulder had traveled through and woken her.

She pulled the eye mask up.

Two blurry, sleep-soft gazes ran straight into his.

Two seconds later — with the delay of a satellite — the facts of the situation arrived. She had been leaning on him. All of her. Substantially all of her.

The oversized stage costume had traveled with her posture, and the neckline sat askew; in the dim cabin light, a stretch of snow-white below her collarbone was doing catastrophic things to the concept of incidental.

“Ah—”

The smallest gasp in the world, and she sprang back into her own seat as if the armrest had bitten her.

“S-sorry — Ji-yeon-ssi — I didn’t mean to—”

The voice came out soft and gluey with sleep, apology threaded all the way through it.

It had been too improper, was the thing. He had kindly lent her a neck pillow, and she had interpreted this as an invitation to climb him like a trellis. And the posture just now — the posture had been well past ambiguous.

He surfaced from his calculations, filed the neurotic handbook under later, and let his mouth curve. Then, with tremendous dedication to the bit, he kneaded his left shoulder and produced a long theatrical hiss.

“Eun-jung-ssi. Your head, I am sad to report, contains no hollow spaces whatsoever. I believe my shoulder has been repossessed.”

He kept kneading as he said it, wincing like a war veteran. Privately he was also marveling: she looked like she would blow away in a good wind, and the head weighed — what, half a ton? A bobblehead in the wild.

Eun-jung’s face went from pink to red at full engine speed, shame and indignation mixed in unknown proportions. She was an idol under permanent weight surveillance; no one in her life had ever once called her heavy. And of all the places — her head. The one subject she was touchy about.

She raised one soft pink fist and put it in the air, promising violence.

It stayed in the air. It remembered their first meeting — what one grab of her hand had done to this man’s arm — and decided, on balance, against manslaughter.

Her mouth went crooked with the injustice of it.

“Yah! Song Ji-yeon! I weigh forty-five kilos! My face is the size of a palm! And you’re calling my head heavy!”

He watched her, deeply entertained, and decided the teasing had reached its natural ceiling. The girl had been running on raw nerve since the Black Ocean; if he kept going and she cried, the crime would genuinely outweigh the joke.

“Okay, okay. Teasing. Retracted.”

He waved a hand and rolled the left shoulder loose.

“Honestly — it was light. Lighter than I expected. Have you been skipping meals on purpose?”

She blinked at him.

There was no impatience in his eyes, and no disdain — only the mild, satisfied gleam of a man who has just successfully teased the household pet and intends to do it again soon.

She understood, then, that she had been played one more time — and she still couldn’t model how the man was built. One side of him was bottomlessly good to her; the other was a public menace. He had once watched her eat herself into a food baby and pronounced it a medical condition. He had flown to an island to hold a light for her, and priced it as not wanting to lose money on the ticket.

She shook the whole tangle out of her head and glared at him.

“…My appetite’s been bad. Lately.”

“Bad appetite or not — you eat.”

Head against the seatback, watching her sideways.

“Back in Seoul, come to my place. I’ll make you something that opens it right up. Guarantee: you’ll finish it licking the plate.”

She burst out laughing.

This man. No plans, ever, at any hour, to be normal in conversation.

Although.

It was exactly the not-being-normal that made her feel she was still standing in the real world.

“Fine. But I want crayfish.”

She said it very small.

“No problem.”
