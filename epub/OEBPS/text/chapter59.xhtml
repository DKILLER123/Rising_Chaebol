MONDAY, LATE AFTERNOON Mapo-gu, Seoul SAVORY F&B — the president’s office

Song Ji-yeon shut the laptop, tipped his head back against the chair, and pressed two fingers into the ache between his brows.

Since the meeting had turned him loose, he had been planted in front of the market, all the way to the closing bell. Samsung Electronics had come off at almost exactly the moment he’d circled in his head; the cash had landed; and then the whole pile had gone straight back to work — every won of it, leverage and all, into Sewon Electronics.

₩5.2 billion of his own money. ₩5.2 billion borrowed on margin against it. ₩10.4 billion deployed at ₩14,200 a share — seven hundred thirty-two thousand-odd shares, give or take the crumbs.

Five trading days, per the card. Plus 37.8 percent. Run the tape out to its close, and the position came home at roughly ₩14.3 billion — a shade under ₩4 billion of profit, on one week, on one card.

Private Trading Log · Monday — the Sewon leg opens

Capital₩5,200,000,000 equity + ₩5,200,000,000 on margin

Deployed₩10,400,000,000 · ≈ 732,000 shares

Projected exit≈ ₩14,300,000,000 — profit ≈ ₩3,900,000,000

And now that he saw it laid out like that, stubbornly grinding on Samsung for three weeks had been a fog-brained move. If he’d cashed out the day that first Forecast Card landed in his wallet and put the money straight to work, the pile would have doubled by now. Minimum.

Live and learn. Then learn it again.

The phone rattled against the desk.

He picked it up. The sender’s saved name glowed at the top of the screen: the little dragon.

He looked at the screen a moment, and a smile got away from him.

He liked the little dragon, honestly — that full-throttle, zero-brakes temperament. And the stock was placed, the office had gone quiet, and his own apartment had been suspiciously un-dogged since a certain small ball of fluff had moved out with a certain someone. Nothing on the schedule tonight but an empty evening.

A LITTLE AFTER SIX Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Cheongdam Daewoo Royal County — Unit 201

He pocketed the phone, took the car keys, and went.

The Mercedes rolled into the Daewoo Royal County underground garage, and he didn’t go straight up. First, a stop at the gourmet grocer by the complex — a lap of the aisles, and then a bottle of champagne that cost enough to make the cashier’s eyebrows move. Only then did he ride the elevator up, card-swipe, second floor.

In Korea — per house rules his mother had drilled into him across an ocean and two decades — you did not show up at someone’s new home empty-handed. Back in LA, the woman had marched into every housewarming on the block armed with toilet paper and laundry detergent, like she was provisioning a bunker. He had at least upgraded the munitions. As for what one properly brings an idol at her housewarming, he had no idea at all — so he’d grabbed the universal answer: something expensive and faintly French.

He stepped out of the elevator, stopped in front of 201, and pressed the bell.

The door opened within seconds — and there was Park Ji-yeon’s grin, arriving half a beat before she did.

“oppa! You’re here!” She hauled the door wide and reeled him in on momentum alone.

The living room held all six of them. Bo-ram and Qri on the sofa; Hyo-min, So-yeon, and Eun-jung in and out of the open kitchen. The apartment was clean now — the morning’s mountain range folded back into actual geography — and the air carried a faint, sweet diffuser scent over the warm undertone of cooking.

When he walked in, the conversation dropped a half-beat. And then every eye in the room arrived on him at once.

“Congrats on the new place, Ji-yeon-ssi.” He held out the champagne. “Something small.”

“Wah — thank you, oppa!”

She received the bottle with both hands and real delight. Our oppa gets it. Sees the occasion exactly the way I do. Champagne borne toward the kitchen, brain already three scenes ahead to tonight’s programming — pity, really, about the roomful of lightbulbs.

“Song Ji-yeon-oppa, that armchair is yours.” A nod toward the seat across from Qri, wine bottle still in hand. “Sit, sit.”

“Thanks — go do your thing.” He settled into the armchair and gave the room a slow once-over. “All unpacked? It looks great in here.”

“Thanks to the unnies.” Ji-yeon bounced back out of the kitchen and dropped down beside Bo-ram. “Otherwise me and Eun-jung-unnie would’ve been boxing till three in the morning.”

Eun-jung heard her name, looked up from the counter — and smiled at him. Sweetly, unguardedly, straight on.

Four eyes met and held.

Song Ji-yeon’s heart performed a maneuver for which no insurance exists.

Let the record show: he was, had always been, and would remain a complete goner for Ham Eun-jung’s face. A certified, incorrigible visual stan — across two lifetimes. The eye contact had arrived with no warning whatsoever, and that smile walked directly into his chest, took its shoes off, and made itself at home.

“Yah! Ham Eun-jung! What are you staring at? The dishes won’t carry themselves!”

Hyo-min’s voice detonated out of the kitchen and blew the moment to pieces.

“Ne—! Coming!”

Caught red-handed, Eun-jung spun back to the counter at a speed that nearly counted as teleportation.

Song Ji-yeon found this extremely funny and kept it strictly off his face. Out of the corner of his eye, though, he could feel it — Qri’s gaze and Bo-ram’s gaze, shuttling back and forth between him and Eun-jung like spectators at a very slow tennis match.

Park Ji-yeon materialized at his elbow, a child presenting a report card, voice dropped to a stage whisper.

“oppa. Be honest. You’ve never seen this many beauties in one room before, right? Now — of the six of us, who do you think is the pretti—”

“Mmph!!”

Jeon Bo-ram’s hand arrived over the maknae’s mouth with the reflexes of a veteran. The tackle followed; Ji-yeon went down into the cushions under her, squirming and broadcasting through the fingers.

“Bo-ram-unnie! Why’re you covering my mouth! Let! Me! Talk!!!”

“Yah, Park Ji-yeon — this question. Again.” Bo-ram kept her pinned and turned an apologetic smile over her shoulder. “Song Ji-yeon-ssi, please don’t mind her. She’s young. She doesn’t know any better.”

He hadn’t caught the words, but reconstructing the crime took no detective. And there was no version of that question with a safe answer. Song Ji-yeon was many things, but he was not a clueless straight boy — standing in front of a room of women and ranking their faces, to those same faces, was a social maneuver he refused to execute under torture.

Mid-apology, the girl under Bo-ram erupted.

One heave, and the pin reversed — Bo-ram went down this time, with the maknae grinning above her.

“Bo-ram-unnie. I’m an adult. I stopped being ‘young’ a long time ago.”

Her hands made a dive for the two softest territories in reach. Bo-ram, refusing to lose on home soil, counterattacked at the waist with both hands — and inside three seconds the sofa had escalated well past anything a broadcast-standards meeting would clear.

Everything after that point, Song Ji-yeon would like the record to show, was strictly pay-per-view material.

He put a firm administrative lid on the warmth spreading through him and cut his eyes sideways at Qri. The glance carried a complete sentence: Are they always like this?

Qri sent back an apologetic look — a general who had long ago accepted that this particular soldier answered to no command structure. For the lawless maknae, she had no cure either.

“All right — enough, both of you. Dinner.”

From the open kitchen, Park So-yeon watched the two of them grappling in front of a guest and felt her eyebrow develop a tic. One evening. One single evening of basic ladylike composure in front of an outsider — was that so much to ask of this team?

The kitchen, meanwhile, had performed a miracle.

The table wasn’t large, and it was not remotely visible under the food. Eun-jung’s samgyetang, whole and golden. Hyo-min’s kimchi pancakes, crisp-edged and stacked. The instant galbitang So-yeon had excavated from God-knew-which moving box, rising steam and all. And presiding over everything like a coronation — Ji-yeon’s delivery order: two party boxes of fried chicken, a whole jokbal, and a large pizza, on the principle that a dinner with one fatal flaw was no dinner at all.

“Eat, eat! Everyone eat!” Ji-yeon conducted them into their chairs like traffic police.

She had, naturally, engineered the seating with the precision of a heist crew. Song Ji-yeon went to her right hand; and to his left, by a coincidence she had personally installed, sat Eun-jung. Neighbors.

Eun-jung sat, and her arm grazed his.

She snatched it back like she’d brushed a live wire, ears going quietly to flame. Song Ji-yeon himself, entirely unbothered, was already reaching for the chicken.

Ji-yeon clocked the whole exchange, nodded once to herself like a scientist watching an experiment behave, and rose to fetch the champagne from the cabinet. She had not forgotten tonight’s program.

“oppa! Let me open the champagne for you — I’ll perform it!”

She bore the bottle to her chest like a trophy, wrapped the neck in a towel exactly the way she’d seen it done in movies, and shook it with her whole body. Hard. Harder.

“Yah! Park Ji-yeon, have you lost your—”

BANG!

The cork left the bottle like anti-aircraft fire, rode a column of white foam straight into the ceiling, and brought the weather down with it. Champagne fell as rain across the corner of the table, and the three nearest — Ji-yeon, Song Ji-yeon, Eun-jung — were soaked before anyone could flinch.

“Aaah — my hair!!”

The living room descended into cheerful anarchy.

Song Ji-yeon moved first — tissues off the table and into Eun-jung’s hands before his own face got so much as a napkin. Only then did he wipe the champagne off his jaw.

“…Thank you.”

Eun-jung took the tissues and dabbed at her clothes with her head down, voice gone small. Her white T-shirt had acquired a fine polka-dot pattern of wine stains; she looked, if anything, a little heroically bedraggled.

Qri and Bo-ram had folded sideways into each other, wheezing. Hyo-min was hunting dry towels and scolding in the same breath.

“Park Ji-yeon — are you demolishing the new apartment on its first day?!”

Order was eventually restored, at the cost of half the bottle. Ji-yeon stuck out her tongue, poured for everyone, and raised her glass.

“To my new home — and to the unnies’ heroic labor! Cheers, everyone!”

Glasses rose all around the table. Even Song Ji-yeon lifted his and touched the ring.

“Congrats, Ji-yeon.”

“Thank you, oppa!”

And the meal began — loud, crowded, and warm, in the way of a table where nobody has to pretend.
