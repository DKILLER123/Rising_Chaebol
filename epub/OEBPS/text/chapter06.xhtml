Chapter Six

Loyalty

SEPTEMBER 2012 Asan Medical Center, Seoul Discharge — mid-morning

Getting discharged took less than ten minutes.

A nurse handed him a slip to sign, tacked on the standard “get some rest, eat your meals on time,” and he nodded, signed, handed it back, and walked out through the main doors of Asan Medical Center.

It took the cold wind on the hospital steps for him to remember one small problem: his car was still sitting in the securities exchange parking lot.

He fished out his phone on the steps, intending to text Sulli that he’d been discharged — and made it all the way through his contacts list before realizing they had never actually exchanged numbers. They weren’t even connected on KakaoTalk.

So much for helping the girl. I don’t even have a way to reach her.

He stood there for a couple of seconds and then, despite himself, laughed out loud.

So the peach had stood guard over him for two hours — and then walked away without leaving so much as a phone number.

He found a random all-you-can-eat barbecue joint near the hospital, ate until the hollow in his stomach forgave him, and flagged a taxi out to Yeouido to collect the car. In the exchange parking lot he found the black Mercedes, walked a full circle around it to confirm no ticket on the windshield, then dropped into the driver’s seat and started the engine.

Hands on the wheel, his eyes drifted up to the rearview mirror and the stock exchange building shrinking in it.

Samsung would open for trading tomorrow. No way to know whether Sulli had actually bought in. The thought made him huff a laugh — she’d personally delivered him to a hospital, and the two of them still didn’t have each other’s numbers.

He was, honestly, a little appalled at himself. He’d had a whole hospital-bed conversation with the girl and never once thought to ask. Now he couldn’t reach her if he wanted to.

“Aigoo. Who knows when I’ll see her again.”

SEPTEMBER 2012 · EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment

He made it back to his place in Cheongdam-dong around dusk, washed his face, changed into clean clothes, and had just sunk onto the sofa when his phone buzzed.

A message from Park Seon-young, SAVORY’s planning director.

KakaoTalk · Park Seon-young (Planning Dept.)

Park Seon-young

Sajangnim, hiring is fully wrapped up. 182 new employees start their trial shifts tomorrow — how would you like the onboarding training arranged?

He held the phone in one hand and thought it over. Renovations on all seven stores were in their final pass, the kitchen equipment was fully installed, and the supply chain had been running more or less smooth for a week. If the new staff started training tomorrow, three days would be plenty — service flow, a briefing on the broth bases, ingredient knowledge, hygiene standards, greeting etiquette. One hard pass through all of it, and the doors would open on the fourth.

He typed his reply.

KakaoTalk · Park Seon-young (Planning Dept.) · continued

Song Ji-yeon

Training starts tomorrow. Three days of it, doors open on the fourth. I’ll come by tomorrow morning and set out the program myself.

Park Seon-young

Understood, Sajangnim. Ah — one thing. We’ve been getting calls asking about opening-day reservations. Should we open bookings early?

Song Ji-yeon

Oh? Bookings already? Sounds like the PR team’s ₩100 million didn’t go to waste.

Park Seon-young

Ne — mostly university students booking private rooms for birthdays. The party setup service, the custom cakes, the staff-sung birthday celebration, the free manicures — all your ideas, Sajangnim, and they’ve really caught on with the students. We haven’t even opened and four private rooms are already spoken for.

Song Ji-yeon

Haha. Then bookings open tomorrow. Old Park, you silver tongue — I can tell you’re a man destined for big things.

Song Ji-yeon

One more thing. The Apgujeong store in Gangnam — reserve me a private room, 5:30 p.m. on the fourth.

Park Seon-young

Yes, Sajangnim.

Song Ji-yeon

Work hard. When this company gets big, you’ll be director over all of Seoul.

Park Seon-young

Ne — LOYALTY!!!

Reading that last one, Song Ji-yeon’s mouth curved despite himself. Park Seon-young would bow to a vending machine if it complimented his tie. But the man could sell, and he hadn’t forgotten the promise to Eun-jung either — the room at Apgujeong was locked in.

He set the phone down and let himself sink deep into the sofa, head tipped back against the warm yellow of the ceiling light strips.

Three days to opening. Whether ONGI landed its first punch would be decided that day, and that day only.

Eyes closed, he ran the whole opening-day flow through his head: kitchen output speed, front-of-house traffic lines, snacks and drinks in the waiting area, the independent AC settings in the private rooms — he even got as far as the fragrance in the restrooms. The service-first hotpot playbook he’d studied half to death back in LA, the one Haidilao had used to conquer every dining scene it touched — he knew it cold. He could reproduce it blind.

The only question was whether the playbook would catch fire here on the Peninsula, in this year, at this moment.

He stared at the ceiling. The living room was enormous; a two-hundred-square-meter flat honestly felt a little hollow for one person.

He looked around and decided the place was, objectively speaking, a little extravagant. Converted to operating cash, it would keep ONGI breathing for at least a year.

“Worst comes to worst, I mortgage this place.” He laughed at himself, then shook his head. “Look at me. Planning the retreat before the first battle’s even started.”

SEPTEMBER 2012 · NEXT MORNING Mapo-gu, Seoul SAVORY F&B headquarters

The next morning, after breakfast, Song Ji-yeon washed his dishes, changed into a dark-gray knit sweater, and headed out.

By mid-morning the streets of Cheongdam-dong were already stirring — luxury boutiques unlocking their doors, staff wiping down the display windows.

He pulled the black Mercedes out of the underground garage and drove toward Mapo.

SAVORY F&B sat on a street in Mapo that couldn’t be called wide, across from a barbecue joint that had held the corner for a dozen years, next door to a boutique café. The building wasn’t big — three floors — but it was clean and crisp. Gray-white walls, a dark-gold signboard hung above the entrance. It looked the part.

He parked in the public lot across the street, killed the engine, and stayed where he was a few moments longer, studying that dark-gold sign through the windshield.

SAVORY F&B — the wordmark sat square and dignified, one small line of Korean lettered beneath it. Tasteful. Neither shouting nor begging.

He sat in the car for about three minutes before he finally pushed the door open.

Nine o’clock. The morning sun slanted low across the facade and laid a thin film of gold over the gray walls. He pushed through the glass doors, and Park Seon-young — standing at the front counter checking something on a tablet — hurried over at once.

“Sajangnim. You’re here.”

“Well? Everything ready?” He glanced around the hall.

“Kitchen stock arrived this morning. The fifty-six head chefs have already walked their crews through the whole line — no issues. One hundred forty servers, all present and accounted for.” Park fell in at his shoulder with the report.

“Good.” He nodded. The fifty-six head chefs were the least of his worries — the broth bases at a service-first hotpot chain were no state secret; anyone who knew the recipe could reproduce them. “Ten o’clock this morning, gather the training team in the main hall. I’m walking them through the program myself.”

Park blinked.

“Ten? You’ll do it personally?”

“I will.” A small smile. “I’m teaching it myself.”

At ten sharp, six members of the training team stood in a row. All of them young — the oldest twenty-six, the youngest freshly twenty — in clean work uniforms, standing straight.

Song Ji-yeon stood in front of them without a trace of boss posture, and his voice stayed level.

“You all learned the basic reception flow in your own training. Thirty-degree bow, hot towel, tea service. I don’t need to repeat any of that. What I want to talk about today is something else.”

He walked over to one of the tables, sat down in a guest’s chair, and looked up at the female staffer named Park Min-suk.

“Min-sook. You’re me now. I’m the guest. Come show me how you receive me.”

She was visibly nervous, but she came forward and bowed, textbook-perfect.

“Welcome! How many will you be today?”

“Two. I’m waiting for someone.”

“Of course, this way please.” She gestured him toward a window seat. “Please have a seat. Here are your hot towels and menus — your tea will be right out.”

He nodded, took the towel, wiped his hands, and looked up at her.

“Now. Did you notice any needs I had just now?”

She hesitated.

“You were… waiting for someone…”

“Right. I was waiting for someone. But you never offered me a drink to start, and you never mentioned you could bring something little to snack on while I wait. So I sit here with nothing. Ten minutes of that, fine. Twenty? Thirty? And if a guest brings a date, and she sits there getting more and more bored — you think that man ever comes back?”

The six of them traded glances.

He stood up and clapped his hands once.

“What they taught you in training is ‘standard procedure.’ What I need you to learn is to see the person. The first look a guest gives you when he walks in, you read it: is he here to eat, here to talk business, or here on a date? A family with kids, a grandmother, a man with his girlfriend — every single one of them needs something different.”

“Kids come in — a small dish of non-spicy nuts or fruit goes out first, before the whining starts. Elderly guests — ask before they have to: would they like a softer sofa seat, is the air-conditioning too cold. A man brings a date — you steer them toward the dishes that photograph beautifully, and you can even tell him which plate will look best on camera.”

He paused, letting it settle.

“You’re probably thinking this is all very trivial. I’m telling you it isn’t. Every small thing you do could be the thing that brings one guest back one more time. The hundred and forty servers are yours now. Three days from now, the people you’ve trained had better not disappoint me.”

“Ne, Sajangnim!”

He left it there and sent them off. A boss’s job was to set the standard and name the bar. The carrying-out belonged to the people under him.

Otherwise, what was he paying a training department for?
