Chapter Forty-Seven

It Was Her

LATE THAT NIGHT Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment

By the time Ji-yeon made it back to the apartment, the night had gone properly deep. He dropped the hoodie on the sofa, snapped one photo — himself and one white fluffball mid-trot, leash taut, streetlights behind them — and sent it to Eun-jung.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Song Ji-yeon

[ photo: one CEO, one fluffball, the streetlights of Cheongdam-dong ]

Today’s dog-walking mission: complete!

No reply came. He gave it a while, then set the phone face-down and stopped waiting — the girls were out of the country for the Hong Kong concert, and this stretch of the run was the busiest of all, rehearsal eating every hour that wasn’t a stage.

He showered off the evening, toweled his hair half-heartedly, fell into bed, dragged the duvet over his head, and was gone.

A night without a single dream.

✦ ✦ ✦

THE NEXT MORNING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment

Morning arrived as something warm and damp, pressed against his cheek.

He frowned, rolled over, and attempted to shove the interruption out of his dream. The interruption was persistent. It wormed its way back, leaned in, and licked his cheek once, thoroughly, like a stamp on an envelope.

His eyes snapped open — directly into a dog’s face at point-blank range.

Mushroom.

Two round black eyes, not blinking, not even slightly.

“Woof!”

Now that the man was awake, the dog announced it officially, and both front paws landed on his chest like a drumroll.

It took his brain the regulation two seconds of reboot to recover the situation. Dog. Temporary. His responsibility.

“Off.” One hand, attempting to relocate the furball.

The hand was interpreted as an invitation. The tongue found his knuckles; then the entire animal began to bounce on him, spring-loaded, joy deployed at every landing point.

His chest was being trampled by a very small, very cheerful mattress, and his sleep did not survive the second pass.

“Aish — you. Settle. Down.” He tried to sit up. Mushroom sat down first — squarely, on his stomach — and tipped his head at him, the very portrait of innocence.

One day. The little guy had been here exactly one day, and had already ruled the house settled: his couch, his human, his terms.

He took Mushroom gently by the scruff and relocated him to the edge of the mattress.

“Stay on the bed. You do not come down.”

Mushroom watched him walk away, tail sweeping the duvet. Then he barked once — brightly, like a promise — launched off the bed, and landed, four-square and deeply pleased with himself, at Ji-yeon’s feet.

…He had nothing left. Arguing with a dog before coffee was a losing genre.

“Fine. Fine! You win. I’m scared of you. Happy?”

In the bathroom, Mushroom attached himself to his ankles like a shadow with a wag. Brushing his teeth: the dog supervised from the doorway. Washing his face: a cold nose introduced itself to his calf. In the mirror, a man with a war-zone hairline and last night still in the corners of his eyes stood above an immaculately perky little dog, and the man sighed first.

“Eun-jung. You really did find me the perfect job.”

He padded to the kitchen and got out milk and bread. Mushroom took up his post at the kitchen doorway and produced, from somewhere in that small chest, a lament of expertly measured tragedy.

“Looking at me is useless. Your owner left explicit instructions — no people food.”

He tore off a corner of bread anyway, crouched, and dangled it one inch from the twitching nose.

The nose twitched. The tongue deployed.

The hand retreated — and the bread went into his own mouth. Two chews. Thoughtful.

“Want some?”

Nodding. Frenzy-level nodding, tail a metronome at maximum tempo.

“No.”

Mushroom’s inner monologue: I may not be a person, it’s true. But you, sir, are an actual dog.

He finished his breakfast at a leisurely pace and fired off a belated reply to the message Eun-jung had sent at whatever hour of the night rehearsal had finally released them — while the dog ran the full program of a sympathy campaign around his legs. A head pressed against his pant cuff. A paw hooked over his shoe. The only thing missing was standing upright and weeping into his slacks.

Last bite swallowed, last sip of milk gone, he finally ambled to the sofa, excavated the dog food from the mountain of supplies Eun-jung had left, and — following the sticky note to the letter — measured half a cup into the bowl.

One sniff of the familiar stuff, and Mushroom defected from the sympathy campaign without notice. He crossed the kitchen at a dead sprint and put his whole face in the bowl.

Ji-yeon listened to the sound of enthusiastic inhalation and laughed once.

“No shame at all. None.”

Mushroom’s rebuttal: Easy for you to say. Somebody skipped my dinner last night.

Breakfast concluded, man and dog regarded each other across the living room.

He had been planning a return trip to bed. Mushroom had other legislation pending: the little ball arrived at his feet, was deposited there, was retrieved when ignored, and was deposited there again — every cycle performed with eyes of flawless, bottomless innocence. Get up, human. Play.

They held eye contact for thirty seconds.

He lost.

“Okay, okay, we’ll play. We’ll play — anything for a quiet life.”

He accepted his fate, picked up the ball, and conducted the ancient, sacred rite of fetch across the living-room floor.

Sunlight came through the floor-to-ceiling windows and scattered across the boards, and a man and a dog chased each other through the patterned light — and it was, against every forecast, harmonious.

A thought drifted through, mild as the light itself: with the little guy around, the apartment wasn’t nearly so quiet a place anymore.

✦ ✦ ✦

One full morning of wits versus dog, and the man was the one worn down to the axles.

This wasn’t pet ownership. This was a live-in ancestor, requiring daily attendance.

By the afternoon he lay on the sofa playing dead with total commitment — by then fully immune to every application of the ball against his ankle — and at that precise moment, the phone rang. An unfamiliar number. He dredged his memory for a second before it surfaced: the owner of Old Chen’s building.

Phone · the owner of 312 Cheongdam-dong → Song Ji-yeon

“Annyeonghaseyo, Song Ji-yeon-ssi. This is the owner of 312 Cheongdam-dong. You do remember me, right?”

“I remember. You’re back in the country.”

“Ne — I landed this morning. Is now a good time? I’d like to meet and talk about the storefront transfer.”

“Now works. Where should we— actually, why not come to us? Our office is in Mapo. I’ll have coffee waiting.”

A pause on the line — long enough to hear a decision being weighed at the other end.

“Eo… would you mind coming to my side of town instead? I’d rather not go far today.”

“Of course. Your call entirely.”

“Then — two o’clock this afternoon. There’s a café called Dusk, in Gangnam.”

“Got it. I’ll be there on the dot.”

He hung up and looked down at the dog, who was once again attempting to negotiate with his ankle using the ball.

He scooped Mushroom up and installed him in the dog bed.

“Little ancestor. Play at home. I have business.”

Head tilt. Maximum innocence. The signature move.

He changed his clothes, pocketed the car keys, and evacuated the apartment at a pace just short of fleeing.

The air of the outside world. It smelled like freedom.

✦ ✦ ✦

1:50 PM Gangnam-gu, Seoul Dusk café

He was ten minutes early.

He took a window seat, ordered an iced americano, and scrolled his phone with half a heart. The café played jazz at conversation-friendly volume, the customers murmured in twos and threes, and the afternoon ran along on oiled rails.

He managed two sips of the americano — yanyak strained over ice, delightful — grimaced, and pushed the cup to a safer distance.

Two o’clock, sharp.

The chime over the café door rang out, bright and clear.

A woman came in: a beige trench coat, sunglasses, a beret. Tall and slender, with the kind of presence that quietly rearranged a room — heads turned, conversations lost their footing.

He glanced up too, the way anyone would.

She paused just inside the door and surveyed the café — and found exactly one table in the whole room occupied by a person sitting alone. The smallest hitch of consideration. Then she came toward him, unhurried, heels ticking a clean rhythm on the wooden floor. Tak. Tak.

A small alarm went off in the back of his mind.

This landlord is far too young. And that aura — that is not a rent-collector’s aura. That’s a leading lady’s.

She settled into the seat across from him, reached up, and took off the sunglasses.

It was a face that required no introduction. Not on this Peninsula, not anywhere. And not in his old life in LA, either.

CLATTER.

The coffee spoon hit the saucer.

Somewhere in the middle of his skull, a bomb went off.

Why was she here?

Unless—

An absurd hypothesis rose up out of the smoke.

No. No way. She was Old Chen’s landlord?
