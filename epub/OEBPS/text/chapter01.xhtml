Chapter One

Oh No, I’m the Creep

SEPTEMBER 2012 Apgujeong-dong, Seoul a side street, after dark

THE QOO · Life Advice — anonymous

1Has anyone here ever been mistaken for a pervert? If you have, please advise: what exactly are you supposed to do once you’ve been caught? Asking for a friend. Waiting online… URGENT!!!

At this precise moment, Song Ji-yeon was face-down on the pavement, right arm wrenched behind his back by a girl in a black hoodie — face mask, baseball cap, not one square centimeter of skin on display.

Her free hand held a convenience-store bag crammed with, frankly, everything — chips, cup ramen, banana milk — and, most importantly, a six-pack of beer.

Ji-yeon had zero interest in learning what a beer bottle felt like against the back of his skull. Discretion is the better part of not getting concussed. He abandoned his dignity on the spot and used his one free hand to shield his head.

“Misunderstanding! It’s all a misunderstanding! I’m not a pervert!”

Heaven as his witness — he was not a pervert.

Somebody walk him through the logic, though. It’s nighttime. You see a girl bundled up like she’s dodging the paparazzi. Who follows that? What is there to follow — a silhouette?

In any case, Song Ji-yeon — Regressor Class, returned to the Peninsula of 2012, long-term career objective chaebol — did not have spare hours in his schedule for stalking anyone.

Right. Context.

Song Ji-yeon was a transmigrator. Regressor, if you prefer — one life ended, another queued up from the very beginning.

The body he’d inherited was also named Song Ji-yeon, born 1990, both parents alive and annoyingly healthy. His father, Song Min-seok, was a Korean-American restaurateur who had spent two decades in Los Angeles building SAVORY, a small restaurant group — and who had recently pointed the family business across the Pacific. Seven locations now, spread across half the districts of Seoul. Not chaebol money, but the kind of comfortable that costs two parents twenty years of six-day weeks.

Reborn into a setup like that? There was no version of him that wasted the second run. You land on the Peninsula and don’t become a chaebol? That’s just poor resource management.

So once he’d got his bearings — new old life, new old parents — he pulled an all-nighter drafting a business proposal and dropped it on his father’s desk the very next morning.

The pitch did exactly one thing: bring the extreme-hospitality hotpot model to Korea. He’d grown up inside a family that worshipped service, and he’d studied the industry’s master class in it — Haidilao, the service-first hotpot empire — until he could recite its playbook in his sleep. In 2012 it was still a China-only kingdom. The overseas map was blank paper.

Song Min-seok found it strange — this son, who until a month ago had treated the family business as scenery — but strange in the best way. He weighed it for roughly a day, then green-lit the whole plan on the spot. Overnight, Ji-yeon became president of the Korean operation: SAVORY F&B, named by his father after the first little diner they’d run in LA.

The company occupied a three-story building in Gangnam — ten departments, a little over two hundred employees. The restaurants themselves were an old, settled brand with a decade of routine baked into their bones; procurement, scheduling, accounting all ran on rails. His actual job description was simpler than it looked: pivot the menu from Sichuan entrées to hotpot, renovate the dining rooms, and bolt on the service doctrine of a company about ten years ahead of its time.

He’d even hung a new name over the doors — ONGI. “Warmth.” Let the branding consultants chew on that.

One frantic month later, all seven ONGI hotpot houses were dressed and ready for inspection.

The moment word came down, Ji-yeon drove straight to the nearest one — Apgujeong — to see the finished work with his own eyes.

✦ ✦ ✦

He’d barely stepped out of the car when a girl emerged from the convenience store next door — cap, mask, fully incognito, hauling a giant bag of snacks.

And — what were the odds — they were headed the same way. Fate was clearly in a mood tonight.

Halfway down the street, the girl kept glancing back at him. Over and over. Strange looks. Ji-yeon didn’t think much of it. Obviously she was sneaking peeks because he was handsome. There was no other explanation. Sometimes, he reflected, being this handsome was a genuine burden.

So he strolled along behind her, and the next time she turned around, he flicked his bangs out of his eyes and squared his shoulders like a man posing for a cologne ad.

The display requires no further narration.

What he did not expect was for the girl to whirl like she’d been waiting all night for precisely this — one blur of motion, and his right arm was locked behind his back, his cheek formally introduced to the pavement.

Yes. This is where we came in.

His brain ran the aftermath math and arrived at the obvious conclusion: she thinks I’m following her. She thinks I’m a pervert.

So the glances back hadn’t been admiration. They’d been warnings.

“Misunderstanding! All a misunderstanding! I’m not a pervert!”

Whatever dignity a regressor is supposed to carry — he’d left that in the previous life. He begged loudly and without shame.

“Yah! Still denying it?! Then why were you following me?!”

Genuinely angry, by the sound of it.

“Uh… because you’re pretty.”

Delivered with a straight face and a resting heartbeat. You don’t slap a man who’s smiling at you — house rules. And a smart man bends so that he can straighten later.

The girl froze. Under the streetlight, he could have sworn something pink flickered across the one visible strip of her face. Then the logic caught up with her: mask, cap, full-body blackout — eyes and nothing else. Her own eomma would have walked right past her on this street.

Pretty. Right. The man was just saying words.

“Yah! And you’re still running your mouth!”

The armlock gained several newtons.

“Yah! Yah! Yah! My mistake! My mistake! I’m honestly not a stalking pervert — I’m going to the hotpot place up ahead!”

She paused. The pressure didn’t let up, but the suspicion behind the mask visibly began recalculating. Her eyes traveled down him and back up, auditing this twenty-something for whatever angle he could possibly be running.

“Hotpot place? Heh. I live right around here. There is no hotpot place on this street. You’re a pervert!!!”

Internally, Ji-yeon was in deep mourning. Fine — he was no statue of a drama lead, but he was a legitimately scarce supply of handsome, and never once in either life had he been classified as a public menace.

He used his free hand to point, with everything he had, at the storefront down the block.

“I’m telling you, I’m not a stalker! I’m going to that hotpot restaurant — we just happen to be walking the same way! Look! Right there! ONGI! Brand new!”

She followed his finger, blinked up — and there it was. A freshly renovated storefront, its sign blinking away in cheerful technicolor. ONGI, in letters doing their honest best to be visible from space.

Her face did a full LED impression — green, red, green again. She got it. She had just performed a citizen’s arrest on an innocent man. She shot upright, backpedaled two steps, and folded into a ninety-degree bow.

“Mianhae! I didn’t know a hotpot place had opened here… there was nothing a month ago, I swear—”

Her voice had gone thin and wobbly, her eyes shining wet. She sounded about three seconds from crying.

Hold on. I haven’t even cried. Why are you crying?

He was, frankly, bewildered. But she was a girl, and he’d been raised in a restaurant family, and certain laws are simply absolute.

Making a girl cry? Sorry. Couldn’t do it. Not in either life.

He scrambled up, dusted himself off, and deployed his most trustworthy smile.

“It’s fine, it’s fine! Consider us even — what’s the saying, the best friendships start with a fight? The place opened just this month, actually. Today was literally the last day of construction.”

Then he fished a crisp business card out of his jacket and offered it up: Song Ji-yeon — President, SAVORY F&B.

“Here. See? Not a liar. This is my restaurant — I came by to check the renovation. Next time, maybe verify the suspect before you perform the takedown.”

She took the card and studied it in the combined glow of the streetlight and the convenience store’s fluorescent hum. A few silent seconds. Then, slowly, she pulled the mask down — revealing a face that was stubborn and soft at once, the kind that made you want to take her side before you knew any of the facts — and bowed again.

“Mianhae, Song Ji-yeon-ssi. Mistaking you for an Anti — I’m so, so sorry.”

Ji-yeon went still.

He knew that face.

In his previous life, his college years had been spent in the blue glow of a PC-bang monitor, grinding ranked games to a T-ara playlist on loop — and of the six of them, the one he always stopped everything for — bias, ult, whatever you want to call it — was Ham Eun-jung.

“Ham Eun-jung-ssi? You’re… T-ara’s Ham Eun-jung-ssi?”

It came out at entirely the wrong volume.

Eun-jung flinched, swiveled to scan the street in both directions, and yanked the mask back into place. Her voice dropped to a squeak.

“Ne… could you maybe… not be so loud?”

She was scared. Two days ago, someone had thrown eggs at her — at her — and since then she hadn’t dared show her face outdoors. The thought of what an angry fan might do next kept the mask on and her head down.

“Mianhae — I’m just… really excited to run into you. I’m a fan. I’ve loved the group for years. I know every song.”

He kept his voice low and contrite, while in the back office of his skull, an entirely different meeting was in session.

The other members of T-ara — he liked them, sure. Fondly, casually, the way you like a group’s B-sides.

But Ham Eun-jung? Sorry, everyone else—

—this one, his heart had actually picked.

White-haired Eun-jung, forever the GOAT!!!!

Then a cold little thought tapped him on the shoulder.

Wait. When exactly did the troll incident happen? …Don’t tell me it’s already started.

Last life, he’d come to T-ara long after the fact. By then the scandal was ancient history — four years buried, the kind of thing fans mentioned the way you point out an old scar. He knew of it. He’d never actually studied it.

“Thank goodness no one’s around…”

She checked the street one more time — a full, careful sweep — and finally exhaled, long and slow. It had been weeks since anyone had said they liked them. Since the bullying rumors caught fire, the internet had been one long, unbroken scream.

“Thank you for still liking T-ara. And about tonight — again, I’m truly, genuinely sorry.”

“No, no — I was the one walking behind you. You were right to be careful.”

He waved it off. Then couldn’t help himself.

“But what are you doing out here alone? And, um… dressed like that?”

For half a second, the light in her eyes went out.

Then it came back on, set to normal brightness.

He read the entire answer in that half-second — the eyes, the mask, the hour — and had the sense not to ask twice.

So it’s already happened. The troll incident is here.
