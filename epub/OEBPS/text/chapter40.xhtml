Chapter Forty

Endorsement Plans

THAT EVENING The Han River crossing en route to Yeongdeungpo-gu

The Mercedes ran smooth and quiet over the Han River bridge, the neon on both banks smearing past the windows in long ribbons of color.

Inside the car, something warm and unspoken had settled in. Sulli and Ji-won sat in the back, and Sulli was a sparrow newly sprung from its cage — set stories, actor gossip, who had flubbed which line how many times — a bright, ceaseless chatter that needed no audience, only the occasional grunt of agreement from the driver’s seat, which he supplied.

Kim Ji-won sat quietly through all of it, and now and then her eyes found the rearview mirror, and drifted, briefly, to the man behind the wheel — the calm line of his shoulders, the unhurried way his hands moved on the wheel.

“Oppa, how much looonger? I’m actually starving now… Is your hotpot even good?!”

Sulli had leaned forward between the seats, chin parked on the passenger headrest, big eyes fixed on the side of his face and shining with entirely unreasonable expectations.

“Almost there. As for whether it’s good—” He glanced at her in the mirror. “You’re about to find out for yourself.”

The set and the restaurant were both in Yeongdeungpo, so the distance was nothing. The delay was Seoul itself: a weekday evening, the roads thick. Ten-some minutes of crawl later, the car slid to a stop beneath a commercial building in Yeongdeungpo-gu.

He walked the two of them in through the front like a regular — and a middle-aged man in a black uniform came striding up at once, bowing a full ninety degrees.

“Sajangnim. Your private room is prepared.”

He’d called ahead from the car — the man bowing before them was the Yeongdeungpo district head himself. Not because Song Ji-yeon enjoyed the theater of it, but because he was traveling with two tagalongs. Kim Ji-won was manageable: The Heirs hadn’t happened yet, so she was a working actress of middling fame — recognizable, not news. Sulli was another animal entirely. A currently trending idol spotted at dinner with a grown man, at close quarters, all evening? That was tomorrow’s number-one search term writing itself — and given the lunatic fringe of her fandom, he didn’t care to imagine what they’d excavate of his personal history by breakfast.

The manager led them personally through the bustling main hall to the innermost private room — the Geumseong room — and the tablets arrived almost before they’d settled into their seats.

It was Sulli’s first time inside an ONGI, and she was inspecting everything like customs.

“Oppa, the service in this place is unreal. You order on tablets? Doesn’t that cost you a fortune?”

“It earns its keep.”

She was already into the menu, and her eyes were getting bigger by the line. “Whoa. Australian wagyu… marbled beef tongue… hand-cut lamb… Oppa, how many kinds of meat does your restaurant HAVE?”

“Order whatever you like.” Kim Ji-won’s voice was soft from beside her. “Tonight is my treat.”

“Then I shall hold nothing back!” Sulli stuck her tongue out and got to tapping.

“Easy there. I’m not clearing your leftovers tonight, just so you know.” He remembered the last beef dinner all too well — a mountain ordered, two bites consumed, the remaining eighty percent marched into his stomach by popular demand.

“YAH, oppa!” She pouted at him. “I’m off duty tonight! No diet, no figure to watch — I have legal clearance to eat everything.”

Kim Ji-won watched her defend her appetite and couldn’t hold back a small smile. She wasn’t a talker by design, and after the day’s scare there remained a fine, indefinable formality in her whenever she faced Song Ji-yeon. But Sulli’s happiness was aggressive and contagious, and it was doing something restorative to the whole room.

The carts came rolling in — broths first. Sulli had ordered four: the doenjang, the nuclear beef-tallow mala, the savory samgyetang, and the bright tomato. Had the divided pot offered more than four quadrants, she would simply have kept going — the house had six broths and she regarded the two she’d left out as personal enemies.

Then the meats began arriving in procession, dish after artfully arranged dish — the deep red of Australian wagyu, marbled beef tongue with its lattice of white, rose-pink hand-cut lamb — until better than half the table had vanished under them.

“Whoa—”

Sulli put her palms together and bowed to the meat, slowly and ceremonially, a believer at a shrine.

“Oppa, I’ve never even SEEN a pot like this. It looks so, so good!”

She was newer to this than she knew. Before ONGI opened its doors, the Peninsula hadn’t truly had hotpot at all — the closest native cousin was sinseollo, the old royal hot pot: ingredients prepped and arranged, broth poured over, everything left to a gentle simmer. Lovely, ancient — and in his honest opinion, an opinion calibrated over years of San Gabriel Valley food runs, soup. There were Chinese immigrants in Seoul who ran proper hotpot kitchens, scattered across the city, but scattered was all they were — no system, no marketing, no scale, and so nobody knew. Then ONGI had arrived, and it hadn’t so much entered the market as dropped onto it from a higher dimension. Add the opening discounts, the campaigns, the word of mouth — and these days, on this Peninsula, when a person said hotpot, the word came back with an ONGI logo on it.

Sulli picked up an entire plate of beef, poising it over the rolling red of the mala quadrant with unmistakable intent.

“Stop, stop — that’s not how this works.” He caught her wrist before the atrocity could occur. “Hotpot is a swishing affair. Watch.”

He took up the serving chopsticks, lifted a slice of the marbled tongue — cut thin enough to read through — and dipped it into the boiling broth. Three seconds. Four. The edges curled, and he lifted it out clean and precise, and set it down in Sulli’s dipping bowl.

“Try that.”

She picked it up, blew on it twice, cautious as a bomb-tech, and put the whole thing in her mouth.

Her eyes flew open.

The tongue snapped crisp-sweet between her teeth, the beef-tallow broth arrived behind it wearing perfume, and the garlic and sesame oil in her dip stepped in at the finish to shake hands with the spice and settle it. What remained was pure, clean savor.

“Mmm— good!” she managed, cheeks working, a hamster in mid-harvest. “Sooo good! Oppa, this is SO good!”

Kim Ji-won watched her, and the smile stayed at the corner of her mouth without asking permission.

She was, by constitution, a quiet person, and the day had left a strange shyness in her where Song Ji-yeon was concerned. But wrapped in Choi Sulli’s aggressive, infectious joy, she felt her shoulders come down an inch she hadn’t known they were up.

“Ji-won-ssi — you try.” He swished a slice of the Australian wagyu through the broth and set it, in turn, into her bowl.

The wagyu was a serious grade of animal — red and white marbled like polished stone. A few seconds in the broth and it drank the soup into itself, and then it simply wasn’t there anymore: it melted the instant it landed, and flooded the palate with beef flavor in full surround.

Kim Ji-won chewed slowly, and surprise crossed her face before she could reorganize it.

She had assumed hotpot was a blunt instrument — everything thrown into one pot to boil, how refined could the outcome be? Tonight, one slice had dismantled the assumption with prejudice.

“It’s genuinely wonderful.” She said it simply. “Far better than I imagined.”

“Good. Glad it lands.”

He smiled — and behind the smile, his mind had already slid two rooms over, into the other matter.

The Saero zero-sugar soju technology was in his hands as of this afternoon. That was no bucket of gold; that was an entire gold mine awaiting a shovel. Building a distillery from zero was a multi-year construction project — the fast play was to buy a soju factory that already existed and deserved its fate. The Peninsula chewed through failing small distilleries every single year — if not a hundred of them, then not far off it. Find one mid-sized, fully licensed, and badly run; buy it cheap; rebuild the line; load in the new formula.

And then — marketing.

The slogans wrote themselves — “zero sugar,” “healthy,” “the soju that won’t make you fat.” Against the Peninsula’s figure-managing young consumers — the women above all — that wasn’t competition, it was a dimensional drop. And on top of it, put a face: a couple of the hottest idols or actors in the country, fronting the brand…

His gaze traveled, without meaning to, across the two women sitting opposite him. Then he shook his head.

His complete inventory of celebrity acquaintances numbered eight: T-ara, plus the two in front of him. The Crown six were currently radioactive — signing any of them as a brand face right now would not be a decision, it would be a confession. And of these two, one had only just crossed the threshold into adulthood, and the other was, as yet, a rookie actress with a lovely face and no name recognition to speak of. Neither of them fit.

He turned the problem over. And over.

And somewhere in the turning — one candidate surfaced.
