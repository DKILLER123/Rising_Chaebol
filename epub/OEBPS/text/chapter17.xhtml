SEPTEMBER 2012 · LATE EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Ji-yeon’s apartment

He walked into the bathroom and twisted the tap. Hot water came down in a roar; steam swallowed the little room and fogged the mirror. Ji-yeon stood under the spray and let it hammer the ache out of his shoulders, eyes closed, while somewhere behind them the ledger kept running.

The Jeju concert. He couldn’t stop what was coming — the Black Ocean would happen whether he was ready for it or not — but afterward, at least, there could be something warm waiting for Eun-jung. A place to set the wreckage down.

And the Samsung Electronics stock was climbing the exact staircase he remembered, steady step after steady step. When the moment was right, he would cash out. Becoming some legendary stock god had never been the point. The point was a war chest — enough to swallow CCM Entertainment whole and park T-ara where he could see them with his own eyes, every single day. He wasn’t so much as dreaming of anything happening with the six of them. He simply refused, on principle, to stand by and watch them live through — again — everything he remembered from the last time around.

By the time he wandered back out, towel around his waist and hair still dripping onto his shoulders, he had made it exactly halfway to a glass of water when the phone began buzzing against the coffee table.

The screen lit up with a name: Her Majesty the Queen Mother.

Ji-yeon blinked. This hour? Why would his mother be calling this late?

He answered. He didn’t get a single syllable out.

Phone · Kang Seol-ah → Song Ji-yeon

“Yah, you brat — did you forget you still have a home?”

“Mom, I’ve just been buried, that’s all…”

“Buried, buried, buried! You moved out almost a month ago! Not one phone call. Not one! Do you even remember you have a mother?”

“Mom, I swear it’s the company. ONGI just opened — there’s so much going on. Renovation, hiring, training. I’ve had my hands on every single detail…”

“That is no reason not to come home! Your father and I are right here in Mapo — thirty minutes from Cheongdam-dong by car! However busy a grown man gets, he can make time for one dinner at his mother’s table!”

He heard it then — the hurt running underneath all that artillery. And guilt arrived right on schedule. Even down a phone line, that voice landed on him like a weighted blanket. In this life and the last one alike, his mother had always been the same woman: complaints on the surface, worry all the way down.

And truthfully, the original owner of this body had never been the homecoming type. Even back in his school days he’d treated the house like a hotel with inconvenient check-in hours; now that he’d settled in Korea and moved out on top of it, a full month without showing his face was hard to defend. And tomorrow, the company could survive a day without him.

“Mom. I’m sorry. Tomorrow I’ll come home — I promise.”

“Really? Then it’s settled — tomorrow afternoon, straight home! I’ll cook everything you love!”

“Okay. Tomorrow. Absolutely.”

“That’s more like it.”

The words were followed by a satisfied sniff and three more minutes of marching orders — eat proper meals, stop living like a bat — and then the line went dead, entirely on her own terms.

He stared at the black screen and shook his head, smiling despite himself.

His mother, Kang Seol-ah, was a typical Korean-immigrant mother: warm, family-first, and constitutionally incapable of not worrying. Her single flaw — if it even qualified as a flaw — was that she cared about her son too much. Meals, grades, jobs, sleep: nothing in his life had ever fallen outside her jurisdiction. Growing up, he’d found it suffocating about a third of the time. He could also say, with the full honesty of a man who had once lived entirely without it, that being somebody’s entire jurisdiction was not a bad way to exist.

He put the phone down, finally got his glass of water, and leaned against the kitchen counter, letting his eyes drift across the empty living room.

In his previous life he had been alone — a solitary animal, long practiced at it. This life had handed him a family, and it turned out he barely knew how to hold one.

He finished the water and headed for the bedroom.

Dinner at home tomorrow. Good.

It would be a chance to talk shop with his father, too — to hear what the old man saw when he looked down the road. Song Min-seok had done business on the Peninsula for years now, and he had never once stepped off the acre of land he’d staked out: restaurants, nothing but restaurants. The scale wasn’t large — comfortable rather than impressive, the kind of business that lost every race to bigger fish and quietly outlived them all. But Ji-yeon would never make the mistake of underestimating a man who had built a fortune from nothing with his own two hands. Men like that always carried something in them that ordinary people didn’t.

He lay down, closed his eyes, and let tomorrow take shape.

FRIDAY · 4:00 PM Mapo-gu, Seoul the Song family home

At four the next afternoon, Ji-yeon turned the car onto a quiet street in Mapo.

It wasn’t a fashionable block. Tall ginkgos lined both sides, canopies dense, gilded faintly gold in the early-autumn light. At the street’s end stood a two-story house of modest size — grey walls, a deep-brown roof surfacing through the tree shade, a few neatly trimmed holly bushes by the gate, ivy climbing the garden wall and stubbornly green.

This was the Song family’s home on the Peninsula.

“Villa” would be overselling it — a two-story house with a small courtyard, no more. In Korea that was comfort, not luxury. But for a family of immigrants who had spent years building a restaurant business in a country that hadn’t originally been theirs, it was the architecture of a life finally landed: solid, quiet, middle-class to the bone.

He parked at the gate, killed the engine, and pushed the car door open.

The early-autumn air carried the green smell of leaves and, underneath it, from somewhere inside, cooking. His shoulders came down without asking permission. He took a deep breath, pushed through the courtyard gate, and followed the stone path to the door.

Before his finger could find the bell, the door swung inward.

“You’re home?” A middle-aged woman stood in the doorway — light loungewear, an apron, hair pinned up any-old-how at the back of her head, a warm smile already in place. Her features were small and fine, and kindness had settled into them permanently. There were fine lines at the corners of her eyes, but her skin was well kept, and it was easy to see that twenty years ago she had been a genuine beauty.

This was Kang Seol-ah, his mother.

“Mom.” The word came out easy, worn smooth by a lifetime of use.

“In, in — come in.” She stepped aside and looked him up and down, eyes filling with delight and grievance in equal measure. “Look at you. Thin. You’re not eating.”

“Mom. It’s been one month. There’s a limit to how much thinner I could plausibly get.” He laughed, traded his shoes for slippers, and followed the hallway into the living room.

Everything was exactly as he remembered. The beige fabric sofa. The dark walnut coffee table. An ink landscape scroll on the wall, a pothos thriving in the corner. On the TV console sat a framed photo of the three of them — the boy in the picture still wearing his high-school blazer, smiling the unguarded smile of someone with no idea what was coming.

“You sit — I have to check the soup, dinner will be ready in no time.” She bustled toward the kitchen, then stopped two steps in. “Oh, right. Your father’s in the study. He says he has things to ask you.”

Ji-yeon nodded and headed for the stairs.

The study sat at the turn of the staircase, its door half open, a bar of lamplight spilling through. He knocked, and from inside came a steady male voice, unhurried as ever.

“Come in.”
