Chapter Thirty-Three

The Cheongdam Storefront

SEPTEMBER 2012 · AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the storefront hunt

The afternoon sun over Cheongdam-dong came down through the gaps in the street trees and scattered across the pavement like a layer of spilled gold.

Song Ji-yeon and Park Seon-young walked along one side of the street at an unhurried pace, the planning director with his little notebook already sweating in his grip. Their mission today was simple to say and miserable to execute: find a storefront in Cheongdam-dong worth renting, and plant the next ONGI hotpot house in it.

Seoul had its districts, and Ji-yeon’s plan for them was brazenly arithmetic — at least three ONGI stores in every one, close enough together to share the crowds instead of fighting for them. Gangnam already had its first, the Apgujeong flagship. Number two, he’d decided, would live in Cheongdam-dong.

That was the theory. The practice, since ten that morning, had been a parade of rents that could fund a small navy and owners who had no intention whatsoever of selling.

Ji-yeon understood it. Cheongdam-dong was where the entertainment companies roosted; the foot traffic ran like a river. Open a restaurant here with flavors even slightly above average, and you would have to actively work at losing money.

“Sajangnim, the rent in this neighborhood is daylight robbery. I ran the numbers just now — what one storefront costs here would open three stores north of the river.” Park Seon-young scribbled at speed in his notebook, then sketched a truly astronomical figure in the air with his right hand.

He paused, frowned at the street, and let out a sigh. “And the company’s capital hasn’t even circulated back in yet. Aren’t we getting ahead of ourselves, talking about new stores?”

“Old Park, widen the frame.” Ji-yeon clapped him on the shoulder. “A neighborhood like this doesn’t sell rice money — it sells face and premium. ONGI is walking the high-end road, and the Cheongdam signboard has to come off the wall and into our hands. It’s called brand tonality.”

They walked a while longer and came to a stop at a crossroads.

Diagonally opposite stood a Chinese restaurant with a signboard gone shabby at the edges. Flanked by sleek, modern glass storefronts, the shop looked not merely old but stranded — a little faded, a little abandoned by the times. Taped to the door’s window was a sheet of transfer paper: PROSPEROUS STORE FOR TRANSFER.

“That one. Let’s go in and look.” Ji-yeon crossed the street in long, steady strides.

Park Seon-young blinked, then trotted after him.

That was a young Sajangnim for you — decision speed that left tire marks. Park felt a pang of pure nostalgia for the old days, when the founder handed tasks down to him, and he handed tasks down to the grunts under him. Now here he was running the streets on his own two legs. He was a department director — he had a dozen workhorses to dispatch!

The heavy wooden door swung open, and a faint thread of sweet-and-sour sauce and long-simmering broth drifted out to meet them.

The interior was dim, the decor stalled somewhere in the nineties. But the dark-red round tables and the plastic-backed chairs were wiped to a shine, and the floor tiles carried none of that tacky film of grease old restaurants wear like a second floor.

Behind the counter stood a man in his forties in a clean white chef’s jacket, wiping down the register top with a cloth — unhurried, methodical, corner by corner. The expression beneath it all was mild.

Ji-yeon took one slow lap of the room with his eyes and nodded inwardly. This owner was a stickler. The shop had aged, but the hygiene hadn’t slipped by a single degree — the mark of a man who cooked with intention, not just with a ledger.

“Two guests? Please, sit anywhere you like.” The owner looked up, set his cloth aside, and smiled gently. “The menus are on the table. Call me whenever you’re ready.”

His Korean was fluent — quick, easy, comfortable. But underneath the fluency sat a roundness in the vowels, a lilt that hadn’t been born in Seoul. Ji-yeon knew that sound the way he knew the smell of his mother’s kitchen: it was the voice of a man who had learned this language in adulthood, standing at a stove, in a country not his own. An immigrant’s Korean. Ji-yeon had grown up inside the English-language twin of exactly that voice, in every kitchen his family had ever run.

“Thank you, boss.” Ji-yeon smiled back, drew out a chair, and picked up the menu — lightly worn at the corners, wiped spotless.

“One mapo tofu. One tangsuyuk. One yuxiang pork — the shredded one. And two bowls of rice, please.”

The owner’s eyebrows rose, just slightly, the way a cook’s do when an order comes in fluent foodie. “Good choices. Two minutes.” He turned for the kitchen, brisk as a drumbeat.

With the order in, Ji-yeon let his gaze wander the room. It was the height of the lunch hour, and yet the tables sat barely a third full. Aside from two parties near the door murmuring in Korean, the shop held a still, settled quiet — only, from the kitchen, the clean percussion of a spatula ringing off an iron wok.

“Sajangnim, turnover’s average at best, but look at the regulars — that table just now didn’t so much as glance at the menu.” Park Seon-young had his voice down to a professional murmur, every inch the planning director. “And a shop this age kept this clean? The owner isn’t the type to chase money with both hands and no principles.”

Ji-yeon nodded. The Chinese restaurants of this generation in Korea lived on their old regulars and nothing else — family workshops, one pot feeding one household. Surviving, yes. Growing? Out of the question without capital, and none of them had ever heard the word marketing spoken aloud. He could map the whole economy of it in a glance. His own family had been that workshop once, in another country, with better luck.

“Two guests — everything’s out. Please, enjoy.” The owner brought the tray himself and set the three steaming plates down with a steady hand. Perhaps it was the order — mapo tofu and yuxiang pork sat on no casual customer’s radar — but the warmth had visibly doubled since the door. “If the rice runs low, you tell me anytime. I’ll keep it coming.”

The mapo tofu gleamed glossy red. The tangsuyuk wore its glaze in an even lacquer. The yuxiang pork shone, oil-bright and welcoming, waiting its turn.

Ji-yeon picked up his chopsticks and tried the tofu first.

It dissolved on the tongue — hot, numbing, spicy, fragrant, all at once, the compound perfume that only true Sichuan cooking carries, blooming across his palate like a struck chord.

“This flavor…” His chewing slowed; the light in his eyes sharpened. “…is the real thing. This is top-tier cooking for Seoul.”

It wasn’t his first Chinese meal in Korea — nowhere close. But Korean palates ran sweet and mild, and the Chinese restaurants here bent their cooking to match, dish after dish arriving at the table neither one thing nor the other. Edible. Middling. Forgettable by dessert.

This place was different. This tasted like the food he’d grown up on — the honest home-style wok work of the San Gabriel Valley, the strip-mall treasure houses of Alhambra and Monterey Park that his family drove out to on weekends whenever the Koreatown menus wore thin. He had been quietly starving for that flavor since the day he’d woken up in Seoul.

And with one bite, the half-empty room explained itself. Food that refused to flatter the local tongue earned no local crowd. That was all.

He tried the tangsuyuk next — crisp at the shell, tender within, the glaze thin and clinging, the sweet and the sour balanced to the milligram. Nothing like the syrup-soaked Koreanized versions whose only ambition was comfort.

“Boss — this mapo tofu is the genuine article.” He set his chopsticks down and looked over at the counter. “You’re from Sichuan?”

The owner startled. Surprise crossed the mild face first — and then came a smile Ji-yeon recognized at once, the smile of a man hearing his hometown named in a strange country.

“Aiya — sharp tongue on you, young man!” The owner stood and came around the counter, warmth pouring into his face. “Sichuan, yes. I’ve been cooking in this country eleven years now, and in eleven years I could count on one hand the people who tasted that tofu and called it proper. The young folks around here want jjajangmyeon sweet enough to rot your teeth. It wears on a cook, honestly.”

Something in Ji-yeon settled into place. A man who cooked for love, in a city that rewarded him with empty tables. Which meant the rest of this conversation was going to be simple.

“I told myself that wok-fire had old-Sichuan bones in it. Craft like yours is a pearl rolling around in the dust here in Cheongdam-dong.” He laughed and gestured at the chair opposite. “What should I call you, boss? And I’ll confess up front — I didn’t wander in by accident. I came in hunting this exact flavor. I grew up on cooking like yours; Los Angeles has an entire valley of it. Seoul, as far as I’ve been able to tell, has exactly one.”

“Old Chen is fine.” The man loosened a corner of his apron and sat down, something wistful crossing his face. “It’s been a long time since anyone talked to me about food like that. So — what brings a young man like you to Korea, if you don’t mind my asking? You look young enough to still be in school.”

“Finished university.” Ji-yeon shook his head. “My family runs a restaurant company here. I came over after graduating to help with the business — and the business wants to expand with new company-owned stores. Today I’m in Cheongdam-dong looking for a location.”

“Oh?” Old Chen’s face lifted at once. “Young man — what do you think of mine?”

Old Chen had been on the Peninsula eleven years. A decade-plus of grinding, dawn to midnight, had netted him exactly this one storefront. He had meant to sell up and go home for a while now — had even been quietly fishing for a buyer these past weeks — but the price had never once come out right.

As for Ji-yeon — of course he had designs on this store. Why else would he have walked through the door?
