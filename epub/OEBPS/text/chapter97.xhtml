Chapter Ninety-Seven

The Little Dragon and the Peach

LATE AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the road home

Song Ji-yeon drove home with the day finally empty behind him — no appointments, no fires, nothing left on the schedule but dinner.

As for Kang Seul-gi’s throat — he did mean to help. He just wasn’t about to go sprinting after Kim Ji-won about it.

The Goddess Handbook might have her file stamped and bound, but of all four bound goddesses, she was the one he’d had the least to do with. They’d crossed paths on the drama set — what was it, a handful of times? — exchanged KakaoTalk IDs at the wrap party, and that was the entire relationship to date. Not one message since. Her ID sat in his contacts like a business card in a drawer: filed, valid, never once used.

You couldn’t exactly open with a “You there?” after months of radio silence. What was she supposed to do with that?

He wasn’t that kind of man. He’d like the record to show it.

So — no rushing it. Let it come about on its own; he’d find the opening later. And besides, the backstop was already in place: even if he never lifted a finger, Kang Seul-gi’s future worked out fine. He’d read that particular file all the way to the end.

He turned the thought over a few more times, and somewhere in the turning, the car slid down into the Daewoo Royal County garage.

He’d just parked and stepped into the elevator when the phone in his pocket began to buzz.

He fished it out. The screen lit up with a name he’d saved a while back — five letters, one fruit: Peach.

He swiped to answer, and before he could get a single word in, the voice on the other end arrived at a frequency only dogs and girl-group leaders could parse.

Phone · Sulli → Song Ji-yeon

“Oppa! OPPA! Are you free right now?!”

“Just got home. What’s got you like this — did you find money on the street?”

“Better than money!” The delight was structural; it bent every word out of shape. “Vic-unnie — she’s OFF DUTY! She said what happens with me and oppa is no longer her department! I’m free! FREE!”

Even through the speaker, the joy had achieved escape velocity. He could see her without seeing her — bouncing, he was certain, on some piece of furniture that had done nothing to deserve it.

Something in his own chest lifted along with it.

“That is genuinely big news. That’s celebration-grade news.”

“Right?! RIGHT?!” She grabbed the agreement with both hands. “So — to celebrate! Oppa, dinner tonight, out somewhere! I want samgyeopsal!”

Sulli had been aching to come see him for days, and the warden situation had made that impossible. Now, freshly sprung, the first thing she wanted was oppa. The dinner was the pretext. The meeting was the point.

He glanced at his watch. Just past three.

He’d eaten exactly one meal today — breakfast — and his stomach had begun filing formal complaints. But he’d been out and running since morning, and the idea of going back out, into the world, for anything at all…

“Going out is a hassle. I just got in, and I’m not moving again.”

He thought for a second.

“How about this — come to my place instead. Whatever you want to eat, I’ll cook it myself.”

Two full seconds of silence on the line.

Then an eruption, at a higher altitude than anything before it.

“REALLY?! I get to come to oppa’s HOUSE?! Then — spicy stir-fried pork belly! And kimchi jjigae! Please please please!”

“Done. And my door’s open to you. Anytime, no notice needed.”

“Okay! Okay okay okay — I’m leaving RIGHT NOW—”

The words were coming out in non-sequential order, stepping on each other’s heels.

For the record: to this girl, oppa’s house outranked every delicacy the Peninsula had to offer. A twelve-course hotel dinner could not compete. An octopus straight off a Jeju boat could not compete.

“Slow down — it’s still early.” He talked her off the ledge. “Come around five. I’ll get everything prepped so it’s hot the second you walk in.”

He gave her the building and the unit, made her repeat the floor twice, and hung up to the sound of a one-girl street festival.

“Then it’s a date — see you at five, oppa!”

“See you at five.”

✦ ✦ ✦

HALF PAST THREE Unit 101, Daewoo Royal County a fridge with a reputation to protect

Out of the elevator, into the apartment. He opened the fridge and took inventory: stocked, absurdly. The last supermarket run with Park Ji-yeon — that expedition had provisioned the place like a bunker awaiting a siege — and a week of eating out had barely dented it.

Perfect timing, then. Waste not.

The menu resolved itself: simple home cooking. Her two requests — the spicy stir-fried pork belly, the kimchi jjigae — plus a couple of clean, cold side dishes to balance all that heat.

He set the pork belly and the frozen items out to thaw, then dropped onto the sofa without a shred of apology.

Half past three. Hours yet.

Rest first. Cook later. A plan with no flaws in it anywhere.

Sprawled there, he took out his phone, opened KakaoTalk, and typed out a message to someone else entirely.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Song Ji-yeon

How did it go? Kwang-soo didn’t give you a hard time, did he?

He remembered — this morning Eun-jung had reported that CCM was summoning all six of them for the transfer talks. He’d been waiting on the outcome all afternoon.

The reply came quickly.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Ham Eun-jung

Not at all. Kwang-soo-Sajangnim settled this year’s performance fees on the spot — clean, no arguments. And he told us he’s transferring our management contracts; he asked how we felt first. Once we all agreed, he gave us the new company’s contact…

He looked at the number she’d pasted in. Kim Min-hyun. Of course.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Song Ji-yeon

That’s SAVORY F&B’s legal director — anything urgent, he’s the fastest route. As for the entertainment company’s own people: once I’ve finished building out the admin framework and staffing it, someone will reach out to you properly. Give me a little time.

Ham Eun-jung

Okay, oppa.

Song Ji-yeon

Busy?

Ham Eun-jung

Mianhae, oppa — I’m with finance settling this year’s fees right now. There are contracts to sign.

Song Ji-yeon

No rush. Work first. Dinner tomorrow night — all six of you, my treat. We’ll celebrate properly.

He remembered, after all, exactly what he’d promised them: the moment T-ara was bought, dinner was on him. This was as good a moment as any to put it on the table.

The chat wound down. He checked the time — knocking on four — got up, and reported for duty at the island.

Apron on. Ingredients out. Knife work.

Vegetables cut, sides prepped, pork marinating. The kitchen filled up with the percussion of pots and pans, then with the smell of garlic and gochujang hitting heat — and the big, quiet apartment gradually started sounding like a place where people lived.

By five-something, the last dish was landing on the table when the doorbell rang.

Apron off. Door open.

Sulli stood in the corridor holding — at ceremonial arm’s length — a plastic pack of tissues. White dress, shoulder-length hair tucked behind her ears, a light, precise makeup job, and those crescent smiling eyes aimed up at him like a cat presenting a successfully hunted sock and awaiting praise.

“Oppa! I’m here!”

Door open, target acquired: she presented the tissue pack with both hands, official as a diploma.

He took it, smiling. Household inventory of ceremonial paper: now three packs. One from Ham Eun-jung, one from the little dragon upstairs, and now this — the Peninsula custom being that a first visit to anyone’s home required tribute, and he had stopped remarking on it somewhere around the second pack. His mother had run the same diplomacy out of LA for twenty years. Some protocols were simply universal.

“Perfect timing. Straight off the stove.” He set the pack down, then crouched at the entryway shelf and set a pair of new slippers by her feet. “Fresh out of the plastic. Fair warning — men’s sizes, it’s all I keep. Squeeze in, or come in socks. Dealer’s choice.”

“Men’s slippers are fine, oppa!” The little peach beamed, eyes bending into crescents.

Shoes off, slippers on. He stepped aside to let her pass.

“In you come. Go wash your hands — we eat the second you’re back.”

“WAH — what IS that smell!”

She made it three steps into the apartment before the aroma ambushed her. She materialized at the table and went down the dishes one by one, eyes lighting up like slot machines.

“Oppa, you’re actually incredible. It looks like a food commercial! It looks illegal!”

And here was the thing — Sulli knew home cooking when she saw it. She’d eaten it before. Vic-unnie, born on the far side of the Yellow Sea, would cook the members a Chinese meal once in a while, when homesickness crested and the schedule allowed. And Vic-unnie had a doctrine she delivered like scripture: a proper dish has to win three times — once for the eyes, once for the nose, once for the mouth. Color, aroma, taste. No exemptions, no mercy.

By the eyes alone, oppa’s table had already taken the series.

“It’s home cooking, nothing more. Go wash up.” He pointed at the bathroom, grinning.

“Got it~!”

She skipped off. By the time she came back, two bowls of rice were already steaming at their places, and he was pouring barley tea.

“Sit. Eat first. Judge later.”

The two of them had barely gotten their chopsticks airborne when—

Beep. Beep.

—the front door announced a code and unlocked itself from the outside.

Park Ji-yeon came in mid-hum — some drama OST, badly rendered — swapping her shoes for house slippers on pure autopilot.

“Oppa, I’m home— …hm?”

She’d come straight from a company meeting. She’d walked past his door. She’d smelled a dinner that smelled exactly like the dinners she held rights to. The passcode had gone in on muscle memory before her brain had finished clearing the hallway.

And then the hallway cleared, and her brain took attendance.

Oppa: present.

Across from oppa, at the table: an extremely pretty girl who had never once appeared in this apartment in her life.

Their eyes met. The pretty girl’s head dropped like a startled quail’s.

Park Ji-yeon, however, had already placed her.

She knew that face. f(x)’s Sulli. Music-broadcast waiting rooms, year-end stage hallways — they’d crossed paths more times than either could count, sat through variety tapings together. Not best friends. Solidly, establishedly acquainted.

Which raised the question.

Sulli lived in the SM dorm. Sulli was, as far as anyone knew, in the SM dorm right now, doing whatever it is SM dorms make people do.

Why was Sulli in this apartment?

Park Ji-yeon’s first, fairest hypothesis: wrong door.

She blearily rubbed her eyes. Looked at Sulli. Looked at oppa. Looked back at the entryway shoe cabinet — her own shoes sitting in it, second shelf, exactly where they always sat.

No. This was oppa’s house. This was definitely oppa’s house.

She began reversing on instinct, back the way she’d come, muttering at floor level:

“Sorry… wrong door…”

“Park Ji-yeon!”

The voice caught her, half-laughing, before the retreat could complete.

“You’ve got the right door. Come in.”

Only then did it land: she wasn’t dreaming, and she hadn’t walked into the wrong apartment. She turned back around, fully awake, eyes going wide, and pointed — at the girl at the table, at the man by the stove, her arm swinging between them like a metronome of pure disbelief.

“Oppa! She— Sulli?! You— HOW do you two know each other?!”

He took one look at the haunted expression and waved her over, unbothered, far too entertained.

“Come sit. I made plenty. You can conduct the interrogation over dinner.”

Ji-yeon drifted over in a daze and dropped into the empty seat beside him — eyes never once leaving the girl across the table, staring as though the right angle might reveal hidden seams.

Sulli, for her part, was running her own calculations, and the numbers were alarming.

Because Ji-yeon-unnie had walked in with the passcode. The passcode. Entered it from memory. Strolled in like a shareholder doing rounds.

Did that mean… unnie and oppa were… living together?

NO. Peach refused. Peach filed a formal objection. This outcome was rejected in full.

Sulli ran through possible conversational entry points, selected one, assembled her courage — looked up, and found Ji-yeon-unnie already studying her with undisguised, cheerful interest.

Being stared at like a zoo exhibit, she felt her face warm, decided attack was the safest form of greeting, stood up, and beamed first.

“Ji-yeon-unnie! Long time no see!”

“Ah— right! Sulli! Long time no see—” Ji-yeon snapped out of it and returned the greeting at a scramble, hands not entirely sure where to land.

Because she had questions too. Since when did oppa know Sulli? At what point? To what degree? They were already at the dinner-at-home stage — the come-over-for-a-meal stage — and she’d never heard one syllable about any of it?

Her confusion was, at minimum, a round of Sulli’s match.

The two of them looked at each other. Looked some more. The air acquired a texture.

In the end it was Song Ji-yeon who couldn’t stand it. He picked up his chopsticks, dropped a piece of pork belly into each of their bowls, and broke the spell.

“All right. Enough of the eye-fencing. Eat first, interrogate after.”

The command landed, and both girls’ mouths opened at the same instant—

“Oppa, how do you know Ji-yeon-unnie?”

“Oppa, how do you know Sulli?”

—then stopped. Blinked. Looked at each other across the table: two identical question marks with different hairstyles.

He laughed at the pair of them, set his chopsticks down, and turned to Ji-yeon first.

“You asked first. So — you first.”

“Me and Sulli—” he sorted for the tellable parts, “—we met last month. At the exchange, over in Yeouido. I recommended her a stock… and that same day my blood sugar quit on me and I went down on the sidewalk, and she’s the one who got me to a hospital.”

He tipped his head toward Sulli, grin arriving in his voice.

“And while I was flat on my back, she also liberated a business card from my card case. So when the stock paid off, she tracked me down through the card and insisted on buying me a thank-you dinner. One thing led to another, and now here we are. Friends.”

Having the story told in front of an audience, Sulli’s ears went pink. She mumbled at the tablecloth.

“Oppa! Why does it come out like I attached myself to you like a barnacle… I was repaying a debt! And you made me— you made me SO much money.”

Ji-yeon, at least, had her answer. She laughed.

“Ohhh — that’s how you two met. And here I thought I’d genuinely walked into the wrong apartment. When I saw Sulli my brain just went— blank. Static.”

Sulli laughed and moved to her own line of inquiry.

“Then what about you, Ji-yeon-unnie? How come you can just… enter oppa’s door code? You two— what exactly is the relationship?”

Asked with tremendous care. The eyes, however, were screaming the actual question — ARE YOU TWO LIVING TOGETHER — in bold, underlined, highlighted twice.

And that question lit Park Ji-yeon up like a marquee. She straightened, chest out, delighted beyond reason to be asked.

“Me? I live upstairs! We’re neighbors! Oppa cooks, I eat — I’m his certified dinner partner, appointed, notarized. Is it a crime to visit now?”

More delight. Increasingly smug. Practically glowing with borrowed status.

“Besides — think about it. One company. He’s our Sajangnim. The maknae of T-ara eating dinner at her CEO’s table? That’s not suspicious. That’s practically office culture.”

“Sajangnim?” Sulli’s head actually swung — that word landed harder than the passcode had. She turned to Song Ji-yeon. “Oppa — don’t you run restaurant companies? Since when are you Ji-yeon-unnie’s sajangnim?!”

He laughed at the whiplash, reached over, and gave her head a light rub.

“Long story. The short version: I picked up an entertainment company along the way. As of this week, T-ara are my company’s artists. And this one—” a nod at the smug creature beside him, “—lives one floor up and has been freeloading since the day she moved in.”

“Ohhh! So THAT’S the thing!” Sulli nodded along, comprehending perhaps forty percent — but the only clause she actually needed had parsed clean: not living together. The remaining sixty percent could go uncatalogued forever, as far as she was concerned.

“Eat! Talk and eat! It’s getting cold!”

He raised his voice a notch, because he could feel another round of questions forming in two heads at once, and he was hungry, and — this was the part that quietly killed him — neither the peach nor the little dragon had so much as touched their chopsticks since sitting down, which meant the eating could not officially begin until he ordered it. Again.

The table finally woke up. The three of them ate and talked — mostly him asking Ji-yeon how the day had gone, and how the meeting had gone, and whether anyone had given them any trouble.

And Ji-yeon’s account matched Eun-jung’s, word for word: Kim Kwang-soo hadn’t tried anything clever. No traps, no pressure, no last-minute games. For once, everything — absolutely everything — had gone easy.
