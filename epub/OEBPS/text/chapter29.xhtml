Chapter Twenty-Nine

Caught Lying Again!

SUNDAY EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment — dinner for two

“Couldn’t handle it? Please. I eat spicy food all the time.”

Eun-jung lifted her chin, shoulders squared like this was a weigh-in. Her shoulder-length hair swung softly at her ears with the motion. True, her heart belonged to sweet-and-spicy — but she could hold her own against pure heat, thank you very much. Don’t look down on people.

Song Ji-yeon watched her sit there radiating competitive urgency, and let a smile climb one corner of his mouth. His head tipped a degree.

“Alright. Tonight, then — show me exactly how well you hold up.”

They took their seats on opposite sides of the table. He slid a pair of disposable gloves across to her and flicked his eyes toward the mountain of crayfish: start there.

She took them and worked them onto her slim fingers like she’d done this before, then locked onto the plumpest crayfish in the pile with surgical accuracy.

The crayfish had been picked out of the fish market that very afternoon — big ones, gleaming under their coat of chili oil and garlic, the flesh tight as a fist. The wok had driven the chili-bean paste and the Sichuan peppercorns all the way down into the grain of the meat, numbing and scorching in the same breath, so that the first bite sent heat and the crayfish’s own sweetness flooding across the tongue at once — a depth of flavor no kitchen in this city bothered to aim for.

“Hsss— so spicy — but this is — this is incredible!”

One piece of meat in, and her bright eyes had lit up like stage lights.

She hissed, fanned her mouth with her free hand, and kept eating anyway. The heat had laid a thin pink flush across her cheeks.

“Here. Tomato and egg — put the fire out.” He reached over with his chopsticks and set a piece of tomato in her bowl.

Sweet-sour juice and soft egg drowned the burn in a way water never manages. And from there she stopped pretending entirely: tomato and egg, braised ribs, big open-mouthed bites of rice — every calorie rule an idol lives by, abandoned on the spot.

“Song Ji-yeon, your cooking is actually ridiculous.” Mouth stuffed, the words came out shapeless — a hamster in full hoarding mode.

“I’m starting to think every Chinese meal I’ve ever eaten in this country was a counterfeit.”

“That’s because chefs here sugar their Chinese food to death.” He leaned back against the chair, watching her eat with her guard all the way down, and felt something in his chest come loose — an ease he couldn’t put a name to.

“Whenever you want the real thing, just send me a message. One dinner is always something I can manage.”

She sniffled — her nose had gone pink from the spice — and was quietly, absurdly touched. And then her brain caught up with the sentence.

This morning she’d messaged to remind him not to forget their promised dinner. His reply had come back flat: busy today — company business. And now, to her face: message me anytime?

“Weren’t you… supposed to be at the office today?” She pursed her lips. “When I asked this morning — didn’t you say your whole afternoon was file work?”

“Finished early.”

He delivered the excuse with a face like still water. His eyes, less disciplined, slid half an inch off-center — because when her message had landed that morning, he’d already promised Sulli the set visit.

He could hardly announce that his afternoon of paperwork had actually been Han River Park and another girl on his activation list. He wasn’t stupid enough to hold up one woman’s name in front of another.

…Tally it up, and today he’d lied to Sulli and to Eun-jung. Both of them.

No — hold on. When exactly did I become the two-timing-scumbag type?

✦ ✦ ✦

She ate until she was properly, completely full. Three platters, cleared almost to the china — the crayfish mountain reduced to a reef of empty shells, the ribs picked clean of their dark caramel glaze, the tomato and egg scraped to the bottom of the bowl. Then she stood, stacking her own dishes out of pure reflex, and volunteered for dish duty — and was pushed gently but firmly back toward the living room by a hand covered in dish soap.

“Go sit. You’re the guest — what kind of host hands the guest a sponge?”

She pressed her lips together around a smile, gave in, and folded herself obediently onto the leather sofa.

The TV was running an entertainment-news roundup, the host tearing through the week’s idol gossip in the most exaggerated register Korean broadcasting had to offer. Ji-yeon came out of the kitchen with a plate of chilled watermelon, cut and ready, and muted the racket on his way past.

He sat down beside her — a careful distance, not too close, not too far — and his gaze settled on the faint tiredness in her profile.

“Weren’t you in meetings all day? You look like you danced from nine to nine.” She’d been drifting toward sleep a minute ago.

Eun-jung’s fork was halfway to a watermelon cube. The motion stalled — a small hitch, nothing — and the glow the meal had put in her face drained quietly out of it. Her lashes dropped.

“After the meeting… we ran choreography.” A smile, assembled rather than felt. Her voice had gone low. “It’s not like there’s any other schedule to run. If we’re not in the practice room dancing, there’s honestly nothing else to do.”

He knew there was more under that. T-ara’s standing in Korea, after the bullying scandal, had hit rock bottom and kept drilling — commercials gone, variety bookings gone, even the music-show invitations cancelled wholesale. What kept the group’s lights on was the leftover thread of overseas schedules and the drip of royalties from old releases, and it was a thin thread. Six members, a skeleton staff, and a name the portals couldn’t print often enough with the word bully attached.

Choreography. Now. At a time like this.

He let his skeptical stare sit on her, and she lasted a surprisingly long time under it. Then a long breath went out of her; she sank into the sofa until it held her whole weight, slim legs swinging idly over the edge, and confessed everything in a limp, unguarded voice.

“The CEO says we can’t just walk away from the Korean market. He wants to build two new sub-units — one focused here at home, one for overseas, Japan — and see if that wins back some of the public.”

“Two sub-units?” His brow creased a degree. “Who goes where?”

“Me, Ji-yeon, Hyo-min, and Areum — T-ara N4. Bo-ram-unnie, Qri-unnie, and So-yeon-unnie — T-ara QBS.”

She hugged her knees, eyes adrift somewhere past the floor-to-ceiling windows, out over Seoul’s glitter. “That’s what today’s meeting was about. The CEO thinks if the sub-units put up big numbers at home, Korean fans will finally understand we’re not the kind of people who’d freeze out a member. So — afternoon choreography.”

His mouth pulled sideways, helpless. History, running on rails. In his first life, Kim Kwang-soo’s answer to the bullying scandal had been exactly this — split the group. T-ara N4 for the home market, T-ara QBS pointed at Japan, and call the pressure dispersed. Nothing solved.

“It won’t work.”

He dropped it flat — no polish, no cushion, no room left for negotiation.

“…What?”

She hadn’t caught up.

“I said Kim Kwang-soo’s idea is pure fantasy.” He turned to face her, and there was nothing soft left in his eyes. “Korean netizens aren’t arguing with the facts right now, Eun-jung. There is no fact good enough. It’s a feeding frenzy — and in front of a crowd like that, nothing you do will ever read as right. You could perform perfectly and it would be entered into evidence against you.”

“And Japan? Does Kim Kwang-soo think CCM has too much money in the account, and it needs burning?”

“A two-front sub-unit buys you nothing. It grinds your bodies down and splits the fandom down the middle — and that’s the complete list of things it does.”

He was reading off a ledger the world hadn’t printed yet. In his first life, N4’s numbers had landed with a thud — worse, the lead single had a line in it, mind your own business, that the internet collectively decided was a swing at Ryu Hwa-young. A fresh round of boycotts, right on schedule. All cost, no return.

She knew all of it, probably better than he did. “But… what else can we do except what the company decides? We’re the company’s artists, Song Ji-yeon.”

A thin tremor ran under the words, and she didn’t try to hide it.

He watched her shoulders — shaking, faintly — and pulled a slow breath all the way down. Then he reached over and set his hand on one of them, light as a paperweight.

“What if I could get you out of this?”

She lifted her head, eyes rimmed red, no plan left for hiding it. “Out? Out to where?”

“Eun-jung. What would you say… if I bought you?”

“Chincha?!”

The watermelon fork slipped out of her fingers and struck the plate — one bright, clean clink. Her eyes went round; her whole small, fine face filled up, top to bottom, with a single word: absurd.
