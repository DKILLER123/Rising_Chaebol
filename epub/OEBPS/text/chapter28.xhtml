Chapter Twenty-Eight

Something Cooking in the Kitchen

SEPTEMBER 2012 Yeouido · Han River Park the parking lot, late afternoon

Ji-yeon walked out of the park, pulled the car door open, and dropped into the driver’s seat.

He didn’t reach for the ignition. He reached for his phone instead, and typed out a message to Ham Eun-jung.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Song Ji-yeon

Busy? If not — dinner tonight?

He hadn’t forgotten the dinner he’d promised her. And there was no day like today.

A minute passed. Two. Nothing came back.

Strange. T-ara’s Korean schedules had all been suspended — by the time he and Eun-jung first met, the girls had already been resting for the better part of a month. Free time was the one thing she ought to have had in reserve. Unless — dance practice.

He shook his head, turned the key, and the Mercedes slid out onto Olympic-daero, riding the river west, steady as a train. The ginkgoes along the shoulder were edging into yellow now; under the flat white afternoon sun, every leaf looked like a flake of hammered gold.

A crisp chime from the center console. He arched a brow, and at the next red light, one hand free, he swiped the screen open.

Ham Eun-jung

Yah! I was in a meeting — my phone was buried in my bag. I just saw this.

[ a chubby bunny sticker, flat on its back, profoundly wronged ]

Tonight?? Then I want tomato-and-egg stir-fry. And braised ribs. Oh — and crayfish!!

The menu arrived before the yes. He laughed out loud, alone, in a stopped car.

This girl and her crayfish — the craving had survived intact, all these weeks. Though it was already September. Did the markets here even still carry crayfish this late? He had no idea. He promised anyway.

Song Ji-yeon

Done. Your wish, my command. My place: Unit 0101, Cheongdam Daewoo Royal County, 30 Dosan-daero 100-gil, Cheongdam-dong. Door code 0818 — punch it in when you get here and make yourself at home. I’m stopping for groceries on the way.

He tossed the phone onto the passenger seat, wheeled the car around at the next junction, and pointed it toward a big international grocer in Itaewon.

His regular stop — the one place in Seoul he trusted with a proper Chinese pantry. The air conditioning ran arctic inside; he pushed the cart through the aisles like a man walking his own hallway. He’d promised her, and he didn’t do promises at half power. If he was cooking, it was going to be the real thing.

Down the condiment aisle his hands worked on autopilot: proper doubanjiang — the fermented chili-bean paste the local knockoffs somehow always sweetened; a bottle of black rice vinegar; and a sealed brick of hotpot base for the mala crayfish. Korean-made seasonings ran sweet across the board, and sweet was exactly the dimension this food could not spare. Hence the detour to Itaewon.

Ribs and dry goods in the car, he drove on to the fish market and came away with a full kilo of crayfish.

✦ ✦ ✦

SEPTEMBER 2012 Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Cheongdam Daewoo Royal County · Unit 0101

By the time he walked back into the building’s underground garage with two bags cutting into his fingers, the clock had advanced exactly forty minutes.

The elevator rose straight to the ground floor. He’d barely swiped the fingerprint lock and shouldered through the heavy entry door when he caught it — small, careful sounds from somewhere inside. And there, in the entryway, neatly aligned: a pair of white women’s sneakers, soles scrubbed clean.

Message received. Ham Eun-jung had beaten him home.

“That was fast.”

He shut the door behind him and carried the bags in.

Eun-jung sat on the leather sofa in a loose home hoodie, freshly showered — her short hair still carried a dampness to it, fluffed softly over her ears, and a faint clean scent of body wash moved whenever she did. At the sound of him she stood, a little stiffly, hands gripping the hem of the hoodie, wearing a small, abashed smile.

“So, um — the code opened on the first try, so I… let myself in. And — this is for you. A housewarming thing.” She picked two economy packs of tissue off the coffee table and held them out with both hands, still visibly off-balance.

He took her in with a sidelong look. The fidgeting, the formal gift, the four different ways of not quite meeting his eyes — she had the exact doomed energy of a son-in-law making his first entrance at the in-laws’.

“What’s there to be nervous about? I’m the one who gave you the code — come whenever you like. And what’s with the gifts?” Why Koreans brought toilet paper on a first visit he would honestly never understand — his own mother had done it at every LA housewarming for twenty years and had never once explained herself — but he respected the institution. He hoisted his bags, which were reaching the limit of his fingers. “Tissue on the table’s fine. I’ll start cooking.”

“Ne.” The two packs went back onto the coffee table.

Smiling, he hauled the heavy bags into the kitchen, upended everything onto the marble island, and unbuttoned his cuffs — rolling them back in two turns, baring a clean, hard line of forearm.

“Do you need help?” She’d followed him in without quite deciding to — drawn along, natural as breathing. She did have some confidence in the kitchen, for the record: she cooked for herself at home, Korean food mostly. Chinese she’d never attempted. But assistant work she could manage.

He hesitated — then the eagerness on her face won.

“Then I’ll trouble Eun-jung-ssi as my prep cook.”

She skipped off to wash her hands and came back with her hair twisted up into a cheeky little sprout, the clean white line of her nape bared. She planted herself at the island and fixed on the ribs poking out of the bag with an openly covetous stare.

“What do we do first?”

“First, we blanch the ribs.” The cleaver came out of the block and spun one lazy flower at his fingertips. Custom-forged, a proper Chinese cleaver — he’d had it made after a month of failing to get along with the narrow, dainty blades Korean stores sold.

The flourish startled a small cry out of her, hand flying to her mouth.

“Yah — your hands!”

“Relax. I’ve been doing this since I could see over the cutting board.” Brow up — blade down. The rack of ribs came apart into even, thumping pieces. Knife work like that didn’t leave you: he’d logged the hours as a kid in his father’s kitchens, and then, all through high school and USC, out in the San Gabriel Valley — hovering at the edge of Sichuan and Hunan uncle-shops’ wok stations, a restaurant kid tolerated, taught how a cleaver was supposed to move, fed at closing time for his trouble.

He assigned Eun-jung the tomatoes and the crayfish. She filled the basin — tomatoes blazing red, scallions, ginger, garlic — and worked them over with her fingertips, gentle as laundry, humming “Day by Day” under her breath, swaying a little on the beat.

She was having the time of her life back there, so he let her to it. Wok on the burner, water in, ribs down. The pot came up to a murmur, then a grumble; the smell of pork and cooking wine unrolled through the kitchen and took the room over completely.

He turned his head and watched her profile, bent over the basin, absorbed. The late sunlight came in slantwise and gilded the damp on her lashes.

“Eun-jung.”

“Mm?” She looked around, a washed tomato still in hand.

“Apron. On the hook by the door — the oil smoke gets serious in a minute.” He nodded at the wall.

She trotted over and fetched it — and then got stuck, arms bent behind her, the strings refusing every attempt. She looked over at him for help, eyes gone soft. At the dorm this was Park Ji-yeon’s job. Today there was only Song Ji-yeon.

He laughed under his breath, crossed over, and took the strings from her hands — fingertips skimming, without meaning to, the small of her back. A waist a man could nearly circle.

She went faintly still. The tips of her ears turned red at a speed you could watch. She glanced sideways — and found him bent over the bow, all concentration, entirely innocent of everything. Something nameless and small dropped through her chest like a stone. Disappointment, she would have admitted, if pressed.

She had always been thin-skinned about exactly these things. After all this time in his orbit, something had wound itself around her heart without her catching the moment it happened — a thread, a filament. And all this time he had kept a perfect gentleman’s distance: in their entire acquaintance, the only time he had ever touched her was on that plane. Every other day of it, manners. She couldn’t get a read on the game at all. Playing hard to get — or genuinely not thinking of her that way?

Did she seriously have to be the one to move first?

“There.” He stepped back, voice gone low. He registered none of the weather that had just crossed her face.

If he had known, he would have howled on the spot — Noona, you could have said so sooner! One word and I’d have skipped the whole gentleman act — twenty-two years of my eomma’s etiquette drills have ruined my nerve.

“Ne… thank you.”

✦ ✦ ✦

The last dish — tomato and egg — came off the wok. Bright red sauce gripping gold curds, sweet-and-sour steam charging around the room like it owned the place.

On the island, the spread stood ready: mala crayfish, giants every one, armored in red chili oil and crushed garlic; and the braised ribs, caramel-dark and gleaming, each piece lacquered in its own thick coat of glaze.

“It smells insane. Better than the Chinese restaurants in Apgujeong.” Her throat moved. She ferried the last two bowls of rice to the table, never once taking her eyes off the red basin of crayfish.

“Ji-yeon-ssi… I had no idea you could cook like this.” Pure, unguarded admiration. A chef, this whole time — hidden inside the hotpot CEO.

He pulled the apron over his head, tossed it across the back of a chair, and let the smugness arrive on schedule.

“Naturally. The Chinese restaurants over here tune every dish toward the local palate — they win the market and lose the food. I cooked tonight the way I learned it back in L.A. — San Gabriel Valley rules, no compromises. Of course it beats the restaurants.” A beat. “One warning, though — it runs spicy. I’m worried it might be too much for you.”
