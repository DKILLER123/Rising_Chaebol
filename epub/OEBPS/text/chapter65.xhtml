Chapter Sixty-Five

The Wrap Party

TUESDAY Mapo-gu, Seoul SAVORY F&B — the president’s office

Song Ji-yeon spent the rest of the day in his office.

The company had nothing pressing — a few documents that legally required his signature, and that was the entire crisis load. Park Seon-young and Kim Min-hyun were more than capable men; most problems died quietly on their desks and never once climbed the stairs to the president’s office.

Honestly, if it weren’t for the signatures, he wouldn’t have needed to show up at all.

All afternoon, his eyes stayed parked on the candlestick chart glowing across the monitor — Sewon Electronics, mounting its staircase one green candle at a time. Ten-point-four billion won of his money, up there breathing.

At six, the sky outside the window began to lose its light.

He rubbed the ache out of his eyes and was reaching to shut the laptop when the phone rang. A glance at the screen. Sulli. He picked up.

Incoming call · Sulli → Song Ji-yeon

“Oppa! I wrapped!!”

The voice came sparkling out of a wall of noise — cheering, music, glasses ringing against glasses.

“The whole crew’s celebrating at a private club here in Cheongdam-dong, and Director-nim told me to invite you over! You visited the set twice, oppa — he keeps saying he owes you a proper thank-you!”

“All right. I’m on my way.”

An invitation — to him? He wasn’t crew. He turned it over for about one second before the gears showed through the casing: this director was a quick operator. What he wanted a closer look at was the Sajangnim title. And what came after the introduction was, naturally, the trivial little matter of investment.

He thought it over. Nothing was waiting for him at home, and he’d never actually seen a drama wrap. Might as well go and see what one looked like.

So he stood, took his coat off the rack, pocketed the car keys, and went.

Halfway there he passed a florist and pulled over. It was a custom he’d carried with him from the States — you did not walk into a wrap celebration empty-handed, and flowers were the safe currency.

“Two bouquets,” he told the owner. “Something that looks nice without looking ceremonial.”

This crew held more than Sulli, after all — there was Kim Ji-won too, and both of them, as it happened, were names written in the Goddess Handbook. She hadn’t called him about her wrap, but he could hardly arrive with flowers for one and air for the other.

The owner’s hands flew, and two bunches went over the counter wrapped in kraft paper — white roses, and baby’s breath.

The white roses had been a deliberate pick. Sulli’s favorite — he remembered.

The baby’s breath was arithmetic. It went with everything, and what else was he supposed to do, not knowing what flowers Kim Ji-won liked?

Paid, both bouquets riding in the passenger seat, he drove on toward Cheongdam-dong.

✦ ✦ ✦

TUESDAY EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul a private club down a quiet side street

The club sat at the end of a hushed little back street, a row of agency vans parked along its curb. He found a spot, gathered up the flowers, and went in.

Inside, the lights burned at full and the music ran louder than it strictly needed to — crew and cast clustered in twos and threes, drinking, talking over the beat, the whole room thick with celebration. One sweep located exactly two familiar faces, and he walked straight toward them.

Sulli was still in the drama’s boys’ school uniform, the short hair raked into party disarray, toasting somebody with a glass of juice, eyes bent into crescents. She spotted him and lit up like a marquee — set the glass down and was already launching across the room.

He smiled and cut her off at the pass, arriving first, one bouquet held out.

“Congratulations on the wrap.”

“Wa — thank you, oppa!” The delight came out at broadcast volume. She took the bouquet, buried her nose in the kraft paper, and inhaled like a diver surfacing — loud enough that half the room turned to look. “It smells amazing!”

He let her sparkle, took up the second bouquet, and crossed to the other side of the room.

Kim Ji-won had changed out of costume — a plain white T-shirt and jeans, black-frame glasses on her nose — sitting quietly contained while the party heaved around her, a comma of stillness in all the noise. She saw him coming and rose, a beat of surprise lifting her out of her chair.

“Ji-won-ssi — congratulations on the wrap, too.” The bouquet arrived at arm’s length.

She went still. Her eyes made a slow lap — the flowers, then him — and in those clear, quiet eyes, confusion crossed briefly with astonishment.

It hadn’t occurred to her that Song Ji-yeon would bring her flowers. Honestly counted, the two of them barely qualified as acquainted. He’d saved her once, on the set — and that was the entire ledger between them. No Kakao IDs exchanged, never mind phone numbers.

“Thank you, Song Ji-yeon-ssi.” She took them anyway. The thanks came out at half volume, and her cheeks came up faintly warm.

The movement tripped the director’s radar. Jeon Ki-sang came over glass-first, alcohol already glowing in his cheeks.

“Aigoo — Song Ji-yeon-ssi! You made it! Come in, come in — sit, sit!”

“Congratulations on a clean wrap, Director.” A smile, a touch of glasses.

“Aigoo, Sajangnim Song is far too kind!” The director’s grin had run out of room on his face. “Come, come — let me introduce you to our leads.”

The hand on his arm towed him toward two young actors, the flush riding high now and the volume with it.

“Sajangnim Song — formally, now.” A flourish at the tall, handsome one. “Our leading man — Choi Min-ho.”

“And our second lead — Lee Hyun-woo.”

“Gentlemen.” A hand out, a smile.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi — we’ve actually met already.” Choi Min-ho shook with a grin wide open at both ends. “A while back.”

“We have,” Lee Hyun-woo agreed, nodding along. “When you visited the set — we all sat around talking.”

“That we did.” He drew his hand back. “But tonight’s the wrap party. Different weight entirely. Congratulations, both of you — it was a long climb.”

“Aigoo — the climbing was the director’s and the crew’s.” Choi Min-ho waved it off, all modesty. “We just flap our gums for a living.”

“Enough, enough — nobody stands around.” Jeon Ki-sang, listening beside them, looked ready to levitate. “Sit! Tonight, nobody goes home sober!”

The big private room kept filling — screenwriter Lee Young-chul, two assistant directors, round after round of bows and names. The assistant directors slid off him without leaving a trace. Lee Young-chul, though, he knew by reputation: the man had written the original High Kick! and its third round, High Kick: Revenge of the Short-Legged — two of the Peninsula’s great eat-while-watching sitcoms. A famous screenwriter, full stop.

✦ ✦ ✦

Dozens of people packed shoulder to shoulder now, food steam and liquor fumes braided into one warm fog.

Glasses went up all around the room.

“Here’s to To the Beautiful You — wrapped clean! Geonbae!”

“Geonbae!”

Glass hit glass, and the room detonated into cheering.

After that, the night belonged to drinking and one-upping war stories.

Choi Min-ho contributed the wire-harness epic: strung up in midair, swinging, for the better part of half an hour — legs made entirely of water by the time they lowered him down. Lee Hyun-woo counter-filed on the director’s cruelty: one wrong look in the eyes and it was NG, reset, again — a single line of his had run past twenty takes before it earned its exit.

Song Ji-yeon listened, quiet, raising his glass on cue, dropping in a sentence here and there.

“Acting really is a brutal trade.”

“A strict director means high standards. To the Beautiful You is going to hit.”

He spent words like a miser, and every one of them landed on Jeon Ki-sang’s sweet spot. Round by round, the director’s private estimate was quietly redlining. He had braced for a chaebol’s ornamental son, hanging around the actresses for atmosphere — and five sentences in, he could hear it plainly: this one was no ornament. The investment angle was looking better by the glass.

Across the room, Sulli and Kim Ji-won sat tucked in with a knot of actresses. Sulli ran hot tonight — the shoot finally dead and buried, her whole body unwound, laughing at everything with her eyes bent shut. Kim Ji-won was quieter by half, sipping juice in small sips, listening more than she spoke — her gaze, now and then, drifting somewhere it hadn’t exactly been invited.

She had concluded that this man was genuinely strange. A company president, supposedly — and yet not a gram of the merchant’s shine on him, no ledger flickering behind the eyes. He sat among actors, traded industry talk without a seam showing. Like an old friend nobody could quite remember meeting.

A few rounds deeper, the director’s face had graduated from glowing to strobing. He steered himself over with a full glass, dropped onto the bench beside Song Ji-yeon, and slung an arm around his shoulders with the ease of an old teammate.

“Sajangnim Song...” A hiccup arrived ahead of the sentence, carrying its own weather front.

Song Ji-yeon sidled over an inch, expression immaculate.

“Young as you are... an empire that size...” The tongue was thickening. “Remarkable. Any interest... in putting a little money... into our line of work?”

Every ear within range stood straight up.

He smiled, picked up the bottle, filled the director’s emptied glass, and topped up his own.

“Director — you flatter me.” Glasses touched. “I sell hotpot. Your film business is a whole sky above my ceiling.”

“Ai, you can’t talk like that!” The director’s hand sawed the air. “Everyone’s heard! That ONGI of yours — the hottest thing in Seoul right now! You clear more in a day than a whole production costs to shoot!”

An exaggeration, obviously. He didn’t argue with it — just drank, and let it stand.

“So what is the director actually proposing?”

“What I’m proposing—” The man leaned in, dropping his voice into conspiracy range. “—is have you ever thought about backing a television drama? There’s a script sitting in my desk this very minute. Hit material — guaranteed! The funding lands, and I shoot you the drama of the year. My personal guarantee.”

Song Ji-yeon looked at the pitch galloping toward him and was privately, warmly entertained.

“Can it make money?” — delivered with exactly the weight of a joke.

“Can it MAKE money?!” The director thumped his own chest like a notarized document.

“Then I’m in.” A grin. “Any business that makes money interests me. If you’ve really got a good script, Director, bring it to me whenever. If the script’s good — the money’s the easy part.”

Pretty talk. The director’s face got its cushion, and not a single door anywhere actually opened. T-ara’s contracts were still unsigned; the label existed on paper and adrenaline — backing dramas now would be sprinting ahead of the baton. But a road could be laid early, and roads didn’t complain about waiting.

The director’s delight went up like a lantern, and three more cups became law.

The room kept heating. Somewhere in it, a voice stood up on a chair and hollered:

“Sulli-yah! Big night, big mood — how about a number to keep it going?!”

“Yes — dance, dance!”

“One number! One number!”

The whole room caught it — table-slapping, whistling, clapping rhythm.

Sulli went pink to the ears, but her eyes had already lit. She checked one direction — found him watching, found the encouragement sitting right there in his smile — and set down her juice.

“Then... I’ll embarrass myself for your entertainment!”

She cleared a square of floor, moving chairs aside with the efficiency of a veteran of a hundred staff parties.

Somebody’s phone found the room’s Bluetooth speaker, and the intro hit — and her entire atmosphere changed state.

FLOOR STAGE · CHOI JIN-RI — f(x), “HOT SUMMER” · WRAP PARTY, CHEONGDAM

The drums landed first, and the whole party converted to fuel in one bar. In the boy’s uniform she’d worn for three months of takes, she danced the room small.

Waist. Head-snap. Kick. Every count struck to the millimeter, as if the milliseconds had signed contracts. The oversized uniform hid none of it — the lines arrived through the fabric like weather through a window.

Small floor, total commitment. A fine sweat surfaced on her forehead within the first chorus and caught the house lights; by the second she was grinning into the choreography like the choreography owed her money.

Hot summer — hot hot hot summer —

The room clapped in time, chanted, whistled. Minho stood for a whistle of stadium quality. From his chair, Song Ji-yeon watched with the appetite of a man being paid to attend.

And credit, where due: on any stage, this child simply emitted. The certainty in the line of her — nothing in common with the offstage peach who priced the world in grapefruit soda and lost arguments with doors.

Track out. Ending pose, struck and held. Breathing hard; smile at full legal wattage.

The room went up.

“Wa — Sulli, that was incredible!”

“That’s our ace for you!”

She bowed through the noise, trotted back to her seat, seized the juice, and downed most of it in one pull.

That broke the dam. Actors filed up after her — one trembling ballad, one heroic trot, deliveries of every possible altitude, nobody caring, the whole point being the noise. The party rolled on through all of it: drinking, talking, games with no rules and no losers.

By the time the clock’s hand stood at eleven, the room was finally leaking people. The director had a broadcasters’ meeting in the morning; the actors had schedules breathing down their necks. Nobody could honestly stay later. Farewells stacked up at the door, and the crowd funneled out into the street in twos and threes.

Sulli and Kim Ji-won fell in beside him.

“Oppa — thank you for the flowers. I really love them.” Sulli still had the white roses in her arms, cheeks glowing pink — from the juice, officially.

“Thank you as well, Song Ji-yeon-ssi.” Quieter, from Kim Ji-won, temperate as ever.

“Don’t mention it. Go home and get some rest — you both have work tomorrow?”

“Ne — magazine cover shoot in the morning.”

“I’m off. I finally get to sleep in.” Six words, carrying an entire paragraph of relief.

“Perfect — I’ll run you back to the hotel, then.”

Sulli squinted at him, grinning.

“Oppa. We’ve wrapped. What hotel?”

He had, in fact, embarrassed himself — how had he forgotten that the wrap killed the accommodation along with everything else?

“...Fair. Dorm, then. And you, Ji-won-ssi?”

She shook her head and pointed down the street — a black agency van idling at the corner.

“No need, oppa — my manager’s waiting over there.”

He blinked. Kim Ji-won was calling him oppa now? Since when? The question formed, got filed, and never reached his mouth; the van was exactly where she’d pointed, and that was that. He nodded.

“Then I’ll get Sulli home.”

“Ne! Good night, oppa — bye, Sulli!” A small wave, a quick jog, and she folded herself into the van.
