Chapter Twenty-Seven

Kim Ji-won

SEPTEMBER 2012 Yeouido · Han River Park To the Beautiful You — between takes

Kim Ji-won — second female lead of To the Beautiful You.

Twenty years old this year, a last softness of baby fat still rounding her face, skin so fine it looked like it would give under a fingertip — and already, in the set of her brows and eyes, the warm, quick-grained glow of the “national actress” she would one day be.

His own impression of her ran on a longer reel. The last time he’d watched this face, it had been carrying a megahit — Queen of Tears, years up the timeline, in his first life.

“Ah — Ji-won-unnie!” Sulli shot up from her chair, flustered, setting her cup down fast. “I’ve got the lines down, I promise. Oh — let me introduce—” She gestured at Ji-yeon. “My friend. Song Ji-yeon — Sajangnim of SAVORY F&B.”

Kim Ji-won blinked. Her gaze rested on him for exactly half a second.

She’d taken him for one of Sulli’s trainee friends — clean-cut, tidy, roughly their age. A CEO at that age meant one thing: family money had cleared the road. Hah. A chaebol heir. Her interest in Song Ji-yeon closed for the afternoon.

“Annyeonghaseyo, Song Ji-yeon-ssi. I’m Kim Ji-won.” The bow was graceful and just deep enough. Her voice was soft and warm, with a thin, even layer of distance poured over it.

“Thank you for the drinks today — everyone loves them.”

“You’re very kind, Kim Ji-won-ssi. Please — just Ji-yeon.” He rose and returned the bow.

His gaze crossed her face, and something in him tipped its hat. Next to the loud, live-wire bloom of Sulli’s youth, Kim Ji-won’s beauty ran quiet and inward — a cup of warm water: nothing at first glance, and then very hard to stop looking at.

“Ji-won-unnie, Song Ji-yeon-oppa’s really nice,” Sulli chimed in, unable to keep a small pride out of it — and drifted half a step closer to him while she was at it. “He drove all the way here with fifty drinks just to visit the set.”

“Did he? Then today I owe Sulli for my free drinks.” Ji-won’s lips curved — but her eyes made a slow, kindly circuit of the two of them, one eyebrow of the mind raised. A man her age who already ran a company: a chaebol heir, plainly. And Sulli was young. What did he actually want from the kid? Would he hurt her?

None of which reached Sulli, who was still beaming like a billboard.

“Well — I’ll leave you two to it. The director seems to be calling me.” She lifted the script in a small salute and nodded to him. “Song Ji-yeon-ssi — excuse me.”

She didn’t care for the species — chaebol boys — and couldn’t be bothered to manufacture an excuse. She simply left.

“Of course. Go ahead.” Ji-yeon’s nod came with a smile.

He watched her cross the gravel toward the director’s chairs, and thoughtfully rubbed his chin.

“Oppa. What are we looking at?” Sulli leaned into his sightline, immovable, a trace of vinegar in her tone. “Isn’t Ji-won-unnie pretty?”

“She’s all right.” He surfaced, amused, and flicked her lightly on the forehead. “But our Sulli is still the most dashing.”

“Yah — what kind of compliment is that?! No. I’m giving you one chance to reorganize your language and compliment me again.”

She rubbed at the spot, complaining the whole time — and the smile in her eyes and brows refused all attempts to hide it.

He looked at her — at that face, at the baggy boys’ uniform hanging off her shoulders. It had to be said. The kid was manufactured to a tolerance the industry had no right to expect. Even with the crop, the freshness leaked out of her everywhere. Dressed as a boy, she wasn’t so much a handsome young man as the delicate, pretty kind of boy who gets followed down the street by creeps.

“You really want the truth?” He settled deeper into the folding chair, eyes smiling.

“Ne!” Chin up, neck stiff.

“It’s honestly hard to rate.” Four words — delivered with total sincerity.

“Handsome? Your face is pinker than the grapefruit I bought today. Pretty? One glance at that haircut and the entire country stops guessing girl.”

Two full seconds of silence. Then a wail of pure, civilization-ending despair.

“YAH! How can Song Ji-yeon-oppa say that! Where, exactly, am I not girl-like?!” She thrust out the chest she did not, strictly speaking, have — evidence, apparently.

His mouth twitched. Thrust away. That’s maybe half a size on Lim Yoon-ah — what is there to be proud of?

She caught the look on his face. Shame and fury arrived together; the script came up, aimed at his skull — and stopped at half-arm, because wrinkled pages would cause shooting trouble. She brandished it twice in the empty air instead, all fury and no delivery.

“Okay, okay — teasing.” He rescued the slightly deformed script and flipped through it: lines packed edge to edge, roads of highlighter running between them. “Look at this. Our Sulli is genuinely serious about the work. This attitude — light-years ahead of the field.”

Praise received: tail, skyward. She sat back down and took a heroic pull of soda — then sat there, teeth chattering around the straw.

“Of course! Do you know what I’ve poured into this drama? Oppa, you don’t know. The high-jump scenes — I jumped until my legs were ready to fall off. And the director keeps telling me my takeoff ‘lacks elegance.’ Please. I’m a girl, okay? I have to clear the bar and look like a national-team athlete doing it. Is that a reasonable ask?”

She poured it all out at him, unstoppable. Times like this, she didn’t need wisdom or perspective. She needed a hollow tree — somewhere to set the trouble down.

Ji-yeon listened, quiet.

This vivid girl, still grumbling over one note from a director. The memories of her — his other life’s memories — surfaced again. The Sulli of this stretch was tired, yes. But her eyes still shone with the future. That other thing — the dark that would one day wrap her whole world, the malice of an entire internet — was still some distance down the road.

And he was here now. Bound, absurdly, to this Handbook.

Which meant those things were simply not going to happen. Not while he was standing in the way.

“Don’t run yourself past the line.” He reached out and pressed her bangs back down — they’d gone airborne with all the gesturing. “Acting is a job. The body’s yours for life. The day you truly can’t jump anymore, tell the director you’re taking the break. Worst case, I ship over a few more truckloads of drinks until his mouth is too busy to complain.”

She very nearly sprayed her soda across the lawn.

“Oppa — are you suppressing the director with pure money power?” A tongue poked out; the face behind it was all quicksilver. “…Though honestly? I kind of love the big-spender energy. Leave it to our Sajangnim.”

They talked a while longer. At one point she performed a stretch of her drama dialogue for him on the spot — the stiff, straining-for-masculinity delivery, laid over that soft, marshmallow face. The dissonance cleared the clouds and kept climbing.

“Song Ji-yeon-oppa — how was that? Presence, right?” The nonexistent chest came out again, going for awe-inspiring.

He watched her in silence for a long moment. In the end, all he could do was put a hand on her shoulder.

“Sulli-ya. Fewer boy roles from now on.”

“Why?!”

“I’m worried nobody will ever marry you.”

“YAH! You came here today specifically to shorten my life, didn’t you!”

She was up like a spring, and for the next minute Ji-yeon’s life consisted of being chased in circles along the path beside the set.

✦ ✦ ✦

Some way off, Kim Ji-won — who had been quietly keeping tabs on the two of them — watched them orbit each other and shook her head, half resigned.

In all her time on this set she had never seen Sulli this loose. The kid smiled on schedule, but it always came with a coat of politeness and caution over it. In front of this Song fellow, she was a feral child on summer holiday.

Maybe she had misjudged it. Maybe Song Ji-yeon wasn’t the species of chaebol heir she’d filed him under.

The assistant director’s megaphone crackled.

“Sulli-ssi! You’re up — come to positions, please!”

The noise cut off like a switch had been thrown. Sulli stopped mid-stride, drew one deep breath — and the laughing face flipped over into work mode with a sound he almost heard. She shoved her soda into Manager Kim’s hands, turned back to Ji-yeon.

“Oppa — I’m off to shoot.” A thin reluctance ran under it. “Are you leaving right after?”

“Mm. There are files waiting on my desk — I have to swing by the office.” Smiling, he straightened the collar her chasing had knocked askew. “Shoot well. When this drama finishes airing — dinner’s on me. The big one.”

It was a weekend; there were no files anywhere. But an audience of one, parked at the monitor, would only tangle her concentration — so he lied, kindly, and took his leave.

“Pinky!” The little finger came up between them, held at eye level. She never seemed to tire of the ritual — invoked it now like notarization.

Helpless, he hooked it.

“There. Sealed — no take-backs for a hundred years.”

“Then it’s a deal!” A row of even teeth, one full-wattage smile — and she was off toward the center of the lights like a fawn with good news.

Ji-yeon stayed where he was and watched her settle among the lamps and cameras. Watched her become, between one breath and the next, the stubborn, cross-dressing Goo Jae-hee.

Then he took his gaze back and walked toward the park exit.

“Sajangnim.” Manager Kim hurried after him. “Thank you — truly — for today. Sulli-ssi’s been carrying a lot lately. Since you came, she’s a different kid.”

“You’re welcome.” A pat on Kim’s shoulder. “Look after her out here. Anything comes up — you have my number. Call.”

“Ne! You can count on it!” Kim bowed him off with real gratitude. Money that arrives in crates, manners that arrive with it — in this industry, that was a patron worth holding on to.
