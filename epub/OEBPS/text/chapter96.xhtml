Chapter Ninety-Six

Kang Seul-gi Breaks Down

MID-AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the f(x) dorm — one warden, one prisoner

Meanwhile, across the neighborhood, the f(x) dorm was under soft arrest.

Sulli had melted into the living-room sofa like a cat with the bones removed, draped over the cushions and wrapped around the person nearest the crime.

“Soo-jung-ah… let’s go out for samgyeopsal… just a tiny little while… I’m starving… I’m actually going to starve to death, and my ghost will haunt this sofa…”

Krystal didn’t so much as lift her chin. Her eyes stayed welded to the TV screen, and her voice came out with no weather in it at all.

“No. Unnie said before she left: watch her. Consider her watched.”

This morning Vic-unnie had gone out on some errand, and before she’d gone she’d issued exactly one standing order — keep Sulli inside. No wandering.

Krystal understood the briefing perfectly. Unnie was worried a certain somebody would go wandering off to find a certain oppa. Again.

“Juuuust once! I promise, I’ll come straight back after we eat! Straight back!”

The plea stretched itself long and whiny, and Sulli got two fists around Krystal’s arm and started swinging it side to side like a metronome with a grudge.

Two days. Two entire days under Vic-unnie’s watch — no oppa, no visits, not so much as a text that wasn’t read aloud over her shoulder. And no idea what oppa was even doing with himself.

Today, at last, Vic-unnie had gone out — and Sulli had made her move. Just a quick slip out the door, find oppa, slip back. A flawless plan with no possible failure mode.

Except that she’d barely finished prettifying herself and reached the entryway before Krystal had appeared in her path, arms crossed, border declared closed.

Which left negotiation as the only remaining tool of statecraft.

Krystal, jostled to the end of her patience, finally tore her eyes off the television long enough to give her a long, withering once-over.

Helpless, truly. Sulli was the older one here — by a whole head start of months — and had never once in her life comported herself like an unnie. She was simply a large child who had refused, on principle, to finish growing up.

“Yah, Choi Jin-ri. Are you five? No means no.”

“But I really, really want to eat…”

Krystal’s mouth twisted sideways.

Dolled up like a pictorial, and it’s for “dinner.” Sure. And I’m the tooth fairy.

Sulli was winding up for the next round of siege warfare when a key slid into the front lock.

Click.

The soft metal turn of the deadbolt.

And the girl who one second ago had been a puddle with a grievance snapped upright as if the sofa had tased her — spine straight, knees together, hands folded primly on her lap, the full face of the world’s most obedient child deployed on demand.

Krystal observed the one-second costume change with something close to professional respect. If any variety show ever needed a transformation corner, here was its headliner.

The door opened, and Victoria came in with her bag over her shoulder.

One glance took in the entire crime scene: Sulli sitting at a level of posture that could only be described as forensic, and beside her Krystal wearing the exact face of a nature-documentary narrator. And here we observe the idiot, in her natural habitat.

Victoria fought the smile down, stepped out of her shoes, set the bag on the entryway cabinet, and ambled over without hurrying.

“My, my. All dressed up and nowhere to go, Sulli-ya?”

The teasing tone told Sulli everything she needed to know: the camouflage had failed inspection.

She abandoned the act on the spot — sprang off the sofa, crossed the room in two bounds, and latched onto Victoria’s arm with both hands.

“Vic-unnie, you’re hooome!”

“Any later and somebody here would’ve taken the roof off.”

Victoria reached out one finger and tapped her on the forehead. Not hard, not soft — calibrated.

Krystal, who had spent the day as sole prison staff, finally had backup, and the incident report came out in one breath.

“Unnie, finally. She nearly talked my ears off. The second you walked out she started up — ‘let’s go out to eat, let’s go out to eat’ — I couldn’t hold her down with both hands and a cushion.”

“And look at her. Does that look like a girl going out for food?”

Full makeup today, applied with intent. Shoulder-length hair tucked behind the ears. A white dress spotless enough to photograph for a catalog. Precision, top to bottom.

Sulli hung off Victoria’s arm, wheedling and litigating simultaneously.

“I have not! I’m just… a little hungry, that’s all! Soo-jung wants samgyeopsal too — she’s just being cold about it!”

Krystal rolled her eyes, declined to dignify the slander, and scooted to the far end of the sofa to surrender territory. Watching Sulli’s guilty, fidgety state, whatever small awkwardness she’d still been carrying over the whole Song Ji-yeon business evaporated on the spot, replaced by a pure and wholesome appetite for watching her suffer.

Then Victoria’s face reset. The amusement left it, and something formal took its place.

“Choi Jin-ri.”

Rare. She almost never reached for the real name — that voice was reserved for genuine anger, or for matters of genuine weight.

Sulli’s heart slammed straight into her throat. She peeked up, very small.

“Unnie…”

Krystal put her phone down too, curiosity dragging her eyes over. When Vic-unnie broke out the real name, somebody’s afternoon was about to have structural problems.

“You. Come with me.”

No pause for appeal. Victoria turned and walked toward her room without waiting for a single reaction.

Sulli’s stomach dropped through the floor. It’s over. It’s over. This is the reckoning. Unnie had cracked the case — the sneaking out, the oppa destination, the white dress — and now came the sentencing phase.

She shuffled toward Victoria’s room at glacial speed, looking back over her shoulder at Krystal every third step, eyes broadcasting SOS in urgent Morse code.

Krystal returned a look that said may the gods have mercy on your soul, folded her arms, and settled deep into the cushions. The show, as far as she was concerned, had sold out the theater.

A furious Vic-unnie was one of exactly two human beings on this earth whom Krystal Jung Soo-jung feared.

The first, naturally, was her own sister. The Ice Princess. A woman who could end an argument in one syllable and make you thank her for it.

Sulli, tears nowhere in evidence, steeled herself and followed her in.

The door clicked shut.

Victoria didn’t start in. She walked to the bed, sat down at the edge of it, and simply looked at her.

Sulli withered under the gaze. Hands and feet with nowhere to live, head bent, she began confessing to the carpet in a voice the size of a coin.

“Unnie… I was wrong…”

“Wrong about what?” The voice was level. No weather data available.

“I… I shouldn’t disobey you. And I shouldn’t have… been thinking about sneaking out…”

The volume shrank with every clause.

Victoria looked at her — this complete picture of abject, wringing-hands remorse — and nearly lost the war with her own face on the spot.

She held the line. Barely. Not yet. Today this child is going to learn something that sticks.

“Choi Jin-ri. A question.” She cleared her throat. “Have you known all along that Ji-yeon is American?”

Sulli’s head came up like a startled bird’s, eyes gone perfectly round.

“Ah? Unnie — how do you know?!”

Victoria was caught squarely between a laugh and a stroke. She gave up on interrogation as a format.

“How do I know. All right. Let me walk you through my morning. I marched myself to that man’s restaurant, walked up to his table, pointed at his face, and informed him that he was a fraud. A chaebol second-generation, toying with a young girl’s feelings, and that he should stay away from you, effective immediately.”

A breath.

“Now. Guess what he said to me.”

And then Victoria sat up straight, chin lifted, and gave the performance of her life.

“‘Tell me something, Victoria — is there any chance you’ve got the wrong man? I’m not a chaebol. I’m a gyopo. Born and raised in Los Angeles.’”

She even reproduced the accent — vowels worn round and easy by two decades of Southern California, delivered at the unhurried pace of a man ordering lunch in his own hometown — and she got it seven parts right out of ten. Which was seven parts too many. She even managed seventy percent of that faint, unbearable half-smile of his.

“Pfft—”

It escaped before Sulli could stop it. She burst out laughing on the spot.

She could see it — the entire scene in perfect resolution. Her unnie, armored and magnificent, charging out to break up the lovebirds… and discovering, mid-execution, that the chaebol wolf she’d come to slay was another import. A kid from Los Angeles. A hometown boy, if you squinted, from the far end of the same ocean.

The laugh lasted all of two seconds before the realization caught up with her.

Vic-unnie went to find oppa this morning!

They didn’t fight again, did they—

She sneaked a glance sideways, running diagnostics. Relief arrived in one warm tide: unnie was teasing her. Joking about it. Which meant unnie now knew — properly, formally knew — that oppa wasn’t a fraud. A woman still prosecuting would never have used the story as material.

The thought settled into place, and she started giggling all over again.

“And you’re laughing—” Victoria, half-scolding, half-laughing herself, flicked her square on the forehead. “Yah! You ungrateful little—! Do you have any idea how mortified I was? In my entire life I have never been that embarrassed! My face is still lost somewhere over the Pacific — you know, the one between here and home! It is never coming back!”

Sulli clutched her forehead and folded down into a crouch beside the bed, laughing so hard the words had to climb over each other to get out.

“Unnie, mianhae… I really didn’t do it on purpose… I just forgot to tell you…”

“Forgot.” One eyebrow climbed. “How does a person forget a thing like that?”

But was where oppa came from really such a big deal?

She genuinely didn’t see it.

“I… I just always thought — oppa’s good to me, so that’s what matters. Where he’s from isn’t important…”

“You—!”

Victoria pointed at her, and for a full second nothing whatsoever came out of her mouth.

Looking at that baffled, blameless face, the last of her anger packed up and left the province entirely. What remained was the usual residue: helplessness, and a fondness so enormous it embarrassed them both.

She shifted over and pulled Sulli down to sit beside her.

“All right. No more interrogations.” Her voice came down to earth, and she reached out and tidied a strand of Sulli’s hopelessly rumpled hair. “Unnie went to see him today. Everything got said. It’s all clear now.”

The giggling tapered off. Sulli looked up at her, careful as a cat stepping on ice.

“Then… is unnie still angry? Oppa really isn’t a fraud.”

“Unnie knows that now!”

One thing he’d said today had gone in deep and stayed there. She’s an adult. Her job is to touch the world with her own hands — read people on her own clock, own her choices, all of them. The exact wording had been blunter. A cage, he’d called what she was building. A greenhouse with no idea what weather is. But the shape of it was the same, and she had carried it home with her, and it had been sitting on her chest ever since.

Victoria let out a long breath.

“Sulli-ya. Unnie owes you an apology. I was too strung tight. I should never have moved to stop you before asking a single question — and I should never have doubted your judgment. That was unnie’s mistake. I’m sorry.”

The apology landed, and Sulli’s eyes went red on the spot.

“Unnie…” She shook her head, voice thickening with a sniffle. “I was wrong too. I shouldn’t have sulked at you just because you said a few things about oppa. I know it was for my sake.”

The elder sister’s hand found the younger one’s, and the rift that had lived between them since that evening quietly finished dissolving. Gone. Nothing left in its place but hands holding hands.

“Sulli-ya.”

Victoria spoke again, and this time there was weight in it.

Sulli’s head came up at once, those still-damp eyes watching her, braced.

“Unnie…”

Looking at that small, prepare-for-anything face, Victoria’s heart suffered a minor structural failure. She reached out and ruffled her hair, gently, right at the crown.

“Unnie’s thought it through. You and Ji-yeon… from now on, I don’t interfere.”

Sulli froze. Blinked. Replayed it, certain she’d misheard.

“Unnie… what did you just say?”

“I said — what happens between you and Ji-yeon from here on is yours. Unnie stays out of it.” She looked at her, and her voice had gone warmer than Sulli had heard it in years. “After talking with him today, I finally saw him clearly. He isn’t the man I’d drawn in my head. And you’re not a child anymore. Learning to read people, making your own calls, living with the consequences — those were always going to be yours to learn. Better now, and gently, than later, and hard.”

Bit by bit, Sulli’s eyes lit up — two stars catching, one after the other.

“Really, unnie?!”

“Really.” Victoria nodded, and then raised one finger, terminal in her mercy. “But — terms. If anything ever happens, anything, unnie hears it first. No hiding. No soldiering through alone. Agreed?”

“BEST UNNIE EVER—!!”

Sulli launched herself up off the bed and hung off Victoria’s neck like a wreath, wriggling with the whole-body joy of a puppy let off its leash.

“Unnie’s the greatest! I promise! I’ll listen to everything you say forever and never ever make you mad again!”

“Okay, okay — you’re strangling me—” Victoria patted her back, laughing, wheezing. “Dry your eyes. If Soo-jung walks in and sees you like this, you will not survive the teasing. She will have material for a decade.”

“Got it!”

Sulli let go, bounced for the door — and at the threshold spun around and threw her a double arm-heart, delivered at maximum wattage.

“Saranghae, unnie!!”

Victoria watched her bounce away down the hall, and her mouth curved, entirely against direct orders.

She took out her phone. Found the new name sitting in her contacts — Song Ji-yeon, SAVORY — and looked at it for a long moment. Sighed.

So be it.

Some things, you only learn the depth of by walking the road yourself.

✦ ✦ ✦

EARLY AFTERNOON Asan Medical Center, Seoul en route — two trainees, one driver

Meanwhile, across the city, Song Ji-yeon’s car was gliding toward Asan Medical Center.

The cabin had been quiet for a while. He checked the mirror — two girls in the back seat, bolt upright, as though the seats had been installed around them — and decided to break the silence himself.

“By the way — where do you two usually go? For checkups, I mean.”

They’d been in the car a good while now, and he still hadn’t asked which hospital they’d been headed for.

“Asan Medical Center.” Joo-hyun answered for the pair of them. “Most of the company’s artists and trainees use it. It’s close to the building, so it’s convenient.”

“Asan, huh.” He nodded. “Then Asan it is. I’ll come in with you, as it happens — I’ve never had the grand tour of a hospital. Could be educational.”

He’d been to Asan exactly once. The fainting spell — the collapse on a Yeouido sidewalk — and it had been Sulli who’d hauled him through these same doors.

Joo-hyun blinked. She recognized a manufactured excuse when one was handed to her gift-wrapped, and her hands came up at once.

“Ji-yeon-oppa, no — really, it’s fine. The entrance is plenty. We know the building, we can manage from the door—”

“See a guest halfway out and you haven’t seen them out at all. House rule.” He cut through, easy and immovable. “Two kids. One bad throat. One doing laps between counters — registration, meds, paperwork. Trouble stacked on trouble. I’ve got an empty afternoon, so I’ll spare you the laps.”

Joo-hyun’s mouth opened. Closed. Whatever she’d been about to say went back down, and what came out instead, very quietly, was:

“Then… thank you for the trouble, Ji-yeon-oppa.”

A few blocks on, Asan’s building slid into view — a plain, workmanlike slab, no gloss on it anywhere.

Among the general public, Asan wasn’t much of a name. Inside the industry, though, everyone knew it by heart: agency artists and trainees came here for everything from headaches to disasters, and the corridors reliably produced familiar faces.

Joo-hyun, clearly, had the route memorized. Registration. Elevator. Straight down to the ENT clinic — no hesitation, no wrong turns. The walk of a girl who had done this too many times this year.

A handful of people were already queued at the clinic door. The three of them found seats.

Seul-gi sat down beside Song Ji-yeon with her hands folded in her lap, fingers strangling the hem of her shirt, eyes welded to the floor tiles.

He glanced over and dropped his voice.

“Nervous?”

She pressed her lips together. The answer arrived at mosquito volume.

“Mm… I’m scared.”

“Scared of what?”

“…That it won’t get better.” Lower still, and now there was a tremor in it, fine as a hair. “If my throat stays like this forever… does that mean I can’t ever sing again? That I can’t… debut?”

He looked at the fear written all over that face, and didn’t answer right away.

What he could not do — could never do — was say the easy thing. Relax. It heals. You’ll debut one day, in a group the whole country learns by heart. Some knowledge doesn’t translate.

After a moment, evenly:

“It hasn’t come to that. Don’t sentence yourself before the doctor’s even spoken. Let’s hear him first.”

Seul-gi nodded, small, and said nothing more.

When their number was called, Joo-hyun went in with her. Song Ji-yeon held down the bench outside for ten minutes or so before the two of them came back out — and one look at their faces rearranged the gravity of the waiting room.

“How’d it go?” He stood.

Joo-hyun shook her head, voice pitched low.

“He wants a closer look before he says anything. A laryngoscopy — the picture from outside isn’t clear enough.”

“Then we go look.”

No debate. He walked the pair of them to the exam room himself.

The procedure itself was nothing much — but by the time the results printed out, an hour had gone by, and all three of them had worn grooves into the plastic chairs.

The doctor was a middle-aged man in thin gold frames. He read the report twice, and the lines between his brows moved in and took up residence.

“The nodules have advanced considerably since your last visit.”

He looked up at Seul-gi over the frames.

“And the position isn’t friendly. They’re sitting right along the edge of the vocal fold. My recommendation is surgery. We go in, and we take them out.”

The color left her face in a single tide.

“Sur… surgery?”

“Minor surgery, as these things go.” The glasses got a small, patient adjustment. “Recovery runs two to three weeks, followed by strict voice rest — and I do mean strict. Done well, the impact on your timbre should be small.”

“And— and the high notes?” Her voice had started to shake in earnest. “Afterward — will I still be able to sing the high notes?”

The doctor was quiet for a moment. He did not paper over it.

“That, I can’t promise. All surgery carries risk, and recovery varies from person to person. But—” the pause got heavier, “—if this goes on much longer, we won’t be discussing your high notes at all. We’ll be discussing whether the voice you have now survives.”

For several seconds, the exam room made no sound at all.

Seul-gi’s eyes flooded red. Her mouth opened, and nothing came out.

Her whole life, the dream had been one shape: a stage, a light, and a song that made the whole country remember her. And now a man in gold-rimmed glasses was telling her, in the mildest voice in the world, that the high notes might be finished — and that even the tone might move, by how much, no one could say.

She couldn’t gamble that. No singer alive gambles that.

“Seul-gi…”

Joo-hyun’s hand closed over hers, tight enough to hurt.

Seul-gi bit down on her lip. The tears stood in her eyes and refused, on principle, to fall.

And Song Ji-yeon — watching a girl shake like a struck bell and still hold herself upright by nothing but stubbornness — heard himself speak.

“Doctor. This surgery. What are the numbers — success rate?”

The doctor’s eyes came up.

“Family?”

“A friend.”

“We’ve done a great many of these at Asan. The procedure itself is mature.” He chose the words with visible care. “For patients who follow the recovery protocol to the letter, the timbre does change — but modestly, in the good cases. What the young lady needs to prepare herself for is the after. For a while, even speaking will have to be done carefully. Whispered, ideally. Singing is off the table for the recovery window entirely.”

But she still didn’t want the surgery. Anyone watching could see her fighting it.

“Doctor… isn’t there any other way? Anything at all?”

He read her face — and clearly, he had seen this exact face before, in this exact chair.

“If you won’t operate, then conservative treatment is what remains. Whether it resolves…” A small shrug, professionally regretful. “…is largely up to luck.”

“Conservative treatment. No surgery.”

It came out of her almost before he’d finished — fast, involuntary, reflexive as a flinch.

She had already done a month and more of conservative treatment. It was precisely because it wasn’t resolving that she was sitting in this chair at all. Deep down, she knew the odds. But she would not — could not — lie down on a table and hand someone a knife and her tone at the same time.

So the miracle would simply have to happen. Luck was the only door left open, and she meant to throw herself at it.

She didn’t even register how fast she’d said it.

Joo-hyun opened her mouth — two words into a plea, maybe three — then looked at her friend’s face, set like poured concrete, and swallowed every syllable.

She knew better than anyone alive what singing weighed for Kang Seul-gi. Asking her to gamble her tone under a scalpel wasn’t asking for courage. It was asking her to hold still for an execution.

The doctor sighed, and didn’t push. His pen moved down the chart, and prescriptions followed.

“Then — anti-inflammatories and painkillers to start. Rest the voice. Speak as little as possible. Nothing spicy. If it worsens further…” The pen stopped. “…surgery stops being an option and becomes the only one.”

He capped the pen.

“Come back for a follow-up. Any time at all.”

“Mm.” Barely a sound.

Song Ji-yeon collected the medication at the window, and the three of them came out of Asan Medical Center into the late afternoon.

✦ ✦ ✦

LATE AFTERNOON Seoul, in the car the back seat, window side

The sun had swung west. It lay soft and slantwise across the concrete at the hospital entrance — the light of a day that had decided to be gentle without consulting anyone.

Seul-gi carried the plastic bag of medicine in one hand. The other was a fist, knuckles gone white, and she hadn’t spoken a full sentence since the exam room.

Her head was down. Her steps were slow. She walked as if each one were landing on something tender.

Joo-hyun read her at a glance and reached for something comforting — but with him still standing there, the words didn’t fit the room. She let them go and did the only thing she could: drifted half a step closer, shoulder almost touching shoulder.

Song Ji-yeon had noticed too, naturally. From the exam room to the lobby to the curb — out of this girl, not one extra syllable had come.

He didn’t say anything either. He opened the rear door and handed the two of them in.

The car pulled away from Asan and folded into traffic, and the cabin went quiet in a different key — nothing left in it but the small patient breath of the air conditioner.

Seul-gi sat window-side in the back, eyes fixed on the buildings sweeping past in reverse, and said nothing at all.

Joo-hyun reached over and laid her hand on top of the white-knuckled fist. Didn’t speak. Just held on.

And that — that was the switch.

Seul-gi’s nose stung, all at once, and the tears came without permission — silent, spilling out of the corners of her eyes and running down her cheeks before she could organize any kind of defense. She turned her face into her other arm and buried it there, and her shoulders started to shake.

She was crying ugly, and she was crying silently — no sound at all, as though sound cost extra, as though she were afraid of being heard.

Joo-hyun lost her composure on the spot, both hands going to her friend’s face, dabbing uselessly, her own voice thin with panic.

“Seul-gi, don’t— don’t cry… it’s going to be okay. We’ll take care of it properly, we’ll be so careful, and it will heal—”

Seul-gi shook her head, lips bitten white, and the tears came harder.

She might never sing again.

Twelve years old when she’d entered the company, and every year since then spent in the service of one stage. She could still remember the beginning — the youngest in the practice room, the weakest at basics, knees blooming purple-black from dance drills, the ache so bad at midnight that she’d cried into her blanket with her face stuffed in the pillow, holding onto her roommate-unnie’s hand… and then gotten up the next morning, dried her face, and gone back to the barre.

Lunch hour, when everyone else headed down to the cafeteria, she’d stay behind in the corner of the practice room and drill her breathing — again, and again, and again — until her throat went dry and raw, then gulp water and start over.

All those years, she had never once told her family it was hard. Never once said she wanted to quit.

One day, she’d thought. One day I’ll stand on a stage, and Mom and Dad, and everyone who watched me grow up, will see me standing in that light.

Her throat was the lifeline. The root system of the entire dream.

And now it was the thing about to snap.

The more she thought, the worse it got, and the tears would not stop, and finally her voice fought its way up out of her throat — hoarse, cracked, shaking:

“Joo-hyun… I practiced for all those years… all those years I practiced, and I don’t want… I don’t want to give it up like this…”

Joo-hyun’s own nose burned. “You won’t have to. Seul-gi-ya, listen to me — it’s a small injury. The doctor said conservative treatment has real hope—”

“But what if—”

The words were coming apart in her hands.

“What if I can never sing the high notes again — never, for the rest of my life — and my voice changes, and it isn’t mine anymore? Then all of it… everything I drilled, all those years… what was any of it for…”

The car went silent for a moment.

In the mirror, Song Ji-yeon watched the girl’s shoulders shake, and something settled down on his own chest, slow and heavy.

He was not, by any measurement, a sentimental man. But all those years I practiced had landed on him like a hand.

Practiced for years. Those three words had a whole childhood inside them — scraped elbows, knees pushed past inflammation, shoe soles worn down to the mesh, and every night spent crying in a corner where no one could see.

A teenage girl who had staked her entire youth on a single road, for the sake of a single stage — and now the road itself was being pulled out from under her.

His fingers tightened on the wheel. He said nothing.

And a thought went through him like a stray current. Kim Ji-won. The Goddess Ability on her file — wasn’t there a card on that list? A Healing Card. One per week, collect in person…

He couldn’t remember it clearly. He’d skimmed that page back then — never gone back to it, never collected a single one. Just an impression, faint as a watermark.

…What if the card could actually heal a throat?

He didn’t know. And he refused — flatly refused — to start hoping out loud. That system of his ran on its own private weather; what it gave and what it took had never once been itemized in advance. If he told this girl there might be a cure, and then the card came up empty — a basket drawn from a well, a moon fished out of water — then what he’d have handed her wasn’t hope. It was one more thing to grieve.

Better to go home, dig through the Handbook himself, and find out what the thing actually did. If it could help — then help. If it couldn’t — then she would never have known, and would never have lost anything.

Decision made. None of it reached his face. He turned the wheel, calm as ever.

“Dorm first. And Seul-gi-ya — enough tears. The doctor said it himself: nursed properly, there’s real hope. The body first. The rest is in human hands.”

She fought the tears down, slowly, hiccup by hiccup, and managed a red-eyed “Mm.”

The car stopped in front of an unremarkable apartment building, and the two of them unbuckled.

At the open door, Seul-gi stood with her eyes still swollen, her voice scraped down to the boards.

“Ji-yeon-oppa. Today… today you saw me at my absolute worst. And you drove us there, and then all this way, and I… I’ve been nothing but trouble.”

Her head was down, fingers wringing the hem of her shirt again, like a confession working up the nerve to be made.

“And crying like that, right in front of you… I’m really sorry.”

He waved it off, and kept his voice light on purpose.

“None of the sorry. Everybody’s wiring shorts out sometimes — better a breakdown than a bottleneck. Especially you. With that throat, ‘keeping it in’ is the one thing you can’t afford.”

A beat.

“Go up, rest properly, take the meds on schedule. The voice is the priority — everything else is noise. Don’t spiral. It will get better.”

Her eyes brimmed again; she sniffed, hard, and nodded once, firmly.

“Mm… thank you, oppa.”

She hesitated.

“Oppa… about today. Could you not… tell my dad? Any of it. I don’t want them worrying.”

“Not a word to Director Kang.” He nodded. “You have my word. Breathe.”

“Thank you, Ji-yeon-oppa. Truly — for all of it today.”

Joo-hyun bowed as well, a clean, precise ninety degrees, and the two of them went in one after the other.

He smiled after them until the stairwell door closed, then pulled away and pointed the car at Cheongdam-dong, letting the city close back over the day.

The car moved. He took his eyes off the mirror and tapped the wheel twice, lightly, thinking.

That Healing Card. First thing when he got home — open the Handbook and read that page properly, all the way through.

…And where, exactly, was Kim Ji-won right now?

Aigoo. Another woman to go charm.
