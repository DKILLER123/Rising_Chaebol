Chapter One Hundred Eight

Yoon-ah, Tae-yeon, and the Uninvited Han So-hee

TUESDAY · ELEVEN O’CLOCK Cheongdam-dong, Seoul ONGI Cheongdam — the new flagship, red carpet out

The next day, noon-ish, found the new ONGI sitting exactly where a flagship should sit — in the marrow of Cheongdam-dong’s main street, a short stroll from the fashion boutiques and the Galleria’s glass frontage.

A red carpet ran from the doors to the kerb. Two ranks of towering flower baskets marched from the entrance clean to the corner, their ribbons spelling out congratulations in chrysanthemums — stage dressing, every basket of it, delivered on the operations department’s order.

When it came to a grand opening, the rule was simple: the grander, the better.

And people had come. A ring of them stood along the cordon — a few genuine customers drawn by the novelty of an opening, but mostly, if one were honest, invited restaurant-review bloggers and food-video creators, cameras already warm.

This was 2012. The genre barely had a name yet, but it existed — the first wave of eat-on-camera creators and café-and-queue reviewers, uploading to YouTube, and ONGI’s single most important promotional channel.

A NOTE ON THE CHANNEL · MUKBANG, 2012

The word hadn’t gone global yet. In 2012 it was just a scatter of kids in Seoul bedrooms and PC-bangs, filming themselves eat — a camera, a table of food, an honest verdict at the end.

But appetite is theater, and theater scales. The early restaurant-review bloggers already moved lines: one favorable video from a mid-sized creator could put a hundred warm bodies in a queue by the weekend.

A hotpot brand that treated the internet as a loudspeaker, in this year of all years, was not gambling. It was simply reading the room early.

A few tabloid-stringer reporters worked the edges of the crowd, lenses sweeping. The most distinguished byline among them would be the Seoul Shinmun — today’s local edition would carry the opening as a city-desk brief, four lines, no more.

A local brief. But a local brief on a page the whole country flipped past on the way to the sports section — which, for a food company not yet two years old, was nothing to sneeze at.

THE SEOUL SHINMUN · CITY DESK — TODAY’S LUNCH EDITION

SAVORY F&B Cuts Ribbon on Eighth ONGI Hotpot House, in Cheongdam-dong

Four lines, bottom of the city page. Every employee of SAVORY will nevertheless find a clipping of it by Friday.

Staff from SAVORY’s Logistics & Training division, all in crisp ONGI uniforms, wove through the crowd — a dozen-plus of them, escorting, directing, handing out hot tea. Everything ran on rails.

The division head, Lee Ji-hoon, spotted Song Ji-yeon hovering at the back of the crowd before Song Ji-yeon spotted anything at all, and carved a path through the bodies to collect him personally.

“Sajangnim — you finally made it!”

Song Ji-yeon fell into step beside him toward the storefront, glancing at his watch.

“When does the ceremony actually start?”

His father had scheduled the ribbon for eleven sharp, and Song Ji-yeon — who did not, as a rule, do early — had come a full thirty minutes ahead of it. And somehow still managed to feel late.

Lee Ji-hoon grinned.

“Any minute now, Sajangnim. We were only waiting on you.”

Song Ji-yeon gave up on the math and followed him to the front of the steps — which was, in truth, all the stage there was: the ribbon stand sat directly on the store’s entrance stairs.

Two hostesses in formal dress stood to the right of the doors, one of them cradling the ceremonial ribbon across her gloved arms.

Song Ji-yeon’s gaze snagged on the ribbon-bearer’s face and stayed a beat too long.

I’ve seen that face somewhere. Cool, clean-lined, the kind of face a casting director would clock across a stadium — and for the life of him, no name came.

He let it go. Somewhere in the last life, probably. Some magazine, some drama, some face in a crowd. The world was full of faces.

He took his mark. The ceremony began. Three ribbon-cutters in all — Song Ji-yeon front and center, Lee Ji-hoon at his shoulder, and the Cheongdam store manager completing the line, with the two hostesses flanking. For one storefront stoop, it was a respectable amount of humanity.

The gold scissors arrived on the ribbon-bearer’s white gloves. He accepted them with a nod of thanks.

While the branded ribbon — SAVORY · ONGI lettered down its length — was being drawn taut, his attention drifted, eyelids at half-mast, over the faces beyond the cordon. Stranger, stranger, stranger, stranger—

Stop.

Left-front corner. Two women, wrapped up to the eyebrows.

Both in low-key baseball caps. Both in oversized black masks that swallowed everything from nose to chin.

To Song Ji-yeon, the sight was less a disguise than a flare. He’d spent the past months marinating in female idols; this particular brand of conspicuous modesty practically glowed.

Other people might have walked past. He took one look at the familiar line of those shoulders, at the one thing no mask on earth could cover — the pair of wide, startled deer eyes above the black fabric — and the answer assembled itself.

A cap hides the hair. A mask hides the mouth. Nothing on this Peninsula hides a pair of deer eyes.

Lim Yoon-ah.

And the woman glued to her arm — small enough to pass for a high schooler — was either Kim Tae-yeon or Sunny. In Girls’ Generation, the short roster had exactly two names on it.

His money was on Tae-yeon. Sunny ran a little rounder than this; the figure clinging to Yoon-ah’s arm was nearly Yoon-ah-slight.

Song Ji-yeon’s inner abacus clattered through the arithmetic and closed with a shrug.

The emcee’s microphone boomed — the auspicious hour had arrived. Song Ji-yeon reeled his mind back in. The reunion could wait; business first.

One hand holding the ribbon, one snip of the gold scissors—

POP—POP—POP—

The confetti cannons went off in ragged salvo, colored paper snowing down over the red carpet, and the crowd broke into honest applause.

ONGI Cheongdam was officially open.

The bloggers and food-creators surged for the doors like a tide through a broken seawall, while the news crews — shots secured, angles bagged — began breaking down their tripods and drifting off toward the next assignment.

Which was only natural. An emerging F&B company opening store number eight was not national news; it was a symbol shot, a press-kit line. Anyone who wanted an exclusive later could book the boss for a short business interview through channels that were very much not Song Ji-yeon’s problem.

Song Ji-yeon dumped the follow-up schmoozing onto Lee Ji-hoon and the store manager, waited until the last long lens had cleared the perimeter, then came down the steps and made straight for the left-front corner.

The two masked women, ceremony concluded, were drifting toward the entrance themselves. He arrived head-on and stopped two steps short.

“Yoon-ah-ssi. If I’d known you were coming, this ribbon should have been yours to cut.”

A top-tier idol on the scissors — the traffic that would have generated didn’t bear thinking about.

A NOTE ON THE CUSTOM · THE LANDLORD’S COURTESY

In the Peninsula’s business world there operates an unwritten rule: whoever rents space to do business — new opening, ground-breaking renovation, anniversary event — calls the property owner beforehand, or sends a card.

It is the same instinct as delivering holiday gifts to one’s elders: a small tribute paid upward, to the person whose land the rice grows on. Nobody writes it down. Everybody obeys it.

This ONGI, as it happened, stood on ground owned by none other than Lim Yoon-ah — the same Cheongdam building whose storefront Old Chen had once surrendered on legacy terms his landlord had seen no reason to raise. The operations team, running true to custom, would have sent her side the courtesy notice along with everyone else’s.

And yet here she was, in the corner, in a disguise. Which meant the invitation had been politely declined, and the appearance was off the books entirely.

Song Ji-yeon understood the shape of it perfectly. The notice had been a formality. Her attendance would have been a fantasy — a top idol with a schedule that ate cities, printing money by the hour, does not spend a Tuesday morning on a tenant’s ribbon.

Except that she had. And that was the interesting part.

Lim Yoon-ah’s step hitched. Caught, she gave up the game without ceremony — one hand, one pull, and the black mask came down off her face, giving the daylight a clean, unadorned, entirely unfair pair of cheekbones.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. Putting me on a stage doesn’t come cheap.”

“Aren’t you this store’s landlord? I’d have thought I get the friend rate.”

He was only teasing. The ribbon was cut, the scissors racked; nobody was un-cutting anything. He simply needed a door into the conversation.

Lim Yoon-ah shook her head, once, pleasantly firm.

“No.”

He laughed, let it drop, and tipped his chin at the smaller shape still mummified beside her.

“And this would be?”

Yoon-ah followed his glance, then leaned gently into her companion’s arm, drawing her forward.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi — our leader, in Girls’ Generation. Kim Tae-yeon.”

Named, the smaller shape surrendered too. Off came the cap; down came the second mask — and out came a face so fair and finely made it looked Photoshopped by weather.

Bare-faced, the whole of it. Not a grain of makeup, and the skin underneath was lit from somewhere the sun didn’t know about.

She bowed, precise and small, and her voice came out clear with that one unmistakable wobble of softness underneath it.

“Annyeonghaseyo, Song Ji-yeon-ssi — hello. Tae-yeon, from Girls’ Generation. First time meeting you. Please take care of me.”

“Tae-yeon-ssi. A pleasure — I’ve admired the name for years.” He returned the bow, and meant the rest of it too. “Two of Girls’ Generation’s front line in one morning. My store is being spoiled.”

That was not protocol-fluff. In this era, on this Peninsula, Girls’ Generation was not a girl group; it was the ceiling — the standard against which the entire Asian idol industry measured its ambitions. And the group’s leader and acknowledged visual was, statistically speaking, a once-in-a-generation artifact.

Well — leader, visual, and Jessica. The three of them topped the member popularity charts, and he was enough of a former stan to know it to the decimal.

Tae-yeon, complimented, went faintly pink at the rims of her ears — but her mouth curved up on its own authority, and her eyes fixed on him, bright and round and shamelessly pleased with his taste.

Visual meant the prettiest face in the group. As far as Tae-yeon was concerned, the arithmetic checked out.

Song Ji-yeon waited a beat for a reply and got a happy silence instead, so he pivoted back to Yoon-ah.

“So how did you two end up here?”

Lim Yoon-ah smoothed her long hair back over one shoulder — the gesture of a woman resigning herself to telling the whole story — and told the whole story.

HIGH CUT · OFF-DUTY SNAP “THE IDOL DISGUISE”

Caught between boutique and banchan — the nation’s center, incognito, failing at it.

Cap — plain black, brim low, adjusted twice a block out of pure habit

Mask — the oversized black kind, tugged down under the chin the second the coast cleared

Top — a heather-grey long-sleeve tee, cuffs shoved to the elbows

Bottom — slim dark-wash jeans, chosen for a full morning of walking

Footwear — flat black ankle boots, broken in, blister-proof, zero mercy for fashion

Carry — one boutique shopping bag from the Cheongdam fashion street, handles already biting

Hair — long dark waves escaping the cap on both sides, smoothed back with one hand mid-story

The whole apparatus is defeated by one feature: the deer eyes — wide, luminous, blinking above the mask line like a signed confession

A tip for the aspiring incognito idol: the disguise conceals everything except the only part that matters.

“I’m on break this week, and I had nothing scheduled. Two days ago your SAVORY office called — the new store was opening, would I have time to attend the ribbon-cutting?”

She shrugged, and the shrug carried a week’s worth of resignation in it.

“I turned it down before the sentence even finished.”

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. You run a company. You know our SM rules better than most.”

“Every commercial appearance by an SM artist is controlled by the business office — tightly. If I’d stood on those steps today as your guest, the whole thing changes species. That’s a commercial booking.”

“If Lee Soo-man-seonsaengnim learned I’d guested at a grand opening for free, he would have my skin. Booked properly through the company, my appearance fee is — well, your operations people clearly didn’t budget for it. So: politely declined.”

Not one word of it was air.

A NOTE ON THE AGENCY · SM’S IRON GRIP

In the Peninsula’s entertainment industry, SM Entertainment is spoken of the way sailors speak of weather: the vampire, the iron hand. Its artists’ commercial resources are controlled to a degree that borders on the theological.

A commercial ribbon-cutting is the least of it. An artist posting so much as a brand logo on a personal feed routes through layer upon layer of internal review. No contract, no appearance — not a guideline, but the iron law of the entire ecosystem.

Song Ji-yeon, who had already paid tuition on this exact subject in the currency of one Victoria Song, nodded along with the weary fluency of a man who had done the reading.

“Understood. Then why risk the company radar and come anyway?”

Lim Yoon-ah gave the leader beside her a look of pure, abandoned suffering, and hooked her arm through Tae-yeon’s.

“Who’s left to blame? This certified homebody woke up transfigured.”

Kim Tae-yeon, thus indicted, lowered her head and scuffed the red carpet twice with the toe of her sneaker.

“Outside of schedules, Tae-yeon is basically grown in the dorm. If a door doesn’t open onto a stage, she doesn’t walk through it.”

“Today some wire crossed somewhere. She hauled me out of bed at dawn — dawn — demanding a shopping day. We did the entire Cheongdam fashion street, all morning. My legs have filed a complaint.”

“Then somewhere between store eleven and store twelve, tired and starving, my brain finally caught up to the calendar — and I remembered today was your opening.”

“ONGI’s all over the internet lately, so it can’t possibly taste bad. And it’s two streets away. So I dragged her over for a look.”

“The plan was: slip in quietly, eat one meal, vanish. Instead we walked directly into a full-scale ribbon-cutting — confetti, emcee, human soup. We couldn’t get near the door.”

“And then — wrapped up like this, standing in the most remote corner on the block — you still picked me out of the crowd.”

She finished the inventory of her grievances and let out a long, theatrical sigh.

Beside her, Tae-yeon nodded along earnestly and added her own testimony, barely above a whisper.

“Ne, Song Ji-yeon-ssi. We really were just passing by. We’re hungry.”

And there it was — the word hungry, hung directly on the fence. A host is honor-bound to act.

Inside, the bloggers and food-creators had colonized the main hall — flashes popping, tripods unfolding, a happy riot of ring lights and reaction faces. It was, plainly, no environment in which to seat two of the most photographed women on the Peninsula.

One stray blogger frame and tomorrow’s entertainment headline would write itself: Girls’ Generation, Fallen on Hard Times — Brawling Niche Influencers for a Lunch Table.

He turned sideways, smiling, and waved them toward the side of the building.

“You’re hungry, and you’re standing on my turf — you think I’m letting you leave hungry? The hall’s a circus today. Come on — I’ll take you up to a private room.”

Yoon-ah’s building ran to two floors; ONGI’s renovation had claimed the ground level for the dining hall and turned the whole of the upper floor into a dozen-odd private rooms.

The two idols traded one glance — and declined to decline. Everyone present was smart enough to know that a refusal now would cost more ceremony than the meal.

“Then we’ll happily accept the meal, Song Ji-yeon-ssi.”

Lim Yoon-ah smiled, pulled her mask back up, and towed Tae-yeon into his wake.

He led them in through the side entrance, skipping the main hall’s carnival of influencers entirely.

The Cheongdam branch had been fitted out with real care — a modern-minimal skeleton dressed in quietly luxurious new-Chinese accents: dark timber, brass inlay, papered light. The entire second floor was VIP rooms with soundproofing rated for celebrity grief, built precisely for diners whose privacy was worth more than the meal.

He chose the room with the best sightline and ushered them in personally. The air conditioning ran deep and cool; the room sat hushed and spotless.

He flagged the floor manager over, borrowed her tablet, and built their table himself — the signature broth, the seasonal vegetable spread, and a private instruction routed to the kitchen: easy on the salt, easy on the oil.

Idols. Physique management written into the marrow. Anything heavier and the meal would sit on their conscience till dawn.

Order placed, he did not linger. Three people, one acquaintance-and-a-half deep, did not yet justify his presence at their table.

“Food will be up shortly. Take your time — anything you need, press the service bell on the table.”

He made his exit with a smile and drew the door shut behind him.

Click.

SAME AFTERNOON ONGI Cheongdam — second floor The carpeted corridor, and a tray going the wrong direction

The second-floor corridor ran quiet under its thick carpet, and Song Ji-yeon’s mood ran correspondingly sunny.

A formal acquaintance with Kim Tae-yeon, and Lim Yoon-ah now comfortably at conversational distance — two more names in the address book of the era’s biggest girl group.

Now — could the Goddess Handbook bind the two of them? And if it ever did… what would the activation reward be? What ability?

He was mid-fantasy, walking, when a waitress in ONGI’s dark uniform came the other way — head down, tray balanced, making efficient time toward the kitchen.

The ribbon-bearer from the ceremony. She’d shed the formal dress for the standard uniform somewhere in the last hour.

He raised an arm and stopped her without breaking stride.

“One moment. Tell the kitchen to hurry Room 0012’s order — that table is important.”

At the Sajangnim’s voice she stopped, pivoted, and bowed by exact degrees.

“Yes, Sajangnim. I’ll tell the kitchen right away.”

The voice was cool, low, and perfectly respectful.

He acknowledged it half-absently — and his gaze, drifting across her face on its way to nowhere, snagged and stopped dead.

This face.

Features cut fine and three-dimensional, with something faintly stubborn and aloof living between the brows — a cold beauty, threaded through with an unnameable, already-broken loveliness underneath it.

His brow creased, and his memory turned itself over in a hurry.

And landed.

That was why the ribbon-bearer had itched at him. He’d filed her as some stranger from the last life, glimpsed once in a magazine margin — but this close, that particular cool was not forgettable. It was practically a signature.

His feet planted. His eyes stayed.

The girl, under the Sajangnim’s unmoving stare, privately wondered what she’d done wrong — but her training held. Head slightly lowered, hands folded, still as a photograph.

“You—” He heard his own hesitation and rode through it. “What’s your name?”

Her lashes flickered — once — at the question. Why the Sajangnim was suddenly interested in the name of a tray-carrier, she could not imagine; workplace rule number one was that wondering happened off the clock.

She stood square, hands folded at her front, and answered softly, clearly.

“Sajangnim — Lee So-hee.”

He nodded slowly, and let a breath all the way out.

The Peninsula’s future queen of headlines was, at this moment, carrying trays in a Cheongdam hotpot house.

Han So-hee — the stage name. Lee So-hee, the name on the employment forms.

The woman who, in the years to come, would carve herself a road through this industry armed with nothing but that cold, broken-doll beauty and a talent that arrived pre-loaded — the next generation’s controversy empress, tabloid catnip, the actress an entire comment section would claim to hate and then wallpaper their phones with.

Exactly as the later reporting had said. And exactly this unglamorous, for now: autumn of 2012, nineteen years old, invisible in a dark uniform on a thick quiet carpet.

The corner of Song Ji-yeon’s mouth climbed into a long, slow, meaningful curve.

He looked at the half-raw future star in front of him, and the abacus in his chest went to work, beads flying.

The artist roster at ShiningYouth Entertainment, it appeared, was about to gain one more name.
