Chapter One Hundred Seven

The CJ Squeeze

MONDAY MORNING Mapo-gu, Seoul SAVORY F&B — the chairman’s office, third floor

Song Ji-yeon stopped at his father’s office door, knocked, waited for the Come in, and pushed through.

He had come to do three things. First, deliver the news that his SAVORY career was over — ShiningYouth Entertainment was about to stand up and start walking, and splitting one man across two companies produces two half-men. Second, process Park Seon-young’s resignation and handover. Third, abduct the two front-desk receptionists, Lee Hye-jin and Jeong Soo-ah.

As for why Park Seon-young wasn’t here doing his own resigning — the man was simply too busy.

Park Seon-young was currently the entire operating system of ShiningYouth. He had run a batch of department-head interviews yesterday, spent the afternoon compiling results, and had the shortlist sitting in Song Ji-yeon’s inbox by evening. The efficiency bordered on the indecent.

Song Ji-yeon had gone through the files and picked out the people who would head legal, finance, human resources, and strategy. Today the man had to hire cleaners, re-plan two floors, and bully contractors — there was not a spare hour in him for a formal resignation.

So the person responsible for burying him under the workload had come to run the errand personally. The two receptionists were a rider attached to the bill.

He had not forgotten, either, that T-ara needed managers.

Inside the office, Song Min-seok sat bent over a financial report, pen in hand.

He looked up at the motion, found his chronically absent son in the doorway, took off his glasses, set them down, and leaned back into the big chairman’s chair. In the week and a half since he’d taken the SAVORY chair back, aside from the standing weekly meeting, he had laid eyes on the boy perhaps twice.

“To what do I owe the honor? I was starting to think you’d forgotten which wall the front door lives in.”

“Cold, Dad. Weren’t you the one who told me to go build an entertainment company?” He pulled out the chair opposite the desk and dropped into it with a sheepish grin.

“I told you to build one. As far as I can see, you’ve been sitting in an office, aging. In at ten, vaporized at six.”

In his father’s ledger, the past two weeks of his son’s life read: sat in office; nothing visible; left punctually.

Song Ji-yeon: ………

Strictly speaking, it was accurate. His total contribution to ShiningYouth had been one idea. Every physical object in it had been acquired by someone else.

The business registration: Park Seon-young. The T-ara negotiation: Kim Min-hyun on the phone and at the table. The office lease: Park Seon-young again, trotting.

His own contribution: capital.

And in this era, capital does not feel burdened. He was, at minimum, psychologically unburdened.

A chaebol in training does not do his own legwork. What, otherwise, would the legwork-having subordinates do all day?

“Dad, forget the office-sitting. The company is nearly on its feet.” He laid out the tour in working order: one girl group signed, the whole roster — T-ara; a four-story building leased in Cheongdam-dong; department-head interviews concluded this weekend; an overseas run through next month. “Which is why I won’t be clocking in on this side anymore. A hundred decisions a day want a signature, and I’m one person.”

Song Min-seok absorbed the report in silence. The quietly astonished look crossed his face and took its time leaving — the son he remembered had needed two reminders to take out recycling, and here he was with a company that had a spine.

He picked up the thermos cup, blew the floating tea leaves off the surface, took one unhurried sip. Only then, the verdict.

“A real company needs a full-time owner. Fine. The restaurant has its people; it runs itself.”

Song Ji-yeon climbed the pole as offered.

“However — I need to borrow some staff.”

“Which staff?”

“Park Seon-young, your planning director — the one I already parked in the representative-director chair at ShiningYouth. His resignation and transfer need your signature. And the two girls on the ground-floor front desk — I want them as my group’s managers. Sharp tongues, quick studies, and they know every entertainment scandal on the Peninsula by heart. Behind a reception desk, that’s trivia. Inside an agency, it’s infrastructure.”

The cup came down. His father regarded him deeply, two fingers tapping the desktop, twice.

For a company of SAVORY’s size, losing one department head and two receptionists was a haircut, not an amputation — staff would fill the gaps by the end of the quarter. And it was for his own son’s conquest of new territory. Left pocket, right pocket; same pair of pants.

“Take them. A new company needs people you know to the bone, not a headhunter’s word for it.”

Song Ji-yeon had two full seconds to celebrate.

Then his father’s face reset, and the old, deep-set eyes pinned him to the chair.

“But the holes you dig, you fill.”

“…What holes? A handover this minor — HR re-hires, or promotes a deputy. Wouldn’t that settle it?”

A snort, unimpressed.

“You say that like the calendar doesn’t exist. ONGI’s new store opens tomorrow, and I had assigned Planning — Park Seon-young — to represent the company at the ribbon-cutting. A department head’s rank holds a stage properly. You’ve now scooped him out, and whichever poor soul I substitute will be outranked by the occasion. The company looks careless.”

The verdict was handed down with the finality of a stamp.

“Since you’re the one press-ganging my staff — tomorrow’s ribbon is yours to cut, Mr. President. Go clean up your own mess.”

Song Ji-yeon’s eyebrows climbed.

ONGI’s new store?

His memory riffled the file and landed on the page: the Cheongdam location — wasn’t that the one in Lim Yoon-ah’s building? The storefront Old Chen had handed over at the legacy terms his landlord had kept for him.

He’d stopped following ONGI’s progress some time ago. The brand had entered its stable phase: grow slow, expand steady, nothing that needed his eyes. Apparently at some point the finish line had been crossed and the store was opening.

He still didn’t get the ceremony, though.

“Dad. Isn’t it just one restaurant opening? A ribbon, a stage, a podium — isn’t that theater? Hang a string of firecrackers and unlock the doors.”

His father actually laughed.

“You still think ONGI is some street stall with a tarp?”

He sat up, retrieved his phone, opened a page, and passed it across the desk.

“A season of operating does this. Our model, our standards — it’s earned a following with real teeth, and the word of mouth online is loud enough to hear from here.”

Song Ji-yeon took the phone. On the screen: a forum.

82cook — one of the country’s big general forums, home to a food board where the entire nation pooled its restaurant intelligence.

The page his father had open was the board itself. At a glance: three separate ONGI threads, each past ten thousand upvotes, each with a comment floor long enough to need its own elevator.

82COOK · FOOD BOARD — SEARCH: ONGI

1[review] finally tried the hotpot everyone won’t shut up about. the banchan alone is worth the queue. would absolutely stand in that line again

2the staff noticed my chopsticks had rolled off the napkin before i did. who raised these people???

3took my parents for their anniversary. dad cried over the braised short ribs. a full-grown man. crying. over ribs.

4is it just me or is the Apgujeong branch somehow even better?? how is that possible

And this was 2012. Not the later decade, where a video could harvest a million hollow likes by lunch. An upvote here was a warm human body — one that had walked in, queued, eaten, paid, and walked out smiling.

“And it isn’t just 82cook.” The phone came back across the desk. “KoreaPAS has ONGI threads. TIMEFORUM has ONGI threads. The brand has officially broken out of the foodie circle.”

Even so, Song Ji-yeon couldn’t stretch all the way to the ribbon.

“It’s popular, yes. But is a ceremony really necessary? Seven stores nationwide is a mid-sized chain. The press turnout will be thin, and the coverage won’t move the needle.”

His father smiled in the way of a man about to produce the second document. From the desk drawer he drew a report and handed it over, and with it a long breath that wasn’t quite a laugh.

“You’re undercounting the brand. And there’s something you don’t know. Last week, CJ Foodville’s president — Heo Min-hoe — contacted me. He wants to acquire ONGI. I refused. He came back offering a capital investment instead. I refused that too.”

“Oh? CJ has taste.”

CJ Foodville — the restaurant arm of the CJ Group, one of the two or three largest names in Korean dining. Being on their shopping list at all was a certificate of quality.

His father shook his head, and tapped the report now lying in Song Ji-yeon’s hands. The smile left entirely.

“Being on their list is no blessing.”

Drawn up at the Chairman’s instruction. The handwriting on the last line is steadier than the numbers deserve.

Song Ji-yeon paged through it, and felt the beginning of a headache.

“So CJ’s trying to starve us into selling? Can the stores even run normally?”

He’d had no idea last week had contained all of this.

“Your father has survived twenty-some years on this Peninsula. I keep a few relationships oiled.” A single nod. “Operations continue. But we’re down to domestic vegetables, the meat comes overland instead of by air, and the ledger feels it. Every month, twice.”

Against CJ, Song Ji-yeon had no lever. He was, at present, a minnow — and that was a whale that swallowed minnows by accident, without noticing.

But the old man had relationships solid enough to keep seven stores running to standard under blockade. No wonder he’d carved a company out of this soil in the first place. And no wonder the man can’t be rattled by anything I do.

He was curious about the shape of those relationships — and asked nothing. When his father wanted him to know, the information would arrive on its own schedule.

The question that actually mattered:

“What does any of that have to do with cutting a ribbon?”

“Everything.” His father’s voice leveled out. “CJ is already circling. If we show no movement, they’ll press closer. Tomorrow is our statement — SAVORY is expanding, outward and upscale, on schedule, while the siege runs. Let CJ watch us open a new front door with their hands around our throat.”

A company being strangled doesn’t open stores. A confident one does.

Song Ji-yeon felt a flicker of genuine startlement at the audacity.

“You’re going straight at the CJ Group?”

“Straight at them? No.” The hand waved it off. “CJ Foodville is one subsidiary of one group — a cloud, not a weather system. I’m not defeating Heo Min-hoe. I’m making the purchase expensive and the siege pointless. When the arithmetic stops adding up for him, he’ll discover he has other mornings to attend to.”

Which tracked. The CJ Group was a mountain; SAVORY was a pebble at its foot. Nobody spends ordnance on a pebble. Once squeezing stopped working, the hand would simply move on.

“Understood. A ribbon with strategic weight, then.” He stood. “If the opening carries that much, I’ll be there in person tomorrow to cut it. SAVORY will not lose face on my watch.”

“Mm. Knowing the weight is what matters. Go do your work. Take your people with you.”

“Thanks, Dad. I’ll leave you to your reports.”

He stood and exited cleanly.

✦ ✦ ✦

SAME MORNING SAVORY F&B — the ground floor The front desk, and two packed cardboard boxes

The conversation concluded, he took the stairs down to the lobby.

Park Seon-young was not in the building — he was over at ShiningYouth, cheerfully hauling the new company on his back.

The targets were Lee Hye-jin and Jeong Soo-ah, the two he had hand-picked as T-ara’s managers.

Eun-jung was his woman, and Park Ji-yeon and the others would in all likelihood end up the same. He had no intention of letting unfamiliar men hover in their waiting rooms for the next decade. Female managers, then.

At the front desk, the two girls stood pressed together, whispering at a frequency only bats and best friends could receive.

A message from the Sajangnim had reached them that morning: pack your things, get ready — I’m clearing it with the Chairman first.

“Do you think Sajangnim meant it? About taking us to the new company?”

“Obviously he meant it. When has Sajangnim ever said a thing he didn’t do? He said we’d manage T-ara. So we’re managing T-ara.”

They had needed the past few days to fully digest the destination: ShiningYouth Entertainment. Dedicated managers for T-ara.

A regular front-desk girl, handed the manager’s seat for a top girl group — their hearts were still hammering like festival drums, and part of each of them kept refusing to sign off on the news, as if believing it would make it evaporate.

So many idols, so close. Could they — would it even be possible to — get Girls’ Generation autographs?!

The sound of leather soles on marble arrived, and the two snapped upright, bowed at a crisp, identical angle, and lifted shining faces.

“Sajangnim — good morning!”

He nodded, walked to the counter, and rapped his knuckles twice on the polished marble, keeping it light.

“It’s cleared with the Chairman. As of today, you two have nothing further to do with SAVORY. Pack your desks — you report to the new company.”

“Yay — long live Sajangnim!”

The younger of the two, Jeong Soo-ah, could barely contain the sparkle.

“Managers for T-ara… we’ll be seeing Eun-jung-unnie and the others every single day?!”

She loved Girls’ Generation first, it was true. But she was plenty fond of T-ara too.

“Don’t worry, Sajangnim — the handover files have been ready since yesterday!”

Lee Hye-jin nodded in rapid agreement and lifted a cardboard box that had been packed with suspicious care — the top layer was entirely snacks — looking ready to run a relay.

“Say the word and we’re gone!”

Song Ji-yeon laughed out loud, infected against his will.

So this was the fangirl in her native habitat? The 2010s model conceded nothing to her future descendants.

“Right, then — no dawdling. Car’s out front. I’ll deliver you to the new company myself.” He turned toward the doors and crooked two fingers over his shoulder.

He was due at ShiningYouth today anyway; the route ran directly through them.

The two girls traded one glance — excitement stacked on excitement until it threatened the ceiling — and hurried after him, boxes in their arms, snacks and all.

As of today, they were no longer SAVORY’s front desk.

They were T-ara’s managers.
