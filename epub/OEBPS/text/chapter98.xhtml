Chapter Ninety-Eight

The Peach Kissed First

NIGHT Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Unit 101 — dinner, the late innings

Outside, the night had finished settling over Cheongdam-dong. Inside, over the wreckage of the dinner, the temperature kept climbing.

The awkwardness of the first ten minutes had burned off entirely. They had known each other three years, after all — music-broadcast waiting rooms, year-end stage hallways, variety tapings — and both groups were second-generation, debut dates barely two months apart. Two girls who had walked parallel corridors since their teens turned out to have a bottomless backlog of shared material.

So they talked, and talked, and the feast went down to its bones. Three faces around the table wore the same specific smile: the overfed-and-unrepentant one.

“Oppa. Your cooking is actually criminal.” Park Ji-yeon set her chopsticks down, patted her belly with full ceremony, and awarded him both thumbs, no hesitation. “If you ever put this menu into one of your restaurants, there’ll be a line around the block by noon. Every single day. I am not exaggerating.”

Sulli nodded along at pecking-chick speed, peach-blossom eyes bent into full crescents.

“Right! Right! Oppa’s cooking beats even Vic-unnie’s! And Vic-unnie is already amazing — I honestly didn’t think anyone could beat Vic-unnie!”

And there was no flattery in it anywhere. That was the girl’s actual, private ranking.

The praise landed somewhere warm and ridiculous in his chest. Outwardly he only managed the modest smile, dabbing at his mouth with a napkin.

“The two of you are laying it on thick. It’s home cooking — the simplest stuff I know. I’m just glad it suits you.”

“Like it? I love it!”

The declaration was still airborne when she was already up, stacking bowls and plates with the brisk efficiency of a woman protecting an investment.

She hadn’t forgotten the arrangement for one second. The ledger between her and oppa was etched somewhere permanent: she freeloaded, she washed. And a girl who intended to keep freeload­ing — indefinitely, ideally forever — did very well to keep her side of it clean.

Sulli saw her rise and hurried up after her.

“Ji-yeon, let me help—”

It was, after all, her first visit. The host had cooked. The other guest was on dish duty. Sitting idle through all of it felt somewhere between rude and unlawful.

Before Park Ji-yeon could answer, a hand landed lightly on Sulli’s shoulder and sat her back down.

“You. Stay.”

He winked at her.

“Let Ji-yeon wash. Washing up is the only job she has in this house — take it away from her, and she’ll be too embarrassed to come freeload next week.”

The line was really for Sulli’s ears. First visit — there was no version of tonight where she lifted a finger. The joke was just the wrapping it came in.

She heard the consideration underneath it. Her cheeks went faintly warm, and she sat back down — embarrassed and pleased in equal parts, and sweet all the way through.

Park Ji-yeon, meanwhile, was already ferrying a tower of dishes toward the kitchen island, lodging a formal complaint as she went.

“Yah, oppa! So in this house I’m just the dishwasher?! Wow. Wow. Wounded. I’m wounded.”

The grin all over her face kept filing contrary testimony.

The contract between them was old and airtight: she ate, she washed — a fair trade, notarized by nothing and honored by everything. Oppa refused her money; labor it was, then. She’d pay it gladly, every time, for food like this.

He shook his head at her retreating back and turned to Sulli.

“Ignore her. Come on — living room. Feet up, something to watch.”

“Mm.”

She followed him to the sofa and folded herself onto it, small and polite.

It was her first time in his home. Having oppa there should have been the whole world — but with Park Ji-yeon’s sudden arrival, her sentences had gone short and rare, her posture a size too careful. The evening she’d been quietly dreaming about all day, two people and one table, had turned into a party of three, and somewhere under her ribs a thin thread of loss pulled taut.

He caught it. Not in words — there were no words — but in the slight stiffness, the hands retracted into lap-space. Worried she’d go quiet on him, he reached for the remote and woke the television.

PAST NINE Unit 101, Daewoo Royal County the living room — SBS, Thursday night

The screen bloomed — SBS, right as a title theme he half-recognized came up, and a title card settled over it: To the Beautiful You.

It was Thursday. Of course it was Thursday — the drama’s night.

“Huh — isn’t this your drama, Sulli?”

Genuine surprise. He hadn’t aimed the remote anywhere; the channel had simply opened onto her.

“Ah— y— yes—” Her cheeks lit on the instant, and her voice developed a stutter somewhere between the first word and the second. “Today… is the finale.”

Her entire person had stopped being okay.

If she’d known, she would never have told oppa she acted in this thing. And of all nights — it had to be the Thursday it ended. Sitting through her own performance with oppa three cushions away wasn’t viewing. It was public execution.

Her heart broke into a sprint without permission. A film of sweat surfaced on her palms.

It wasn’t as if she’d never watched her own work before. Just yesterday she had dragged Krystal and Vic-unnie onto the dorm sofa for a private retrospective of her own acting — and enjoyed it, even.

But tonight was oppa’s living room, and there was no governing it. She didn’t understand it herself. Nothing was wrong, everything was fine, and she wanted the floor to open up anyway.

He, for his part, saw no problem at all. He settled in with the easy appetite of a paying audience — a friend’s work was on, and he wanted to see it do well.

Sulli lasted about ninety seconds before the squirming set in. She stole a sidelong glance — his face calm, attentive — and her stomach dropped another floor. She began, quietly, to pray for Park Ji-yeon to finish the dishes and come break whatever this was.

The kitchen, however, offered only rushing water and the occasional ceramic clatter. The little dragon remained deep in combat with the greasy plates.

On screen, the pacing was tightening. Tonight’s stretch had arrived at a two-hander between Goo Jae-hee and the male lead, Kang Tae-joon—

SBS · TO THE BEAUTIFUL YOU — SERIES FINALE · LIVE

He has discovered her secret fear of heights, and is enjoying the discovery far too much — towing her up the gymnasium’s overhead walkway to “enjoy the view.” Jae-hee hangs off his sleeve with both hands, knuckles pale, letting go of absolutely nothing.

He watches her — furious, terrified, declining to admit either — and the laugh gets out of him. Then his hand comes up, smoothing her wind-blown bangs back into place, and the voice that follows carries a gentleness he hasn’t yet noticed in himself:

“Pabo. Afraid of heights, and still acting tough.”

— score: strings, rising —

Sulli went into the sofa cushion face-first — buried to the ears, one eye operational through the seam, auditing the screen in installments. The ears themselves had reached a red that was approaching medical.

Too much. Far, far too much. Her face was putting out heat in wavelengths; her cheeks could have fried an egg. How did I not notice how cheesy this was when we were shooting it?

He watched her complete her transformation into an ostrich and found it — there was no getting around it — genuinely adorable.

He tipped his head sideways, closed the distance to her ear, and dropped his voice to something that belonged to the two of them only.

“Good acting. The feelings really landed.”

Not loud. It didn’t need to be. It went through her like current — one clean jolt, neck to fingertips, and her whole body locked.

She surfaced from the cushion in one motion and came up directly into a pair of smiling eyes, far too close, with no plan for that whatsoever. The tomato comparison was, at this point, simply the accurate one.

“Oppa… quit teasing me…” A small protest, filed in a whisper, carrying one thin thread of pout she would deny under oath.

“I’m not teasing. I mean it.” The face he put on for this was the serious one. “As an actor, you did well. The emotion comes through, the eye line with your partner holds — that’s professional work. Genuinely.”

Her craft was real. Child actress, years of sets before f(x) ever existed — a résumé most idols couldn’t fake. And he was a demanding audience; whatever else was true, nothing she’d played tonight had made him want to look away. He would know. He had sat through her entire filmography in another life, some of it twice.

And the praise did what the hiding could not: the shame loosened, and most of it drained off on the spot. She sat back upright — still pink, but vertical, facing the television like a professional again.

Which was precisely when footsteps arrived from the kitchen.

Park Ji-yeon came out bearing a plate of fruit cut into immaculate geometry, still humming some drama OST, badly.

“Oppa, fruit’s rea— …whoa.”

The sentence never landed. Her eyes had snagged on the television — on Kang Tae-joon smoothing Goo Jae-hee’s wind-blown bangs, on the two of them smiling at each other, one teasing, one flustered, with enough pink froth coming off the screen to foam over the coffee table.

Her eyes went round. Two full seconds of total stillness — and then, blooming across her face, the single most brilliant, least trustworthy smile of the year.

The fruit plate touched down on the table without a sound. A body wedged itself onto the sofa beside Sulli. An elbow established contact. Eyebrows entered freelance negotiations.

“Oho~ oho~ our Sulli’s got some acting chops~ those shy little glances~”

The freshly cooled cheeks re-ignited on impact.

Sulli now wanted exactly two things in this world: a crack in the floor, or a zipper for Park Ji-yeon’s mouth. In the absence of both, she seized the other sofa cushion and jammed it at her, straight for the face.

“Ji-yeon!”

Running her mouth — in front of oppa?! What “shy glances”?! It was called acting! ACTING! Did she not understand the word?!

Park Ji-yeon swayed out of range laughing, caught her wrist on the follow-through, and pressed the advantage.

“What’s there to be shy about! It’s the job! Actors act! Come on — how many NGs did that scene take? And the male lead — that handsome in person too, yes or no?”

“YAH! PARK JI-YEON!”

Full name deployed. Dignity formally disbanded. The living room collapsed into open riot — laughter, shrieks, one cushion held hostage, terms of surrender under active negotiation.

Song Ji-yeon presided over the riot with the expression of a man who owns the zoo: helpless, indulgent, a smile with no plans to leave.

He picked up an apple slice and delivered it, peacekeeping-style, to Sulli’s mouth — the practiced gesture of a man calming a cat whose fur has fully risen.

“All right, all right. Ceasefire. Try Ji-yeon’s fruit — it’s sweet.”

The interception worked. Her mouth opened on pure reflex; the apple went in; the juice, clean and bright, detonated somewhere pleasant. The static in her chest ratcheted down two notches. She still glowered at Park Ji-yeon over the bite — cheeks stubbornly, structurally pink.

Park Ji-yeon, having achieved total victory, took a pear slice for herself and spoke around it.

“Fine, fine, I’m done. But seriously, Sulli — your acting’s good.”

The register dropped all the way down to honest.

“That bit just now — startled, mortified, and secretly a little pleased about it. All three at once, layered clean. That’s not nothing. You’re better than me.”

Flattery she could have handled. Sincerity ambushed her. Sulli waved both hands in front of her face like she was erasing a whiteboard.

“No — no, Ji-yeon, yours is the good one! Mine’s… mine isn’t even close—”

Wrong move. Park Ji-yeon’s head began shaking at freight-train speed.

“Aigoo, don’t put me in that conversation. My acting is a disaster area. I can’t watch my own dramas — the second I come on, I want to change the channel. I’m being completely serious.”

At which point Song Ji-yeon lost it — one sharp pfft escaping before he could get a lid on it.

Because a certain scene had surfaced. A famous one.

Park Ji-yeon’s head came around at once, eyes narrowed with suspicion.

“What’s funny, oppa? I’m telling the truth! There is zero modesty happening here!”

He waved a hand, wrestling the grin back down to size.

“Not you. Not laughing at you — something just came back to me, that’s all.”

He looked at her.

“You did a drama in 2010, didn’t you — God of Study, the KBS one?”

“Yeah. Why — you watched it?”

“A little.”

Understatement, technically. He had done his archaeology — in his last life, somewhere inside a 3 a.m. YouTube spiral, he had excavated the little dragon’s complete comedy archive, and it had been one of the finest nights of that entire incarnation.

“There’s a scene where your character gets chased down and worked over by a whole pack of delinquents. That scared, wronged little face you played — painfully real. I always wondered how you held that in, acting pitiful in front of them. That’s some fine acting too, for the record.”

Context, for anyone keeping score: Park Ji-yeon was T-ara’s licensed team tyrant — the maknae famous for terrorizing her own unnies under full diplomatic immunity. Producing a bullied, pitiful little face out of that was its own minor miracle of craft.

And the girls doing the chasing, as he happened to know, had been those very unnies. In cameos. Holding a scared-to-crying expression while her own seniors swung on her with stage-prop fury — who could say how much resolve that had taken.

Park Ji-yeon went blank for a moment — then placed which scene he meant — visibly cycled through embarrassment — and then the laugh broke containment anyway.

“Oppa, you have NO idea—”

The floodgates opened. Full grievance mode, arms conducting the orchestra of her own suffering.

“The unnies corpsed! Every single one of them, every single take — I racked up NG after NG after NG! They finally had LEGAL COVER to rough me up, you think they weren’t thrilled?! They could not stop laughing — take after take after take — and in the end the director just gave up. Shot the whole thing in long shot. Didn’t dare let a single unnie’s face into the frame. NOT ONE.”

By the finish she was laughing harder than she was complaining — the memory had that effect. Being “group-beaten” by her own unnies: swallowing the laughter, playing scared-to-crying, while her seniors swung on her with prop fury and actual, undisguised joy. The taste of that had been worse than getting hit. Considerably worse.

Song Ji-yeon was laughing too by now — quietly, but beyond recall.

Of course the long shot hadn’t helped. Of course it hadn’t.

As for how an outsider knew the inside of a set anecdote like that — archaeology again. The clip had existed, in his last life. It had circulated. It had ended him at the time.

Even through potato-grade footage, the “delinquents” mid-snarl had mouths that curved unmistakably upward. Bo-ram in particular had been lit up like a festival — quite possibly because heaven had at last granted her a sanctioned opportunity to discipline the little dragon, and she had thrown her whole heart into it.

None of which, naturally, left his mouth. He simply watched her conduct her own victim testimony, eyes bent with a smile that refused all orders to stand down.

Sulli had never seen the drama — but between the sound effects and the stage directions, she could see the whole set anyway: the delinquents corpsing in formation, the director despairing in his chair. She laughed until she had to hold her middle.

Whatever tension the living room had left, that story took with it.

On screen, right on cue, Seol Han-na appeared — the second female lead, trading lines with Kang Tae-joon under the gym lights. Kim Ji-won’s face, framed in his living room.

And something occurred to him.

“By the way — Sulli. You and Kim Ji-won-ssi still in touch these days?”

Casual as a man noticing the weather. But the question had a second floor. Kang Seul-gi’s throat. And the Healing Card that might be sitting on Kim Ji-won’s file in the Goddess Handbook… Not urgent — nothing about it was urgent. But the person herself was on his television, and questions this cheap didn’t come around often.

“Ji-won-unnie?” Sulli shook her head, honest as a ledger. “Haven’t actually seen her in ages. After wrap, everyone scattered into their own schedules — we trade a few lines on KakaoTalk once in a blue moon, that’s all. I heard she’s about to shoot some idol’s debut-single music video, so her schedule’s slammed. Past that — I don’t know.”

“I see.”

A nod. Nothing after it.

Reaching Kim Ji-won, then, would take its own opening, whenever that arrived. He’d only asked because the screen had handed him her face — not to mine Sulli for intel. The Healing Card was a long-game question, and long-game questions got long-game answers.

The drama ran on toward its close. Every detour survived, every misunderstanding unwound — and at the end of it, the two leads finally said the thing out loud.

Sulli let out an exhale long enough to qualify as an athletic event. It felt like finishing a marathon.

Sitting through her own finale — in full, start to credits — with oppa and Ji-yeon-unnie as the panel of judges: once in a lifetime was already too often.

Park Ji-yeon, for her part, had watched the whole ending with the appetite of a Sunday viewer, and applauded when it closed.

“Good. Good! Happy ending — everybody wins, nobody cries. My favorite kind of finale.”

Night deepened. On the wall, the clock’s hand slipped past ten without ceremony.

Park Ji-yeon produced a yawn of structural significance and stretched until something in her shoulders clicked.

“Aigoo, it’s late. I’m going up — I was up at dawn today, and this body is filing for recovery sleep.”

Sulli rose with her.

“Mm — I should get home too.”

“Taking a taxi?”

“Mm.”

Song Ji-yeon was already on his feet, keys off the sofa arm, voice set to the register of settled matters.

“No. This late? A girl alone in a taxi — no. I’ll drive you.”

“Ah? No — no, oppa, that’s way too much trouble—”

“It’s no trouble. I could stand the air anyway — walk off some of this dinner.”

Her dorm and his building were both in Cheongdam — a round trip that barely cleared thirty minutes. The voice stayed gentle, and stayed absolutely immovable.

“It’s no distance at all. Come on.”

Driving the peach home was long since routine — an established service, no notice required. And there had never once been a night he minded it.

Up against that tone, resistance had no material to work with. Something warm rose in her instead, wordless, and simply stayed.

Park Ji-yeon watched the two of them, eyes doing slow laps between the pair. A lone idol hailing a taxi at midnight was — by any sane measure — a bad plan. She piled on.

“Sulli, take the ride. Late-night taxis are a no. Listen to oppa.”

Outvoted twice over, Sulli surrendered.

“…Then it’s a favor, oppa. Thank you.”

“And I’m heading up! Night, oppa! Night, Sulli! Next time — all three of us again!”

Off-key humming, light footsteps, the door — and the little dragon was gone.

PAST TEN Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the passenger seat, window side

Down in the garage: two people, one car, doors shutting in stereo. The engine turned over, and the car rose out of the ramp and folded itself into the river of nighttime traffic.

The cabin was quiet — just the radio keeping a low, slow hand on things. Outside, neon smeared past the glass and laid its moving colors across Sulli’s face, and a profile that was already fine to begin with went softer under them.

She sat properly — hands folded in her lap, knees together — and every little while, a glance would escape toward the driver’s seat. Stolen, then quickly returned.

He looked different like this. Different from kitchen-mode, different from sofa-mode. The lines of the profile came out clean and hard in the low light — a grown man’s face — and everything about the way his hands sat on the wheel read as handled. It was a steadiness you could lean on. Her heart appeared to have opinions about this, and had begun — thump, thump — filing them against her ribs.

The f(x) dorm arrived sooner than either of them had budgeted for. He brought the car to the curb, level and easy, and killed the engine.

“We’re here.”

“Mm.”

The seatbelt came off. The door did not open.

She turned toward him instead. In the dim, her eyes had gone a little misted — and the reluctance sitting in them had given up on disguising itself.

“Oppa… today, I — I was really, really happy.”

The two-person evening had been hijacked by a dragon, sure. It hadn’t mattered, not for one hour of it. Being at oppa’s home, eating oppa’s food — the peach had been full since the front door.

“Me too.”

He smiled, and felt it — the girl’s gravity, steady, pulling gently at his side of the car.

She bit her lip lightly, gathering herself, and finally got the courage assembled.

“Then… I’m going up. Drive safe going back, oppa, okay?”

“Okay.”

The door opened; she stepped out — and then went nowhere at all.

The night wind moved past, cool with the first suggestion of autumn. She stood just outside the car, back to the amber glow of the window, and her heart was going again — fast, disordered, entirely off-script.

And then, as if something braver had borrowed her body for one second, she spun on her heel and bent back into the car.

He had “What is it—” maybe a third of the way assembled—

—when something soft and cool landed on his lips, unannounced, ahead of every thought he owned.

Light as a feather touching down. Sweet as a first stolen sip of honey — gone before it could be held, and warm for a very long time after.

Crimson, instantaneous, total — ears included.

And the moment it was done, whatever courage had possessed her let go of her all at once. Her own boldness startled even her. She shot upright, dropped “Oppa, good— good night” like something too hot to hold, gathered her skirt in one fist, and bolted — no backward glance, straight into the stairwell.

Her shape was gone in seconds.

Tap. Tap-tap-tap.

…footsteps going up and up, crisp and bright against a sleeping street.

He sat in the dark car, several seconds behind the present tense.

His hand came up on its own. Fingertips pressed, lightly, to his lips — where a warmth was still sitting. Or its afterimage. The two had become difficult to separate.

It took a while. And then, slowly, without any authorization from him, the corners of his mouth bent upward.

This little peach and her ambush raids. Last time, and now this time. At least this one came without teeth. The previous model had not.

He started the engine — and didn’t immediately turn for home. The window slid down a few centimeters instead, and the cool of the night came in across his face and started carrying the cabin’s heat away with it. He leaned back into the seat, at rest somewhere between defeated and delighted.

Because the facts were being stubborn about it. That very morning — not even twelve hours ago — he had sat across from Victoria, laid his word on the table, and closed the case: him and the little peach, strictly, categorically, entirely innocent. And now the same day was ending with her kiss still warm on his mouth.

How, precisely, was a man supposed to report that?

The peach kissed first?

Rehearsed silently, to an audience of zero, it still came out sounding like the opening statement of a certified dirtbag.

…Forget it. Nothing about tonight was fixable. He’d cross that bridge when the peach marched him to it — one step, then the next, then the one after that.

Song Ji-yeon put the car in gear and drove home.
