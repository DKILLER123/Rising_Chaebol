Chapter Nine

So You Should Call Me Noona~

SEPTEMBER 2012 Apgujeong-dong, Seoul ONGI HOTPOT · MAIN HALL, PRIVATE ROOM 3

Park Ji-yeon arched up out of the booth like a cat casing a birdfeeder, sneaking a look at the man coming through the door. Rugged build, sharp angles, tall — a quick eyeball said 185 and change — in a navy shirt with the sleeves rolled and dark tailored trousers. Her eyes lit up like a switch had been flipped.

“THAT’S him?” Jeon Bo-ram kept her voice under the restaurant’s hum, disbelief leaking through anyway.

Girl. I knew you were eating well. I did not know you were eating this well.

“It has to be him!” Park Ji-yeon’s eyes had gone frighteningly bright. “Who comes to a hotpot restaurant alone at dinnertime? He’s meeting somebody!”

“Enough.” Bo-ram caught her by the sleeve. “Ji-yeon-ah. One more look and this officially crosses into stalker territory.”

They were still busy theorizing when the man stopped in front of a door at the end of the corridor, knocked twice, and let himself in.

In the last half-second before the door swung shut, a laughing voice slipped out through the gap: “Traffic was brutal. They’ve had you waiting long?”

The door clicked closed.

Park Ji-yeon and Jeon Bo-ram sank low in their booth, twin cats mid-burglary.

Ji-yeon was still working out how, exactly, one bugs a private room when a server in an apron came over with a tray — and found two grown women crouched in a booth whispering like conspirators.

“Excuse me — is there anything I can help you two with?”

Ji-yeon shot upright, dusted invisible lint off her knees, and deployed a smile at full wattage. “No, no — we’re fine! Just waiting for friends!”

The server gave them a long, unconvinced once-over, then carried her tray off toward the private rooms.

✦ ✦ ✦

“Annyeonghaseyo~” The door of Room 3 opened on Park Min-suk, tablet in hand. One man and one woman inside: the woman wore a mask and a cap, unidentifiable. The man, she knew very, very well. She bowed at once, quick and respectful. “Sajangnim, welcome back. What can I bring you tonight?”

This wasn’t Song Ji-yeon’s first time in his own ONGI. He’d spent two days here before the opening, drilling the staff — kitchen and floor both — until ONGI’s way of doing things lived in their reflexes, so that on opening night nobody would be drowning.

Min-sook held out the tablet menu. Ji-yeon tipped his chin toward the woman across from him.

Eun-jung waved both hands, flustered. “First time here — how would I know what’s good? You’re the boss. You order.” A beat, and then, as an afterthought: “Oh — one thing. Stinky tofu. I don’t eat it.”

…We don’t serve stinky tofu.

His lips twitched. So Eun-jung had already blacklisted stinky tofu at this point in the timeline. He handed the tablet straight back to Min-sook.

“Then one of every signature broth — the gochujang, the samgyetang, the tomato, and the beef-tallow mala. Every meat dish on the menu. Add a tofu platter, the Chinese lettuce, the baby napa, the potato slices — and bring the dipping sauces with them. The classic trio.”

ONGI was, at heart, the extreme-hospitality hotpot playbook he’d studied half his life — Haidilao’s model, page for page — with the menu rebuilt from the ground up for Korean palates.

Min-sook tapped at the tablet, nodding along with a smile. “Ne, Sajangnim. Everything will be out shortly.”

She withdrew, politeness oozing from every step.

And then the room was just the two of them.

However bright their chat logs had been burning, this was the first time they had ever actually sat down at the same table to eat.

It was, unavoidably, a little awkward.

Eun-jung caved first.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi — is this place of yours a big operation? I counted a dozen servers out there, easy.”

She lived right upstairs in this very building, and yet tonight was her first time setting foot inside ONGI. She’d assumed it was some little neighborhood shop — two, three hundred square meters at the very most. It was not. It was a shade over five hundred square meters of polished, high-end restaurant.

He shook his head. “Not that big. Seven trial locations in Seoul, that’s all. But ONGI is a service-first hotpot house — the entire concept is hospitality refined down to the last millimeter — so we carry more floor staff than a normal hotpot place would—”

A knock cut him off.

Min-sook wheeled in the cart, a server behind her bearing the four broths in their divided pot.

“Your broths and first courses. Please enjoy.”

The door closed, and quiet settled back in. The four broths came up to a murmur over low flame — red, white, green, and gold, all four breathing steam — and the beef-tallow mala above all the rest, its aroma frankly tyrannical, claiming the entire private room inside a minute.

Eun-jung finally pulled off the mask and cap. A bare, scrubbed-clean face underneath. She drew one deep breath through her nose, and her eyes lit up. “It smells amazing.”

“Then eat.” Ji-yeon picked up the serving chopsticks, swished a slice of fatty beef through the tomato broth, and set it down in her sesame dip. “This one’s not spicy. Start here.”

She didn’t stand on ceremony — lifted it with her chopsticks, blew on it twice, popped it in. Her eyes bent into crescent moons. “It’s good!”

He smiled and started loading the other three pots. He’d always worked clean and fast — anyone watching him blanch meat could tell he’d grown up on a restaurant line. Timing exact to the second, every slice coming out silky and tender, never once overcooked.

A few bites in, Eun-jung set her chopsticks down, propped her chin in one hand, and studied him — something distinctly evaluative in her gaze.

“What?” He felt it land and glanced up.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi.” She stretched the name out, teasing baked into every syllable. “Honestly now. Are you secretly a chaebol heir?”

His chopsticks paused mid-air; then he laughed. “Where’s this coming from?”

“You answer first~.” She batted her lashes, the very portrait of softness.

“No.” He shook his head, and it wasn’t a joke anymore. “My family’s comfortable, at best. Small money — chaebol is a different universe entirely. You of all people should know what a real chaebol heir looks like.”

That much, at least, was true. Eun-jung had spent enough years in this industry to collect her share of chaebol sons — the posture, the entourages, the air they carried like a birthright. Nothing about the man across the pot matched the file.

Still, she wasn’t done. Head tilted, she let her gaze make a slow lap of his face and finally came to rest on his eyes — those disconcertingly, unreasonably clear eyes.

“But a Sajangnim at your age — what is that, if not a chaebol heir?” She pointed one finger at him. “You look… twenty-five? Twenty-five-ish?”

He smiled and didn’t answer right away.

“Too high?” Her brow arched. “Twenty-four?”

He shook his head, deposited a cooked shrimp ball into her bowl, and said it like a weather report. “I’m twenty-two this year.”

“Chincha?” Her chopsticks very nearly went straight into the pot. Her eyes went perfectly round. “Twenty-two?”

“Mm.”

“Daebak…” She said it faintly, one hand rising to her forehead as if she’d been shot. “I’ve been using formal speech with a twenty-two-year-old this whole time?”

He was rather at a loss. Nobody said anything about a kindergartner. “Eun-jung-ssi, I’m not a child.”

“Yah — how are you not a child?” She’d found her angle now and she straightened up, mischief catching light in her eyes. “I’m two whole years older than you. Did you know that?”

Of course he knew. Her profile lived in his head rent-free — class of ’88, two years older than him exactly. He’d known it before he’d known her favorite color.

But he never got the sentence out, because she was already leaning in closer, syllable by deliberate syllable: “So you should call me… noo~na~.”

She bit down on both syllables like they were candy, then sat back and watched him, smiling, absolutely waiting for a reaction.

Ji-yeon set his chopsticks down and looked at her.

The room’s lamp lay warm gold across her face, fine as the laugh-lines at the corners of her eyes. She was smiling like a cat that had already gotten away with it — not one gram of the careful politeness she’d walked in with still attached to her.

“Eun-jung-ssi.” Unhurried, the corner of his mouth lifting. “I’m not from here. Back in the States, nobody does the whole age-hierarchy thing.”

“Eh?” Her head tipped sideways, amazed. “You’re not Korean?”

“Korean-American. Born and raised in L.A.” The boggled look on her face pulled a laugh right out of him.

“I don’t care, I’m still older—” She recovered with her dignity mostly intact, cheek propped in her right hand, head cocked, watching him expectantly, voice gone all soft. “So you have to call me noona~”

He gave it a moment of genuine thought. Then he picked up the teapot, topped her cup to the brim, set it down, and met her expectant eyes — three parts put-upon, seven parts indulgent.

“Fine,” he said. “Eun-jung… noona.”

She blanked completely. It had been a joke — she’d never actually expected him to say it — and the tips of her ears went quietly, traitorously pink.

“Ahem.” She cleared her throat, lifted the tea, and took one measured sip to paper over whatever that had just been. “That’s better.”

He filed every microsecond of it away, lowered his head, and smiled. The 2012 model of Eun-jung — blushes at factory settings.
