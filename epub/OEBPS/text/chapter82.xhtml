Chapter Eighty-Two

Showing Me This First Thing in the Morning? Little Dragon, You’re Up to No Good

SUNDAY · LATE MORNING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment, first floor

Song Ji-yeon shuffled out of the bathroom, face dabbed dry with a towel, teeth freshly brushed. He checked the phone on his bed — still nothing from Goo Hara — killed the screen, and wandered out toward the kitchen.

“Lunch, then. It’s past ten anyway.”

He swung the refrigerator open, already half-planning the menu. The refrigerator answered with a gust of cold, empty air. A few bottles of mineral water. One plastic-wrapped loaf of bread flirting with its expiration date.

“Right. Completely forgot I haven’t restocked in over a week.”

So much for cooking lunch before duo-queuing with Park Ji-yeon. He shut the door and detoured into the walk-in closet.

It was a large closet with a small population: a few white T-shirts, black slacks, jackets, two suits. He pulled on a tee and a pair of casual black pants, then dropped onto the fitting bench and remembered something.

Going out anyway. Might as well burn the Good Luck Card. See if money literally falls out of the sky.

He opened the Goddess Handbook and used it. The card simply… stopped existing. No animation. No golden shimmer. Gone.

“That’s it? Seriously? Would a little sparkle have killed you? A chime? Anything?”

Honestly, of every reward the system had ever shoved at him, the Good Luck Card was the most useless object in existence. Not even a flavor text. You used it, and it evaporated, taking its mystery with it.

“Fine. Let’s see what good fortune finds me today. Don’t disappoint me.”

He grabbed the car keys and went out.

Waiting by the elevator, he called Park Ji-yeon. It rang for a long while.

“Ugh~ah… oppa, why are you calling this early…”

The voice that answered was pure dragon cub — thick with snot-toned sleep, words melting into each other, not one syllable fully conscious.

Ji-yeon’s eyebrows knitted on instinct.

Of course. Still in bed.

“What time did you even sleep last night? And what do you mean, this early? It’s ten-thirty. The sun is actively tanning your backside.”

“Mmm…” A rustle — she’d rolled over. The voice came out flattened into the pillow, padded with a shameless little whine. “Doesn’t matter~ No schedules these days anyway. I’ll sleep as long as I want…”

Speechless. Genuinely speechless.

He seriously wondered whether this little dragon could survive a single week without her unnies running her life. Up all night gaming, three meals of whatever, junk food as a food group, a sleep schedule that had surrendered entirely.

Forget idols — a normal human being couldn’t sustain this lifestyle. Some day she’d just keel over mid-rank in her apartment and nobody would find her for a week.

“Weren’t we supposed to duo today? Up. Wash your face. I’m buying you lunch, and we game after.”

A long pause. Then more dragon noises, half protest, half yawn.

“Mm… okay, okay. Oppa, wait for me — getting up right now!”

“Mm. I’ll be in the underground parking lot.”

He hung up and stepped into the elevator.

Downstairs, he found his car, leaned back in the driver’s seat, and scrolled his phone at a lazy, half-hearted pace.

Ten minutes passed.

Twenty minutes. Why is there still no dragon.

The gap between “right now” and “now” kept widening at an alarming rate. No footsteps. No buzz of the lobby door. Nothing.

She didn’t… fall back asleep, did she?

He gave up waiting and rode the elevator up to the second floor.

He’d already called ahead; he couldn’t be bothered with the doorbell. He reached for the keypad and punched in the code.

Click. The lock gave. He pushed the door open and walked straight in —

— and stopped dead, two seconds later, in the middle of her living room.

Park Ji-yeon stood in front of the sofa wearing exactly one item of clothing, and that item was underwear. Everything else — all of it — was just… out in the open air.

The sofa back and armrests were buried under an entire wardrobe in progress: skirts, jeans, hoodies, coats, a pile with no organizing principle whatsoever. She held a black hoodie against her chest, tilting it one way, then the other, muttering to herself about what matched what — deep in the vital strategic work of choosing an outfit.

The noise at the door made her turn.

Their eyes met.

Two full seconds of silence.

Then—

“YAH!!!! Oppa, why are you UP here??!!”

Her face detonated scarlet, cheek to ear. She screamed, snatched whatever fabric her hands found, clamped it against her chest, and stumbled backward in a blind panic — legs hitting the sofa, body following, landing in a heap of cushions with the hoodie hugged to her like a shield.

Ji-yeon spun around so fast his own shoes squeaked, presenting the door with a spectacular view of his back.

“You said ‘right now’! It’s been half an hour! I thought you’d conked out again, so I came up to check!”

“Th-then you still can’t just walk IN!! I’m not DRESSED!!!” Her voice cracked with mortification, trembling somewhere between fury and tears.

“Who stands around the living room with nothing on?!”

She had no comeback for that. Abandoning the argument entirely, she swept the entire mountain of clothes off the sofa into her arms and bolted for the bedroom like a startled rabbit — thump-thump-thump — and the door slammed behind her with a bang that rattled the hallway.

Only when the lock clicked did Ji-yeon slowly turn back around.

He looked at the firmly shut bedroom door. He rubbed his nose.

Well. How to put this.

That glimpse had been, perhaps, a touch more… vivid than the morning called for.

Showing me this first thing in the morning — little dragon, what exactly were you planning.

Look — in his head, Park Ji-yeon had always filed under pocket-sized gremlin. Thin arms, thin legs, permanently curled into a gaming chair, one hundred percent delinquent internet-addict energy.

Turns out, underneath all that, the little dragon was… distinctly not nothing.

She wasn’t tall, but her proportions were frankly unreasonable. A waist you could circle with your hands, legs pale and long and straight as porcelain columns, not an ounce of anything extra anywhere. And above that — nothing exaggerated, but perfectly balanced, exactly the right size and shape, pitched precisely where the mathematics of it all worked.

Measured against the idols of his previous life — bodies maintained under schedules of near-criminal discipline — this ranked comfortably in the top tier.

He leaned against the sofa, the two-second replay looping unbidden behind his eyes, and the corner of his mouth curved entirely without permission.

…Mm. The little dragon does, in fact, have some things.

He shook his head hard, evicting the entire line of thought.

A good while later, the bedroom door finally cracked open.

Park Ji-yeon emerged dressed: solid black hoodie, black skinny jeans, hair pulled into a simple ponytail — fresh, crisp, energetic. A completely different species from the creature he’d walked in on.

He privately filed his preference. The courtroom did not need to know.

Her face, however, was still glowing like a street lamp, and her eyes were doing desperate laps around the room, landing anywhere that wasn’t him.

Ji-yeon, meanwhile, had recomposed into perfect serenity. It wasn’t as if he’d never seen a woman. He’d seen Eun-jung — at this point his immune system had documentation on file.

He glanced at her flustered, guilty expression and couldn’t resist.

“Done? We’re going out for food, not a pageant. What’s with the full styling session?”

“What does oppa know!” She finally scraped together a crumb of dignity and pursed her lips at him. “We’re idols. Of course we go out polished! What if someone recognizes me looking like a homeless zombie — those entertainment dailies would have a field day!”

He couldn’t argue. The Peninsula’s paparazzi and tabloids operated with the same playbook as Hong Kong’s scandal sheets: the more outrageous the headline, the higher the sales, and no headline was too ugly to print.

Park Ji-yeon spotted bare-faced and disheveled entering an apartment. T-ara member’s late-night debauchery. — this stuff would be on every newsstand by dawn.

“Fine, fine. Let’s go.” He stood and headed for the entryway. “It’s eleven. Eat fast, game fast. That rig was bought for the both of us and you are not monopolizing it.”

“I know~!”

She followed him to the door, dug a mask and cap out of the drawer, and suited up with the focus of a bomb-disposal tech — cap tugged down, adjusted in the mirror, checked from the left, checked from the right, and only when not one strand of identifying hair remained visible did she exhale.

Watching her, something in his chest went quietly soft.

Being an idol really did mean surrendering the freedom to simply exist in public. And T-ara especially — still radioactive from the bullying scandal, public opinion one solid wall of hostility. If Park Ji-yeon got recognized today, it wouldn’t be a few snide comments waiting for her. It would be eggs.

“Let’s go.” He said nothing about any of it — just reached over and tugged her cap a centimeter lower.

SUNDAY · NOON A Japanese restaurant, Cheongdam-dong the counter seats in the back

Down in the garage, he opened the passenger door for her, walked around, started the engine, and pulled out into traffic.

“What do you feel like?”

She melted into the seat beside him, boneless. “Anything. Oppa picks. I’m not picky — it just has to be good.”

“Then… Japanese.”

The little place in Cheongdam-dong he always came back to. The one he’d taken Bae Joo-hyun to, actually.

…Huh. Still owe Joo-hyun-ssi a meal, come to think of it. Who knows when our schedules will cross again.

Park Ji-yeon nodded agreeably. Food was food; good food was good food.

Twenty minutes later they walked in and took the table deepest from the door — her on the inside, him on the outside, a human screen between her and the rest of the room.

The seating put them shoulder to shoulder. The contact clearly reminded her of something, because her ears began their slow climb back toward red.

He felt her stiffen. Nothing to be done about it, though — a family-run place like this didn’t have private rooms. They’d just have to sit close.

A waiter brought menus. Ji-yeon ordered from memory — grilled eel over rice, a plate of teriyaki chicken skewers — and handed the menu over. Park Ji-yeon studied it with tremendous gravity and added tonkotsu ramen and a shrimp tempura platter.

“Oppa. Is this place actually good?”

“It’s the best Japanese food I’ve had in the country. Palates differ, though — judge for yourself.”

“Okay!”

Steam started rolling out of the kitchen not long after.

Ji-yeon went first: a slab of golden grilled eel, glaze lacquered thick, flesh tender clean through, the char exactly where it should be. He nodded to himself.

Across the table — well, beside him — Park Ji-yeon inhaled a mouthful of noodles the size of a small garden hose and got to work, slurp-slurp, with total commitment. Broth gone, noodles gone, soft-boiled egg popped into her mouth whole — and her eyes flew wide open.

“Mm!! This is SO good!!!” she declared around a mouthful, already spearing a tempura shrimp, dipping it, dispatching it, face pure contentment. “This too! Oppa, excellent choice!”

Watching her eyes crinkle shut with happiness, he cracked up.

One bowl of ramen and the morning’s incident has been fully laundered from the dragon’s memory. Food really is the way.

They demolished the meal. Park Ji-yeon in particular formed a solemn, lasting bond with that tonkotsu ramen — came within a hair of drinking the bowl itself, and spent the rest of lunch issuing promises: “We’re coming back.” “I have memorized this restaurant’s face.”

SUNDAY · EARLY AFTERNOON A large supermarket, Cheongdam-dong fresh produce aisle vs. the snack kingdom

“Grocery run first.” He turned the wheel toward his usual big-box mart. “My kitchen has achieved a singularity of nothing. Restock time.”

“Ah — oppa’s place is out of food too?”

“Bottled water and existential dread. Yours is worse, I assume.”

“Um.” Guilty eyes, guilty voice, aimed at the floor. “There… might not be anything there either… I’ve been ordering delivery…”

“I’m confident a mouse could starve in your kitchen and file a complaint.”

(ꐦÒ‸Ó) Why expose me like this, though!!

The Sunday crowd was busy but not packed. The moment they passed the doors, the two of them split like raid parties.

Ji-yeon took a cart straight into fresh protein country: beef, pork belly, chicken breast, salmon — into the cart one by one — then a lap through produce for whatever greens were in season, weighed, bagged, dropped in.

He was, by lifelong habit, a cook-at-home person. Balanced plates, real ingredients, an actual list. His cart looked like a nutritionist’s textbook.

Park Ji-yeon, meanwhile, shot into the snack aisle like a torpedo.

By the time she drifted back, hugging her haul in both arms, he was already waiting near the registers — and watched her approach pushing a shopping cart stacked into a small, colorful mountain. Chips, chocolate, candy, cookies, spicy snack strips, six species of puffed corn product, sodas in every color the 1990s had ever invented.

His eye twitched, taking it in.

That’s not grocery shopping. That’s a hostile takeover of the snack aisle.

“Oppa, look!” She pushed the treasure trove toward him, presenting it, smiling so hard her eyes had closed. “I picked out so, so many good things!”

He looked at the cart. He looked at her. Two seconds of silence while he fought himself — and lost.

“Park Ji-yeon. You are never quitting junk food. Not in this life.”

She just pouted — immune to judgment — and glanced into his cart instead: beef, vegetables, fish, clean ingredients squared away in neat rows. Her eyes went round.

“Oh, right! Oppa, you can cook. I forgot that was a thing you could do.”

“Obviously I can. Why else would I be buying all this — to display it?”

Her eyes lit up like a slot machine hitting jackpot.

Context: she had recently moved into the unit upstairs, and her one ongoing crisis was food. In the old neighborhood she’d had delivery on speed dial — a personal directory, phone numbers memorized, wants fulfilled. The new area delivered none of her favorites. She didn’t know which restaurants here were good, had no numbers, and the delivery apps offered an infinity of fried chicken and hamburgers, and only that.

So it had been fried chicken, cola, fried chicken, cola, repeat — until she was on the verge of clucking.

And all along, one floor down, there had been a man who could cook.

Ding.

A scheme hatched itself in real time.

She plastered on her sweetest, most suspicious smile, sidled up, and attached herself to his arm, voice going soft and syrupy.

“Oppa~~~ can we talk about something?”

His soul left his body slightly. Whenever the little dragon deployed this exact face, history showed, someone was about to be exploited. Last time it was this face that got him drafted as a pack mule through the entire electronics market.

“No.” No eye contact. Instant rejection.

She rocked back on her heels but rallied instantly, clinging harder.

“Oppa~~~ don’t say no before you’ve even heard it! Hear me out first! Pleeease?”

“Not interested.” He pushed the cart forward, attempting to outrun her.

“It’s a GOOD thing! I promise!” She stuck to him like taffy, eyes spinning with calculation. “Okay so — oppa, you can cook, right? So let’s do meals together! From now on, whatever you want to cook — I’ll pay for the groceries! Oppa just cooks, and I’ll do the dishes!”

He stopped. That… was genuinely not what he’d braced for.

He’d braced for conscription. Manual labor. Something with his car and her furniture. Instead it was just: feed me.

He looked her over. A girl living on delivery and snack carts, on a path straight toward malnutrition with extra steps. If a meal partnership nudged her onto actual food for a while — where was the harm?

“Fine. Come over whenever. And keep your money — I’ll cook, I’ll call you when it’s ready. Just know I’m not home for lunch on weekdays, and I don’t necessarily cook every dinner either.”

Park Ji-yeon froze completely.

She’d been angling for a little extra shelter, a little extra oppa-coverage — a small clever trick, nothing more.

He’d said yes. And declined her money.

Her nose went inexplicably hot, and a warm current welled up through her chest.

“CHINCHA?!!” She launched herself at him, arms locked around his waist, face buried in his chest, rubbing once, voice spilling over with un-hideable joy. “I KNEW oppa was the best!!!”

The volume turned heads for three aisles in every direction. Caught mid-impact, Ji-yeon rocked back half a step, equal parts resigned and amused, and patted the back of her head.

“Okay, okay — people are watching. Composure. You’re an idol.”

She snapped back to reality, flushed to the ears, released him, and pretended to fix her hair with tremendous sophistication.

The smile, however, could not be detained. It leaked out of her no matter what she did with her face.

Heh. Heh heh. Free oppa meals, forever.

The ghost of her warmth still on his ribs, Ji-yeon shook his head and let it go.

Big and careless as she acted — the little dragon was another one running on empty, love-wise.

Well. Trainees raised from childhood probably all were, in one way or another. Sulli. Probably this one too.

Explains a few things about her last life, too.

Checkout done — he with bags of real food, she with both arms wrapped around one giant sack of contraband — they swayed back to the car like two different breeds of refugees.

SUNDAY · MID-AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong Park Ji-yeon’s study, two gaming chairs

Groceries squared away in his fridge, he called upstairs: “Food’s put away. Come on, let’s go up.”

“YESSS!”

They rode up together, and he walked into Park Ji-yeon’s apartment like a man on a schedule — because he was. The study.

Two brand-new, high-spec gaming rigs sat side by side, one black, one white — the spoils of yesterday’s death march through the electronics market, hand-picked, haggled over, and hauled personally.

Ji-yeon pulled out the black chair, sat, hit the power button. The machine hummed awake and slid smoothly to the desktop.

He rolled his wrists, cracked his neck, and glanced at Park Ji-yeon bouncing into the white chair beside him.

“What’d you install?”

“League of Legends, MapleStory, KartRider, CS:GO…”

He nodded. All the 2012 crowd-pleasers — long-lived games he’d dabbled in, back in his previous life.

Naturally, League was where he was strongest. That day at the office had purely been the office’s garbage internet. Unshackled, his Yasuo was a public safety hazard.

If destiny hadn’t assigned me chaebol ambitions, who knows whose name would be on the title of best in the world.

Right… Faker?

Faker: Yes. Yes, absolutely. For sure.

“Come on, then — League.” He smiled sideways at her. “Let me show you what an actual climb looks like.”

“Hmph! Big talk, oppa — don’t come crying to me at 0-and-10!” She gripped her mouse with both hands like a battle axe.

“You just keep your support alive and stop feeding the lane, and I’ll carry you to the moon.”

Is every man like this the second a game loads??
