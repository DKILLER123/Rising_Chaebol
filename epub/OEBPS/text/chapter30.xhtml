Chapter Thirty

Buying T-ara

SAME NIGHT Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the living room — seconds after the bombshell

The air found its mute button.

Eun-jung’s pale little face filled up with doubt, and she stared at him dead-on, no angle taken. The watermelon fork hung suspended somewhere over the plate.

“Song Ji-yeon… exactly how much did you drink?”

Her hand came up, hovering, hesitant, aiming for his forehead.

He blocked it gently aside, finished the watermelon in his mouth, and smiled. “Look at my eyes. Do I look like a man having a drunken episode? I’m completely sober, and I meant every word.”

“And you want to buy us.”

She couldn’t make his angle make sense. Anyone with one working eye could see that T-ara was a sunset in progress — buy them now, and there was nothing on the books to gain.

“Right now, on the back of your bad press, CCM’s valuation is sitting at a low the company has never seen before.”

“Kim Kwang-soo came up inside the old industry. The man’s entire crisis playbook is ‘bury it’ and ‘test it with sub-units’ — museum pieces. He has no idea what to do with attention except wait for it to die.”

“To him, you’re a line item about to be written off. To me, you’re a gold mine with the seal still on it.”

Nothing he’d told her was arranged for her comfort. Next to Korea, the Chinese market was the mine nobody had properly struck — and he happened to be walking in carrying the survey from his first life. T-ara had gone into that market once already and won. He knew the road by heart.

Nor was he planning to buy CCM whole. What he wanted came in one package: the six members’ exclusive management contracts, the group’s name and trademark, the sound-source rights to every album they’d ever released, and the MV library.

All of it, priced honestly, came to three billion won at the outside. If he weren’t in a hurry, he could sit on his hands until next year and probably walk T-ara out the door for two.

But waiting was the one thing he no longer wanted to do. Not after tonight — not after hearing, in her own tired voice, what the next year under Kim Kwang-soo’s plan would do to them.

“Buy… our contracts?” She bit softly at her lower lip. She knew, to the digit, what those contracts were worth this quarter.

To a Kim Kwang-soo desperate to get the weight off his books, any fair price to cart the whole tray of hot potatoes away — the old fox would wake up in the middle of the night just to check the offer was still real.

But even at the bottom of the well, that was still billions of won. For a twenty-two-year-old who had opened seven hotpot restaurants in Korea roughly a minute ago, it was a number that didn’t fit inside a normal head.

“Song Ji-yeon, stop joking.” She shrank a little deeper into the sofa, voice muffled by the cushion. “Your seven restaurants are brand new — everything costs money right now. And… we’re not worth that kind of gamble. We just aren’t.”

She didn’t want to be a weight tied to anyone’s ankle.

And she absolutely did not want the one man who’d handed her warmth inside this dark stretch to go broke proving a point.

“Think I’m talking nonsense?” He understood her exactly — she was afraid the offer existed because of the two of them, the dinners, the conversations on her sofa. He kept his voice patient. “Eun-jung-ah, in the restaurant business back home there’s an old line — a deal can fall apart and the goodwill stays standing. Me, I’ve never bought anything on goodwill in my life. I buy returns.”

He turned his head. His gaze crossed to her face — set tight now — and the last of the playfulness left his voice. “Kim Kwang-soo is a fool. But he’s a fool in possession of you six. In Korea, your press is terminal — call it unsalvageable, because it is. And outside Korea?”

“Outside Korea,” he went on, “the Chinese market is bigger than anything you’re picturing.”

China landed, and her eyelids flickered. It wasn’t a new idea — Korean agencies had circled that market for a decade — but the standard playbook was one joint concert, or parking a Chinese-fluent member on some token program. A real, systematized push had never once succeeded. When idols here said overseas, they meant Japan. T-ara’s own passports told that story.

“Why China and not Japan, then?” Low, careful, uncertain. “We don’t have a single inch of fan base over there. Not one.”

“Because your sound was built for China.” And that wasn’t a line he’d improvised on the sofa. Earworm hooks stacked over hard choreography, arriving at the exact front edge of a wave: the generation born in the nineties — China’s trendsetters — just now clocking into their first jobs with the first money of their lives. T-ara walking into that market today would be shooting fish in a barrel.

He laid two fingers on the coffee table and tapped, unhurried. “So. Be good, and wait at the dorm. The day I put the contract down in front of you — no refusing to sign. That’s the deal.”

“And when you go back, sound out Bo-ram and the other four. Save me the labor of winning them over one at a time later.”

Buying T-ara needed more than Kim Kwang-soo’s signature. All six members had to say yes — one refusal, and there was no “T-ara” left to transfer.

She looked at him — at the confidence sitting out in the open where anyone could see it, unhidden and unhurried.

“Okay.” The first easy smile of her evening finally arrived, and she settled into the sofa with it. “If that day comes — mine is the first signature on the line.”

“Pinky swear?” He held out his little finger — then caught himself and blinked at his own hand. Contagious. Sulli really is contagious.

She glared at him, half amused, and puffed out her lips. “Yah. How old are you? Honestly — such a baby.”

All of that said, she still reached out and hooked her slim little finger through his, and held on tight — cool fingers closing around warm ones.

Fingertips met. Warmth traveled through skin — the steady, settling kind.

Girl-group hands really were another species of hand, though. Cool as porcelain. Not bad at all.

Somewhere in the middle of that observation, Eun-jung snatched her hand back. She checked the time on her phone and yelped.

“Yah! It’s past nine — I have to get back to the dorm, or the unnies will start getting ideas.” She scrambled to her feet and swatted the wrinkles out of her hoodie.

“I’ll drive you.” He was already up, lifting the car keys off the hook.

“No — no need! I drove myself.” Her face went pink on the spot, both hands waving him off.

He didn’t insist. “Fine, no escort, then. Message me on Kakao when you’re home.”

“Ne. Then… Chef-nim. Thank you for the crayfish today. It was really, really good.”

She stepped into her shoes at the entryway, and just before the door, she turned back and gave him one sweet, full smile — those smiling eyes of hers, the ones half the fancams in the country were built on, bending into two neat crescents.

“Off I go!”

“Drive safe.”

The security door swung shut behind her with one deep, final clunk.

✦ ✦ ✦

Ji-yeon stood alone in the entryway, which had become noticeably larger, and looked at the guest slippers sitting side by side, perfectly straight. He shook his head, defeated.

All that squirming shyness on the way in — and out the door like a delivery scooter.

But everything he’d told her tonight was the actual plan.

To buy T-ara’s contracts, step one was pulling his ₩8 billion back out of Samsung Electronics.

September’s rally was barely open. Per the chart he carried in his head, foreign capital was already streaming in — and two more weeks would bring the mid-September primary wave, the strong one, the kind that turned a position into a snowball. His ₩8 billion would come off that hill at double.

By then, the ace he needed to take T-ara off Kim Kwang-soo’s hands would already be sitting in his palm.
