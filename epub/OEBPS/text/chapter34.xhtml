Chapter Thirty-Four

Why Don’t You Just Read My Whole Account

SAME AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Old Chen’s restaurant

The corner of Ji-yeon’s mouth lifted, and something unguarded came into the way he looked at Old Chen.

“Old Chen — honestly? We came to Cheongdam today hunting a storefront for our brand. I saw the transfer notice on your window and came in to ask about it.”

Park Seon-young, hearing his boss lay the cards on the table that baldly, nearly swallowed his own tongue. He leaned into Ji-yeon’s ear, urgent and sotto voce: “Sajangnim — shouldn’t we at least sound out his price first? That direct, and what if he asks for the moon?”

Ji-yeon shot him a sidelong glance, amusement all through it.

“Old Park. You don’t run the playbook on a man who keeps his floor that clean. Restaurant folk don’t haggle each other into the ground.”

He said it in Korean, pitched at exactly the volume a man across the table could hear on purpose.

He had grown up in restaurant kitchens; his whole family had. The oldest unwritten rule of that world was that you did not squeeze the man holding the ladle — and he had no intention of running games on this one.

Old Chen’s homely smile deepened, and the approval in his eyes needed no translation.

“Straightforward young man.”

The old cook sighed, lifted his teacup, and drank like a man settling an argument with himself. “Then I’ll tell you the truth of it. I do want to sell. Eleven years in this country. I came out here young, to make something of myself — and now I’m not young, and I want to go back to my roots. Back to Sichuan, to my wife and kids. This store is my life’s work. The site, the renovation, every pot and bowl in that kitchen — I placed them with my own two hands. If I could still stand the hours, believe me, you couldn’t pry this place out of my hands.”

Ji-yeon listened without interrupting once. He understood that particular ache better than most men his age — his parents had carried the same one across the Pacific for twenty years before they finally got to come home.

Ji-yeon nodded and quietly took the thread off his hands for him.

“Old Chen — what number did you have in mind?”

Old Chen rubbed his hands together, and the awkwardness finally surfaced.

“Young man, this is… a little complicated. Strictly speaking, what I hold is the business right — the license to run the store. The building itself isn’t mine.”

“Whose is it?” Ji-yeon’s expression didn’t so much as ripple. This was ordinary enough in Korea. Seoul commercial property ran neck and neck with Manhattan and San Francisco; without ten billion won in hand, an ordinary buyer never even got invited to the table.

“Couldn’t tell you.” A wry smile. “Half a year ago, the old landlord sold the building and left the country. The buyer was a young woman. She and I only ever speak by phone — she seems to be a very busy person. Once a month, when the rent is due, my phone rings. That is the entire relationship.”

“So we’d be negotiating with the young woman,” Park Seon-young cut in, quick and unhappy.

This was precisely the scenario he least wanted to meet today. Taking over a storefront in Korea meant clearing three hurdles in order:

Park Seon-young’s Mental Checklist — Storefront Transfer, Korea

Step 1Business-rights transfer — negotiated with the operator

Step 2Key-money deposit — typically ₩500–800 million, Cheongdam rates on top

Step 3Monthly rent — payable to whoever holds the title

With the business right and the property title sitting in two different hands, you could shake on your side of the table and still get vetoed on the other. Two negotiations, two chances to fall through.

“Right.” Old Chen nodded, fished a battered flip phone out of his pocket, thumbed through it, and slid a memo slip across the table. “Here is her number.” He knew exactly how much trouble he was handing over, and he patched at it awkwardly. “The young lady is very polite. It should… work out.”

Ji-yeon picked up the slip. The digits had been written in a hand that was graceful and yet angled like it meant business — elegant strokes with a blade hidden in them. He laughed under his breath.

“It’s fine. Business is business — anyone’s money spends the same.”

He took out his phone and glanced at Park. “Old Park, keep Boss Chen company. I’ll step out and make the call.”

And out the door he went.

Park Seon-young watched his boss’s back and felt his stomach perform a slow rotation. All he could do now was pray that the “very polite” young lady on the other end of that number was not another moon-asker — he had met a full parade of them today, and he had stopped keeping count somewhere after lunch.

Outside, under the restaurant’s awning, Ji-yeon leaned against the wall and dialed.

Phone · the owner of 312 Cheongdam-dong → Song Ji-yeon

Two rings. Then the line picked up — into silence. No hello. No greeting of any kind. Only the faintest sound of breathing, light and careful, as if she were waiting for him to go first.

“Yeoboseyo — am I speaking with the owner of 312 Cheongdam-dong? My name is Song Ji-yeon. Boss Chen passed me your number. I’d like to talk about taking over the restaurant.”

Three full seconds of quiet on the line.

“Ah — Uncle Chen isn’t renewing?” A woman’s voice. A pleasant one — cool and clear, like water running over stones.

“He’s put up the notice, yes. I’d like to take over the whole operation, kitchen and all. If it’s convenient, could we find a time to sit down and go through the details?”

“I’m… out of the country for the next few days. Could I contact you in a couple of days?” A note of apology had crept into the cool voice.

“That… works. I’ll be in touch, then.”

“Ne. Sorry for the trouble.”

Ji-yeon blinked, phone still at his ear.

Sorry for the trouble? Wait. That wasn’t how this was supposed to go. Boss Chen had promised him a polite landlord, yes — but wasn’t Ji-yeon the one asking to rent here? How had he ended up feeling like the client? This young woman was, if anything, too earnest for the part.

He was still assembling a courteous reply when the line went dead — a flat busy tone. She had simply hung up.

He stared at the phone for half a second. Then he laughed out loud, alone under the awning.

“Interesting.”

Back inside, Park Seon-young was keeping Old Chen company with the dedication of a man watching a pot that might boil over. He shot to his feet the moment the door moved.

“Sajangnim — how did it go? Is it settled?”

“We’ve agreed to meet.” Ji-yeon sat back down and lifted his teacup. “In a few days, to talk it through.”

“And the other party… is she reasonable?”

“Hard to say.” The cool voice was still replaying somewhere in his head, and the smile on his mouth only deepened. “But I can promise you one thing — this negotiation is going to be interesting.”

He turned to Old Chen. “Old Chen, thank you for the meal today. The money for it is under the teacup.”

The business right was still Old Chen’s to sell, which meant a transfer fee still had to be discussed — in the neighborhood of three hundred million won, by Ji-yeon’s own rough math. The accounts would be kept clean, every line of them. They were both, when you got all the way down to it, men whose families had ground out their living in kitchens far from home — and he was not about to shave a single won off another man’s life’s work.

Old Chen looked at the ten-thousand-won notes pinned under the teacup and waved both hands in protest.

“Young man, what’s this? A plain home meal! Any guest who knows my tofu by its first bite is family in this shop — I can’t take your money for it.”

“It’s only right.” Ji-yeon stood, clapped Park Seon-young on the shoulder. “Let’s head out.”

Park hurried after him, and even out on the sidewalk the worry refused to let go of his face.

“Sajangnim, I still think this whole thing is shaky. What was her attitude like on the phone? Difficult?”

Ji-yeon considered it for a few steps. “She doesn’t say much.”

“…She doesn’t say much. Is that good or bad?”

“It means she isn’t fluent in the process. And the voice was a young woman’s. I’d call that good.”

The profile was already assembling itself in his head, complete and confident: a young woman; not much formal business training; money in hand and unwilling to gamble it on the stock market, so she’d parked it in the safest vehicle a nervous investor knows — brick and mortar; a steady temperament; and a very clear-eyed sense of exactly what she was and wasn’t good at.

And the young woman on the other end of that call, wherever in the world she was: “Hold on. Did you just read me cover to cover? Why don’t you just read my whole account while you’re at it.”

✦ ✦ ✦

They spent what remained of the afternoon dragging themselves through two more storefronts, sun still hammering their backs. Neither survived contact. One was dark and chopped into too many cubbies to open up into a hotpot floor; the other needed a ductwork retrofit that would never clear legal and fire code in this district — or possibly in this century.

Everything kept circling back to the same conclusion: nothing fit like Old Chen’s.

Park Seon-young mopped at his neck, collar soaked through, and finally surrendered to complaint. “Sajangnim, these Cheongdam landlords are bandits to a man. The rents are armed robbery and they won’t move a single won. I say we look somewhere else entirely.”

Four-plus negotiations in one day, every monthly rent floating around ten million won — steep money by any measure on this Peninsula. Mind you, each of ONGI’s seven flagship stores paid just eight million a month, and those were prime corners.

Ji-yeon checked his phone: 4:40 PM. The September sun had begun sliding west, the horizon shading into a dulled orange-red, and the wind carried a chill now that you could actually feel.

“That’s a day. Back to the office — tidy up and get out of here.”

“Ne — thank you, Sajangnim!” Park’s long-suffering face finally remembered how to smile.

✦ ✦ ✦

EVENING Mapo-gu, Seoul SAVORY F&B headquarters, the CEO’s office

They drove back to the company in Mapo. Ji-yeon reached his office, hung the grey suit jacket on the coat rack, and sank into the broad leather chair behind the desk — and before he’d managed so much as a glass of water, the phone on it began to buzz like an angry hornet.

He picked it up. Ham Eun-jung.

This was a first. Since the day they’d exchanged numbers, every message between them had been typed. She had never once called.

He answered with a teasing note already loaded.

Phone · Ham Eun-jung → Song Ji-yeon

“Yeoboseyo, Eun-jung-ah. Calling at this hour — missing my crayfish already?”

“Yah, Song Ji-yeon-ssi! Who misses your crayfish?!”

The protest came through the receiver half-squeak. Even down a phone line, you could hear the heat in her ears.

On her end, Eun-jung turned to find five members watching her with five raised eyebrows, and withered internally. She ducked her head, dropped her voice, and produced the actual reason for the call at roughly half speed and one-third volume:

“So — the thing is… it’s Park Ji-yeon, and the others, and So-yeon-unnie… everyone was hoping to buy you dinner.”

“Oh? All six of you, inviting me out?”

Surprise, then a fingertip tapping the desk — and then it clicked. Eun-jung hadn’t managed to land her unnies on her own. Time to call in the heavy artillery.

“Ne… After we came back from Jeju, we talked — all of us, for a long time. So-yeon-unnie said that for you to stand up and support us in that hall… she’s grateful. Truly. And a certain someone recites ‘the gallant hotpot CEO’ from morning to night and insists she has to meet you in person and fleece you down to the—”

A cough. Two coughs. “—thank you. She meant thank you.”

The slip had no cover whatsoever. Ji-yeon laughed before he could stop himself. That little dragon and her one-of-a-kind wiring.

But it suited his plans perfectly. To pull T-ara out of the CCM firepit, he needed more than Kim Kwang-soo’s signature — he needed the transfer consent of all six members, and tonight the persuasion work could begin over charcoal and hanwoo.

“When six great beauties extend an invitation, what kind of man says no? What are we eating? My treat.”

“Absolutely not — we agreed, this one is ours! The maknae asked for hanwoo, so hanwoo it is. We found a place in Cheongdam-dong — very private, sasaeng-proof and Anti-proof. I’ll send you the address right now.”

“Then I’m in your hands.”

They hung up. Barely seconds later, his phone buzzed once — a Kakao message with a location pin.

KakaoTalk · Ham Eun-jung

Ham Eun-jung

Tteurak Hanwoo House, Cheongdam-dong. Private Room 3.

[ location pin attached ]

Ji-yeon looked at the name on the screen and the corner of his mouth curled up. Tteurak — one of Seoul’s storied old hanwoo houses, the kind of place where politicians, chaebols, and top-tier entertainers went to eat where nobody could see them chew. Privacy and prestige were the entire menu, and the prices made your teeth ache. The girls had genuinely splurged for this.

He stood, took the suit jacket back off the rack, and squared the collar of his white shirt in the glass.

On his way out he passed the reception desk, where Park Seon-young — bag on shoulder — was making the front-desk girl shake with laughter hard enough to rattle her name plate. Ji-yeon’s admiration, at that moment, was sincere and bottomless. The man was thirty-two, built like a parking cone, with a face so aggressively ordinary it circled back around into abstract art — a man who, by every law of nature, should have died alone, and had instead sweet-talked a genuinely beautiful woman into marrying him.

When Ji-yeon had first joined the company, back before half the staff could recognize him on sight, he’d overheard the break-room intelligence on Director Park. The summary: the flag at home stood firmly while a whole row of colorful banners fluttered merrily outside.

At the time, he had felt not one gram of judgment. Only a powerful, swelling urge to bow and ask to be taken on as a student.

Hyung, I genuinely want to learn this.

“Old Park, I’m off. You clear out too — and stop terrorizing my reception desk.” He jangled his car keys once, grinning.

“Ne! Safe driving, Sajangnim!” Park beamed, entirely unrepentant.

In the underground garage, Ji-yeon slid into the black Mercedes, and the low growl of the engine carried him out into the evening.
