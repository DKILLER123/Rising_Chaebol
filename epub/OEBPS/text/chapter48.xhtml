Chapter Forty-Eight

Lim Yoon-ah

SAME AFTERNOON · CONTINUOUS Gangnam-gu, Seoul Dusk café — the window table

“Annyeonghaseyo, Song Ji-yeon-ssi.”

“Annyeonghaseyo… Yoon-ah-ssi.” He was on his feet before he knew he had moved, and he made no attempt to hide any of it. “You’re the owner? Of that building?”

He had never once connected that cool, brisk, phone-only voice with Lim Yoon-ah — the center of SNSD, the nation’s girl group, its official face.

Lim Yoon-ah took in his expression — the one usually reserved for seeing a ghost in broad daylight — and the corner of her mouth curved, polite and precisely one millimeter distant. That he recognized her did not surprise her even slightly. A face like hers carried that expectation around the way other people carried a phone.

“It’s me. Lim Yoon-ah — Uncle Chen’s landlord.” Her voice stayed even, emotion carefully rationed. “Surprised?”

He drew one long breath and let the world reassemble itself, because it had clearly been attempting something magical.

Did I kick over an idol nest somewhere in a past life?

Lately, every other person he met existed — as far as his previous self had been concerned — only inside a television. Eun-jung first. Then Sulli. Then Kim Ji-won. And now Lim Yoon-ah was materializing out of his call log.

Lim Yoon-ah, a landlord. In what universe was that a sensible sentence? How old was she even? A ’90 liner — his own year — and she was already collecting rent in Cheongdam, where they sold dirt by the gram. Was the idol business genuinely that profitable? Mr. Song privately rated his own face a solid B-plus. Perhaps he ought to tidy himself up and debut as well.

His regressor’s superiority complex took a direct hit — the first real one — and staggered.

And the worst part: that confident little profile of the mystery owner he had assembled off a single phone call. Young woman. No formal business training. Cautious money, parked in brick and mortar. Steady temperament. Honest with herself. Letter-perfect, every line. He had read her cover to cover, exactly as she had complained — and somehow missed the headline entirely: the brick and mortar belonged to the center of SNSD.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi?”

He had gone quiet and started — staring, frankly, staring — and she prompted him in two cool words. She did not enjoy being inventoried like that; her internal ledger of goodwill toward him marked down a full row.

“Ah — mianhae.” He came back to himself, retracted the stare, and took a large medicinal swallow of iced americano, letting the bitterness do the cooling. “It’s only — the landlord turning out to be Yoon-ah-ssi. That was more surprise than my afternoon was built to carry.” He coughed, once, dryly.

She nodded, unruffled. She held more than one property on the Peninsula, most of it leased out, and every tenant upon first meeting her wore some version of this exact face.

“Many people say that.” Flat as still water. She lifted a hand to the server, ordered a lemon water, and returned her attention to him. “Let’s talk business, Song Ji-yeon-ssi.”

Her posture was square, her hands folded on the table — the complete table setting of a person here strictly on business.

“Uncle Chen tells me you have your eye on his shop. The whole operation — you’d take it over outright?”

“That’s right.” He banked the fanboy somewhere out of sight, and what replaced it at the table was a restaurateur with seven stores to his name. “The location suits us, and the floor plan suits us. We intend to convert it into a high-end, service-first hotpot house.”

“A hotpot house?” One fine eyebrow rose a fraction, a question mark riding in the tone. “In Cheongdam? Forgive my frankness, Song Ji-yeon-ssi, but dining in this neighborhood runs to Western, to Japanese, to upscale bars. Hotpot… doesn’t seem to match the air of the place.”

She had eaten hotpot before — the cuisine had its own unmistakable charm. But Cheongdam was Cheongdam, and what she was actually running was risk: whether this young man could keep a storefront alive on this street at all. If the shop died inside the year, what would follow was a rent-relief negotiation, an early-termination wrangle, months of back-and-forth — and her calendar had no vacancies for any of that.

“Yoon-ah-ssi isn’t wrong.” He smiled, entirely unshaken. “And that’s precisely why the seat is empty. It’s a market gap.” He let it sit for one beat. “What I have in mind isn’t the traditional hotpot floor — all noise and steam and shouting. My company, SAVORY F&B, operates high-end service hotpot and nothing else. Ingredient quality, broth development, one-to-one dedicated service — the working benchmark is a Michelin restaurant.”

He paused, and the confidence in his voice needed no footnote. “We run seven direct stores in Seoul already, open for under two weeks — and the response has been very good. I believe the same experience will sell in Cheongdam. It brings the neighborhood something it doesn’t currently have.”

Lim Yoon-ah listened to all of it without one interruption. When she didn’t understand a thing, she stayed quiet about it — a survival skill the entertainment industry had drilled into her over many years, and one she wore like a seatbelt.

“So — what I’d like to know is where Yoon-ah-ssi stands. On the sublease. Or a sale, if it came to that.”

“A sale isn’t on the table.” Prompt, and clean. “I bought the property half a year ago as a long-term holding — preserving capital, not flipping it. I don’t intend to part with it lightly.”

“As for the sublease—” unhurried, she drew a folder from the Chanel bag on the seat beside her and slid it across the table “—that, I can consider. This is my lease agreement with Uncle Chen. Read it first.”

He took the folder and read it through, properly. The terms were ordinary; strip away the boilerplate, and only two lines had ever really mattered to him — the monthly rent, and the key money.

He read the two numbers. Then read them again.

312 Cheongdam-dong — Existing Lease Terms

Monthly rent₩10,000,000

Key money (deposit)₩400,000,000

Neighborhood benchmark₩12,000,000+ per month

He looked up, and the puzzlement was all over his face.

“Yoon-ah-ssi. Isn’t this… a little too low?”

“Is it?” She lifted the lemon water and took a sip, cool and unhurried. “Uncle Chen kept his shop in that building for over ten years. It wasn’t easy for him. When I took the property over, I never intended to raise his rent — and I haven’t. You’re taking the shop from his hands, so you’ll be held to the same standard he was.”

His mental file on the nation’s goddess quietly rewrote another page. He had walked in braced for a price-gouge — Gangnam-adjacent, star-adjacent markups, the works — and had instead found this.

“Yoon-ah-ssi — thank you.” He closed the folder, and this time there was nothing performative in the thanks at all.

“Don’t thank me. This is a business arrangement.” She looked at him evenly. “But I do have one condition attached.”

“Please.”

“A five-year contract. And the first year’s rent paid to me in advance, as a lump sum. After that first year, monthly.”

She didn’t believe in this hotpot concept — not yet, not really — and so she was buying herself one full year in which she would not have to think about the building even once. It was, he thought with genuine admiration, exactly the clause he would have written himself.

“No problem.” A smile got loose before he could hold it. “You read my mind — I’d have asked for the same terms myself.”

Frankly, if he’d had the spare capital, he’d have offered to buy the building outright and parked it on the company’s books. Cheongdam property only knew one direction, and by now it was trading at Manhattan and San Francisco prices.

“Then, Song Ji-yeon-ssi.” Her hand came across the table. “To a pleasant partnership.”

“To a pleasant partnership.” He took it. Her hand was warm and soft, and the clasp was over almost the moment it had begun.

With the letter of intent signed, Lim Yoon-ah rose to go.

“I have a schedule. I’ll leave first.”

“Of course. Drive safely.” He didn’t try to hold her for even one extra minute. Whatever existed between the two of them at this moment, it was landlord and tenant — complete, and closed.

And that suited him fine. The Goddess Handbook set no mandatory binding missions — everything ran on his own initiative — so there was no rushing Lim Yoon-ah, and no cause to start engineering proximity. Business first. Whatever else might ever happen would happen on its own clock.

EVENING Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Song Ji-yeon’s apartment

Home again. He paid Mushroom the scheduled tribute of a long round of fetch, then sat down at the computer and ran the numbers on his Samsung Electronics position.

Song Ji-yeon — Samsung Electronics, Running Tally

Position openedhalf a month ago · 2× leverage

Half-month resultup, down, net up — +₩1,000,000,000

Verdicttoo slow · liquidate at month’s end

And he still wasn’t satisfied. If anything, he was mildly annoyed.

Half a month for a billion won — and that with the leverage turned up to two. By any ordinary human standard, spectacular. By the standard of what he intended to build, one more brick in a wall that needed a quarry.

“Long-term holds really don’t build capital.” He sighed at the screen and made the call: cash out at the end of the month. And once the money was actually in hand—?

He hadn’t forgotten the two cards sitting in the Handbook — the Tenfold Rebate Card, and the Stock Forecast Card.

As for the three great cash incinerators — buying T-ara outright, the ONGI expansion, the zero-sugar soju campaign — those could keep, until the treasury was deep enough to plan them properly.

The plan, in any case, was already drafted. The Stock Forecast Card called out the single fastest-rising stock of its week — forecast, buy in, sell out five days later, done. A conveyor belt, against Samsung’s slow bake. The two weren’t in the same league for efficiency.

There was, of course, one input requirement: Eun-jung. Weekly, in person, close enough — and sufficiently heart-flustered — to trigger the Goddess Ability and print the next Stock Forecast Card.

Which meant making a national idol’s heart flutter. On a schedule.

That was, by any measure, not going to be an easy thing.
