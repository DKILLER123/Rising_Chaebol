Chapter Thirty-Nine

Well, You Never Asked

SAME DAY · LATE AFTERNOON Yeouido, Han River Park To the Beautiful You — location set, minutes after the accident

“Unnie — you’re sure you’re not hurt? Anywhere at all?”

Kim Ji-won shook her head slowly. Her eyes, though, stayed on Song Ji-yeon.

Their gazes met — and in his she found something she had no way to file: a faint, glowing eagerness. Greed, almost, banked low like a furnace.

Her heart picked up an erratic little trot and refused to name its reason. Was this a flutter? Or the aftershock of standing half a second from a hospital gurney, every nerve still ringing? Perhaps the two had simply pooled their money.

Tch. She scolded herself silently. Two whole minutes of thinking better of you, and it turns out you’re a lech like all the rest.

“I’m fine.” She accepted Sulli’s arm and got properly upright, and said it again, softer, for Sulli’s sake. Then she lifted the bottle he’d given her and drank. Cool water sliding down her throat did a surprising amount for a heartbeat that refused to behave.

Song Ji-yeon, for the record, was not ogling anyone on purpose.

The situation was this: the Goddess Handbook’s ordinary rewards were, as established, mostly chicken ribs. The bond rewards, however, were crown jewels, every single one. And he had just read — not imagined, read — the prize hanging off Kim Ji-won’s bond.

The underlying source code of TikTok.

For anyone who spent the twenty-tens under a rock: the international twin of the app that would eat the world. In his old life the thing had lived on his phone like a second nervous system — thumb, scroll, hours gone — and it had lifted a hundred-billion-dollar empire on its back. A nuke you could carry in a briefcase.

And in September 2012, none of it existed. Anywhere. Zhang Yiming was still heads-down on his news aggregator, Toutiao. The short-video prototype with the unpronounceable name — A.me — wouldn’t so much as appear for another four years. Which meant the window stood open for four entire years. Get that source code into his hands early enough, and he could hijack the unicorn before it was ever foaled.

How was a man supposed to keep his eyes politely neutral while reading that off someone’s face?

He was, at that moment, entirely prepared to drop to both knees and kowtow to Kim Ji-won if it would activate the bond today. Call her eomma while he was down there? …Not necessarily out of the question.

A man’s knees are cast in gold. They simply haven’t met the hour worth kneeling for.

Kim Ji-won, meanwhile, had no access to any of this context. All she had was a man whose gaze kept returning to her with escalating boldness, considerably hotter than before, and her own face flooding from deathly pale to rosy in response. Shy on the surface — and, in the privacy of her own skull, a small, unworthy flicker of triumph. So my charms really are unavoidable. Noted.

She hadn’t started out charmed, for the record. Chaebol circles being what they were — everyone in this industry knows how that story goes — she had heard plenty from company sunbaes about heirs and young Sajangnims and what happened to female idols who caught their eye. A twenty-something restaurant company president had slotted, automatically, into that exact drawer. She’d wanted nothing to do with him.

But today had bent the drawer. That gaze of his burned hot, yes — yet there was no slime in it, none of that greasy calculation she’d braced herself against. And if not for him, she would currently be horizontal in the back of an ambulance, being asked to wiggle her toes.

She put the chaos away, bowed to him properly, and meant every syllable.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. Thank you. Truly. If you hadn’t moved the instant you did, I would already be in that ambulance.”

“Don’t mention it. It was nothing.” He surfaced from his spreadsheet long enough to smile at her.

His mood, in fact, was excellent. Historic. He looked at Kim Ji-won and saw nothing but promise — more with every look. Actress? What actress. This was a walking cash register. A future dairy cow of dividends. A hen that laid golden eggs on schedule.

The Saero zero-sugar soju technology was sitting in his system space at this very moment, patient as a deed. In his last life that product had been an outright monster — the “zero sugar” health pitch had marched straight into Jinro’s territory and bitten off a slab of it. A hundred billion won a year in sales. Not revenue vapor, either — real, distillable profit.

And then there was the TikTok code. Bond completion looked… geological, timeline-wise. But it was like holding a winning lottery ticket before the redemption date — useless tonight, and utterly intoxicating to own.

Somewhere in his skull the abacus was clacking at full gallop, and the smile on his face kept brightening accordingly.

Kim Ji-won watched that undisguised, luminous grin and felt her understanding of the man lose structural integrity. When he’d moved to save her, he’d been fast as a hunting cat — power and certainty, the kind that made a person’s knees offer opinions. When he’d turned on the production, his presence had flattened the argument before it existed; she had felt, for one strange second, shielded. And now he stood there beaming like a kid who’d found a wallet on the sidewalk — a pure, glittering delight with no visible target.

“Um… Song Ji-yeon-ssi.” She hesitated, then made herself ask. “Your arm — it didn’t get scraped, when you dove? Just now?”

“Me?” He lifted the arm, rolled the shoulder, gave the elbow a bounce. “Fine. Right as rain.”

The impact hadn’t even qualified as a warm-up. Praise be to body enhancement: useless junk right up until the exact moment it saved your life, at which point — well. So much for useless junk.

Sulli had circled back around, and she was looking from one of them to the other with her big eyes gone round as saucers, fully in worship mode.

“Oppa, that was SO cool. Like — like they were shooting an action movie and you were the stunt double!”

“Yes, yes. Enough.” He rubbed a hand over her hair, wrecking what the wind hadn’t. “Go distribute the desserts. Sugar’s good for shock — everyone needs one.”

The crew descended on the site with admirable speed. The guilty lighting tech received a dressing-down from the assistant director that could strip paint, then shuffled over to bow, gray-faced, to Kim Ji-won and Song Ji-yeon in turn. The stand was hauled upright, the sandbags doubled. Within fifteen minutes the set looked exactly as it had before a two-meter steel tower had nearly flattened one of the drama’s leads — the entire operation efficient to the point of coldness, as if the whole thing had happened to somebody else, on some other set.

Director Jeon Ki-sang clapped his hands, twice, loud.

“All right, all right! Departments, places! We go again on the next setup!”

He did not ask — at no point, in any form, did it occur to him to ask — whether Kim Ji-won needed five minutes to stop her hands from shaking.

She stood where she was, pulled one long breath in, and pressed the last of the tremors down flat. Then she looked over at Song Ji-yeon, hesitated, and finally gathered the courage to walk over.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi.” A small bow. “Thank you again — for today, all of it. When we wrap, I’d like to buy you dinner. It’s the least of what I owe.”

That surprised him into a blink.

Honestly? His entire agenda today had been one line item: farm the Goddess Ability reward off Choi Sulli — the Tenfold Rebate Card. Ever since binding Sulli out of nowhere and picking up the body enhancement, he’d probed and prodded and patted and never re-triggered the ability, and the card had sat there uncollected like a parcel nobody would sign for. And then today it had simply dropped into his lap — and brought an entire Kim Ji-won with it as a bonus. Mission accomplished with a bow on top.

Nothing else on the schedule. He smiled.

“Sure. I’ll take you up on it, then.”

She nodded and returned to her mark. Sulli materialized instantly, bouncing.

“Oppa, you’re not leaving?! Yes! Then I’m coming to dinner too!”

“Fine, fine. Go. Act. First.”

He found the rest area at the set’s edge and folded himself into the fishing chair standing there. The chair was pitifully low; his legs had nowhere to go and jutted straight out in front of him like a foal’s; he sat sunk in the thing with his knees at his ears, a fully grown man in kindergarten furniture. From the outside it looked, frankly, ridiculous.

He dug out his phone and performed some serious-looking scrolling. Behind the screen, the abacus galloped on.

T-ara’s contracts: thirty billion. The ONGI expansion: seven hundred twenty billion at minimum. His current funds, set against those two gold-swallowing monsters, were a teacup in a reservoir.

But now he held the piece that broke the board.

Saero zero-sugar soju. That was the first bucket of gold — no. The first gold mine. Building a distillery from zero took years he didn’t have; the smart play was to buy an existing, half-dead soju factory outright. Retrofit the line, load in the new formula, then bury the country in marketing until the thing went nuclear.

And the money for that came from where?

His mind drifted, fondly, to the Tenfold Rebate Card he’d collected off Sulli — the entire reason he’d come out here today. The card was, functionally, a seed-capital amplifier built to his exact specifications: put in one pile of money, receive back ten piles. Nobody had told him the ceiling. But even if the thing topped out at a billion won per activation, on his balance sheet that was still a small miracle.

As for TikTok — that was the nuke, yes. He didn’t currently own so much as a silo. Source code was only the first stone: servers, an engineering team, a content ecosystem, global marketing — every step of it burned money at astronomical rates. That was a problem for the version of him with actual capital. Rice gets eaten one bite at a time. Roads get walked one step at a time.

Besides — Ji-won’s bond wasn’t activated. Dreaming was free, and that was all this was.

He settled back against the brutally hard chair, and his eyes drifted, on their own, to the two women filming.

Kim Ji-won stood at her mark running lines at the camera, chin high, an aristocratic little scoff living in her eyebrows — not a single visible trace of the near-death experience she’d walked away from twenty minutes ago.

Song Ji-yeon found this genuinely fascinating. He’d spent limited hours with her, but even he could tell Kim Ji-won was the reserved type — quiet, measured, a locked-door kind of person. How did she flip a switch and do that on command? And come to think of it, the scripts kept handing her the same register: the prickly princess. From High Kick: Revenge of the Short-Legged to To the Beautiful You, to that chaebol-heiress role coming in The Heirs — the one that would blow her name into every household — tsundere, tsundere, tsundere.

The gap was enormous!!!

Perhaps this was what the manuals called an actor’s discipline. He didn’t understand it. He respected it.

As for Sulli, he would withhold review. Whether the writers simply didn’t trust the acting of a first-time adult lead and had sanded the character down to careless, sunny, hot-blooded — or whether the character was written that way from the start — either way, what he was watching was a very beautiful little fool. …Fine. He conceded the tinted glasses. In his eyes Choi Sulli had always registered as silly; the data could not be trusted.

EVENING · WRAP Yeouido, Han River Park golden hour over the river

Sunset had poured warm orange across the whole sky when the director called the final “CUT” of the day, and Choi Sulli was airborne before the echo faded — a bird shot out of a cage, beeline for Song Ji-yeon, joy plastered openly across her face from the first step.

“Oppa! You waited forever, didn’t you!”

Kim Ji-won took the long way: proper goodbyes to the director, to the staff, unhurried and exact, before she came walking over with that easy, trained grace. She stopped in front of him and bowed again, precise and sincere.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi. I’m sorry to have kept you waiting so long.”

He hauled himself up out of the chair — his rear end had gone thoroughly numb — and stretched until his spine crackled.

“No complaints. I had thinking to do anyway.”

Specifically, about a seven-hundred-billion-won hole, and a rebate card he had not yet found a chance to spend.

“Then shall we?” Kim Ji-won offered. “There’s a very good hanwoo house near here. Quiet. Discreet.”

“Hanwoo?” Sulli’s mouth folded into an instant pout, and she latched onto Ji-won’s arm, wheedling at maximum output. “Unnie, forget hanwoo, let’s do gopchang! That new cheese-gopchang place in Hongdae looks SO good—”

One of them wanted ambience. One of them wanted a food fight. Song Ji-yeon looked from princess to gremlin and privately enjoyed the problem.

“Or — hotpot.” He offered it into the middle. “Sulli’s been dying to try my restaurant forever, hasn’t she? Tonight’s as good a night as she’s getting.”

“Anywhere is fine with me.” Kim Ji-won was the host tonight; from where she stood, the entire point of the dinner was standing right there, and the menu was decoration.

“Yah, really?! But—” Sulli’s eyes went off like flashbulbs, then dimmed just as fast. “But Yeouido’s all the way out here. Gangnam’s far…”

The Peninsula is roughly the size of a Post-it note, he did not say. How far can any two points on it possibly be?

What he said was: “ONGI doesn’t only exist in Gangnam. There’s a company store in Yeongdeungpo-gu, too.”

He wasn’t being modest about the real estate logic, either. Yeouido was the Peninsula’s Wall Street — finance, broadcasting, and politics pressed into one island, and Yeongdeungpo’s daily foot traffic gave Gangnam a genuine race. No chance a man like him left that kind of gold seam un-mined; one of the original seven ONGI stores had gone in right there.

And, privately: he absolutely was not taking these two to Apgujeong. Ham Eun-jung lived directly above that ONGI. She barely used the apartment these days, but the odds of an accidental encounter were exactly the kind of odds his luck enjoyed. His body might be reinforced two and a half times over, but he still lived in fear of Eun-jung’s roundhouse — five oxen of force behind it, and the nightmares from that night still hadn’t faded.

…Wait. She and I are just friends. What, precisely, am I afraid of?

He shook his head at himself, gave up on the spiral, and smiled at the two of them.

“Let’s go. I drove — take my car.”

“CHINCHA?! I’ve wanted to go to oppa’s hotpot place forever, there was just never time! Oppa… why didn’t you TELL me there was a store in Yeouido!”

She knew perfectly well he ran a hotpot chain — she’d wanted to try it basically since they’d met — but she’d been chained to the Yeouido shoot, and once back in Gangnam there’d never been anyone to go with: Vic-unnie and Krystal were forever sprinting between schedules, and a restaurant alone held no appeal. If she’d known a store sat ten minutes from set, she’d have dragged Ji-won-unnie there in week one. This was all oppa’s fault, obviously.

She delivered this entire indictment with her cheeks puffed out, pouting at maximum peach, and Song Ji-yeon found himself, inexplicably, the one feeling guilty.

“Well — you never asked.”

No, hold on, is that fair? You never once said you wanted hotpot. Every time you took me out, it was a beef house. Did you forget that part?

Sulli opened her mouth for the rebuttal — and stopped. Had she… actually asked? At any point?

…It appeared she had not.

The little peach went from pout to scarlet in one smooth gradient, developed no counterargument, and elected silence.

Kim Ji-won watched the two of them ping-pong, and the corner of her mouth lifted without permission. She found — unexpectedly — that she liked this. The atmosphere sat warm and easy, like a tablecloth someone had already smoothed.

The three of them walked out through the park, past the dark river, to where the Mercedes was parked — and pointed it toward Yeongdeungpo.
