Chapter One Hundred

Let’s Go Camping

FRIDAY AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul Park Ji-yeon’s flat, second floor — the living room

“Daebak! You will NEVER guess who I walked in on at oppa’s place yesterday!”

Park Ji-yeon dropped backward into the sofa like a stone into silk, a giant plush bear hugged to her chest, big eyes doing slow laps across every unnie’s face. Everything about her posture said ask me. ask me right now.

Jeon Bo-ram was working through a bag of chips at hamster pace, cheeks fully loaded. She stopped mid-crunch.

“Who? Some top idol? Or— did Song Ji-yeon-ssi have a girlfriend over and you caught them?”

A loud maknae was weather. Bo-ram had long since adapted.

Park Hyo-min set her coffee down and leaned all the way in, eyes lit up like a switchboard.

“Wait — Ji-yeon-ah. You went downstairs for one freeloaded dinner and came back with a state secret? Out with it. Anybody who puts that face on is not holding nothing. Is it really a girlfriend?”

Back in the dorm days, Hyo-min had shared a room with the little dragon, and the dragon was a leak with a lease — she’d kept a steady drip of intelligence flowing ever since, roommates or not. The Eun-jung-and-oppa material, for instance, had reached her exactly that way.

How far had that one run by now, she wondered?

And now a second woman surfacing — that couldn’t be good for Eun-jung-unnie’s mood, could it?

She sneaked one look sideways. Eun-jung-unnie was at the kitchen island, elbow-deep in a sink of fruit, serene. Hyo-min turned back, all ears.

Lee Qri was doing her nails. At the question she looked up too, swept her long hair back over one shoulder, curiosity moving behind her eyes.

“Don’t tell me you walked in on something you weren’t supposed to see. A kept woman tucked away somewhere, hm? Aigoo — we’re artists under his company now, and you’re out here broadcasting the boss’s private life? Careful. Men like that have long memories and small revenges.”

Ham Eun-jung came out of the open kitchen carrying a platter of cut fruit, caught the drift of the speculation escalating behind her, and smiled the smile of a woman folding laundry during a house fire. She set the platter on the tea table and, in passing, tapped one finger lightly against the maknae’s forehead.

“You. Stop dangling the carrot. Who is it that has you wound up like this?”

Her voice carried its usual settled warmth.

“And all of you — don’t overcomplicate Song Ji-yeon-ssi. He’s handsome, he’s comfortable, he’s good-natured. Of course he has friends. That’s the whole mystery.”

“Aigoo, Eun-jung-unnie, you’re the least fun person alive!”

Ji-yeon rubbed her forehead and dropped her voice to a spy-film murmur, sweeping the room with her eyes before committing.

“This person isn’t just a great beauty. She’s an industry insider. One we all know. Guess who?”

Park So-yeon had been quiet this whole time — a still, cool arrangement of long hair and a fashion magazine, giving off the specific temperature of someone reading in a room where a party is breaking out. The soft-sweet face with the frost setting: friendly at rest, glacial when she chose.

The boss’s romantic history did not, as a rule, interest her. An industry insider, however — that did.

She closed the magazine and looked up.

“Yah, Park Ji-yeon. Enough theater. Say the name, or it’s house discipline.”

The maknae and youngest of six, and the most feral member of the group by a distance. Getting a straight answer out of her without force had, historically, never once occurred.

“So-yeon-unnie! Finally! An actual reaction!”

The threat rolled off her completely — with So-yeon-unnie’s build, Ji-yeon was fairly sure she could take two of her at once. What actually registered was the fact itself: the eternally indifferent So-yeon-unnie, invested in oppa gossip. Now that was newsworthy.

She cleared her throat with full ceremony and delivered the headline.

“It’s Sulli. From f(x)!”

“Sulli?”

Nearly in unison — and even Lee Qri’s eyes had come fully open.

Eun-jung was surprised too. She settled into the corner of the sofa, frowning lightly.

“Why would Sulli be at oppa’s home? He doesn’t follow the industry — apart from us, I don’t think he knows a single idol. How did those two even meet?”

“See, this is the part you don’t know. I walk in the door yesterday and there’s Sulli sitting at the dinner table — I genuinely checked the house number. Thought I’d walked into the wrong building.”

Ji-yeon was fully launched now, legs folded up under her, hands conducting the story.

“So listen. They met last month. And guess where?”

“Yah, Park Ji-yeon! Are you telling it or not?” Hyo-min’s patience had been hooked, and the little dragon’s love of riddles was not helping. Her hand rose in a threat at the maknae’s thigh — a slap performed entirely in mime, zero force behind it.

Ji-yeon grinned, entirely unbothered by the interference.

“The securities exchange.”

“The securities exchange?” So-yeon blinked. “What was Sulli doing at a brokerage? Does she trade?”

“She was there opening an account, ran straight into oppa, and he offhandedly pointed her at a stock.” Ji-yeon picked up a slice of apple and bit through it with a crack. “And then — get this — oppa’s blood sugar apparently gave out that same day and he collapsed on the street. Sulli found him and hauled him to a hospital.”

“Collapsed?” Eun-jung’s brows pulled together, fast.

Him. Low blood sugar. That build.

She would not believe a single syllable of it.

A man with that kind of engine in bed — low blood sugar? On what planet.

Playing hard to get, is he? Of all the nerve. Just wait — I’ll settle this account the second I see him. See if he ever dares flutter around outside again.

Though — her thoughts snagged on a memory — she had once flipped that man clean onto the floor. The account had a balance going both ways. And it wasn’t as if she was afraid of him turning the tables and carrying her off to settle it in bed.

The thought completed itself before she could stop it, and heat climbed her cheeks without filing a request.

Ji-yeon, unaware that her unnie’s inner life had left the building, motored on.

“So — hospital, and Sulli sees his business card and quietly keeps the number. Later, the stock actually pays out, so she calls and insists on a thank-you dinner. One thing leads to another, and now they know each other. Yesterday she had time and came over; oppa couldn’t be bothered going out, so he cooked for her himself — and that’s the exact moment yours truly came downstairs to freeload. Caught red-handed.”

She told it with the breezy satisfaction of a woman narrating a drama she’d personally produced.

Eun-jung listened to the end and felt her soul briefly leave through the ceiling. What a plot. Screenwriters would be embarrassed. And only Park Ji-yeon would file that under “major intelligence.”

Two adults. One dinner. Not one moment of anything she’d call intimacy — and this was a briefing?

Her face, by contrast with the room, was calm water.

“I see. Then Sulli’s half his benefactor, honestly. He’s young and he’s doing well — good-natured, comfortable to be around. More friends is a good thing. Perfectly normal.”

And she meant it, which was the quiet part. He was his own person with his own orbit — industry friends, outside friends, whatever friends. Nothing in that required her permission, and nothing in it needed her feelings.

Ji-yeon, mid-victory-lap, stopped. Blinked.

Eun-jung-unnie wasn’t affected? By oppa having women friends?

She had been operating, all this time, on the firm assumption that unnie and oppa were circling each other in a slow, doomed orbit.

And if Sulli qualifies… then do I qualify?

The maknae drifted off into a landscape of new and dangerous possibilities.

The rest of the room, however, had already left Ji-yeon’s channel entirely.

Lee Qri’s gaze rested on Eun-jung’s face for one deliberate beat — then moved, unhurried, to the maknae. She had extracted one phrase from the story the way a jeweler pulls a stone: the eyes that always looked like they were doing math went narrow.

“Wait. Ji-yeon.” Not loud — but every word landed like a dropped coin, and the living room went quiet around it. “You said… oppa recommended a stock to Sulli. At the exchange. And she made real money on it.”

So-yeon got there half a second later — her palm came down flat on her own thigh.

“Unnie — you saw it too, didn’t you?! Stocks! A recommended stock! The stock information Eun-jung gave us!”

Bo-ram got there last and hardest. The chips were abandoned outright.

“Oh my god… Eun-jung-ah. Confess. That stock you made us swear on and buy — that was Song Ji-yeon-ssi’s tip too, wasn’t it?”

Six gazes converged on the corner of the sofa. Eun-jung held still under them for a moment — then nodded.

She had genuinely hoped this would hold until it stopped mattering. He’d asked her, specifically, not to tell the members where the information came from. And she’d understood his reasoning perfectly: put his name on it, and they would all buy a polite, tiny amount, out of politeness. Anonymously sourced, they’d bought like they meant it.

Which had been the entire point. It was for everyone’s good.

But the position was up forty percent now. At this point the secret had no value left to protect.

Besides — advice taken is money made. The round had already paid out generously. Her own slice of it: a shade under two hundred million won, on paper.

“So you were keeping secrets from us, Ham Eun-jung!”

“All right, all right. Yes. The stock was Song Ji-yeon’s tip.”

She gave up with a laugh.

“A few days ago he was watching the market, and he mentioned it in passing — Samjin Group was going up, and if I had idle money I should put some in. I half believed him, honestly. But he said it like a man reading tomorrow’s paper, so I followed. Then he said — we’ve got no schedules and no income right now — tell the others, tell them to put in what they can spare. And he worried you’d all buy light if you knew it came from him. So I laundered it. Told you it was my own research.”

The living room detonated.

Not one of them had so much as brushed against the possibility. The tip that had fattened every account at this table in a matter of days — actually came from him.

“Daebak! It really WAS him!”

“A restaurant company CEO — who’s secretly a stock-market genius?!”

“I did the math already — once this lands, I’m buying an apartment in Cheongdam, cash, no loan. Oppa is straight-up our god of wealth!”

Eun-jung watched the three of them orbit the ceiling — Ji-yeon, Hyo-min, Bo-ram, fully airborne — and could not keep her mouth from climbing. Hearing her sisters praise that man did something warmer to her than praise of herself ever had.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi is impressive, I’ll grant. He does everything like he’s already seen the ending.” She poured a little cold water, gently. “But the money isn’t in the accounts yet, is it? Let’s not celebrate before the count.”

For all the noise, two people at that table had gone completely silent.

Lee Qri and Park So-yeon said nothing at all — and were quietly, thoroughly shaken. Because those two had not bought light. So-yeon had put in five hundred million won. Lee Qri had put in one billion.

The position had paid out like a slot machine with a broken governor — the equivalent of eight or nine overseas tours’ earnings, banked in a week. Their goodwill toward a certain young CEO was now climbing a chart of its own, and pulling curiosity up behind it.

So young. Running a restaurant group that printed money. Reaching into the stock market and moving it with one hand. And — the part that actually mattered — treating six fallen idols with not one gram of prejudice, spending his own money and leverage to catch them on the way down.

What was the calculation? There was an old saying for people with a working money channel: get rich, quietly. Would either of them have shared a channel like that with their own members? Unclear. Honestly — probably not. And this man had handed it to Eun-jung outright, then worried the others wouldn’t trust him, and asked her to keep his name off it.

Being good to them — for what?

Between a man and a woman: if not for money, and not for name—

So-yeon did not, on balance, believe in pure friendship at that exchange rate.

Lee Qri was thinking one floor lower than that.

She already knew — had filed it as evidence, in fact — that something unspoken ran between Eun-jung and Song Ji-yeon. Whether he did all this for Eun-jung, or for aims not yet on the table, the tip had reached all six of them, not one. And that moved her, genuinely. People who had never fallen off a peak could not understand the view from down here. He behaved like a man who had memorized it anyway.

“Enough, enough. Stocks down. We’ve gossiped so long we’ve forgotten why we’re even at Ji-yeon’s today.”

They had gathered, of course, for the promise — the dinner he’d owed them since the day he bought T-ara. Eun-jung had reconfirmed it only yesterday at the company, after her messages with him: everyone, at Ji-yeon’s, today. Ji-yeon’s place had the space for six, and it sat one flight above his — convenient for the guest of honor.

Nobody had actually forgotten the purpose. The maknae had simply outgrown the format.

Two dinners at her place already — eat, drink, repeat. She’d been bored by the second round of soju.

“So… what if we go camping instead?”

She came up off the sofa like a surfacing dolphin, eyes blazing.

“We haven’t been OUT in forever! No schedules, perfect weather — tents in the countryside, grilled meat, stars! Tents! Stars! When did we last do that?!”

“Camping?” Bo-ram’s eyes lit — one full watt. “I’ve never properly camped. That actually sounds fun.”

Qri sat up straighter, interested despite her own better judgment.

“It’s a good idea. Being cooped up at home has gotten old. Just the six of us, though?”

Ji-yeon’s eyes did one full rotation, and the grin that followed had teeth in it.

“How could it be just us?! Today exists because oppa bought us — that’s the whole holiday! Eun-jung-unnie. Call him. Right now. Ask him.”

Eun-jung hesitated.

“Ask him? Is that— we’re artists. If someone photographed us together, out in the open—”

“Aigoo, so what!” The maknae applied pressure immediately. “He’s our boss now! A boss taking his own artists on an outing is the single most normal event in corporate history! Besides — we all promised, remember? He buys T-ara, we all celebrate. The camping trip IS the celebration!”

“Exactly, exactly.” Hyo-min piled on. “More people, more fun. Six girls alone in a field is just a documentary about cold.”

Outflanked on every side, and — privately — unable to find a single flaw in the maknae’s logic, Eun-jung surrendered and picked up the phone.

“Fine. I’ll ask. If he’s busy, we go without him.”

✦ ✦ ✦

PAST TWO Mapo-gu, Seoul the president’s office — Temple Run, then the tape

In Mapo, the owner of everything under discussion was in the broad leather chair, playing a game on his phone.

It was 2012. The app store was still mostly a desert with a few tents in it, and the tallest tent was Temple Run. He’d spent the past hour in a series of do-or-die sprints from a temple that, as far as the game was concerned, had a legitimate grievance against him.

Three escapes and one humiliating cliff later, the clock read two in the afternoon.

He thumbed the screen dark and opened the laptop instead — Samjin Group’s chart.

His fingers moved across the keyboard; his eyes settled onto the candlesticks stacked like a stairway to somewhere no stairway should go. After the morning session, Samjin was up forty-four percent from Monday.

One more point of climb was left in it, by his read. But he had a rule older than the position: never take the last bite of the move.

And he executed rules. Nearly forty-five percent from entry, inside a week, on somebody else’s casino floor — that was a harvest, and harvests don’t negotiate. He was mid-calculation on exit prices when the phone buzzed against the desk.

He glanced over. Caller ID: Ham Eun-jung.

A short laugh escaped him. He had been about to call her — to tell her to ready the sell orders. She’d beaten him to the phone by two minutes.

Great minds, and all that.

He picked up, and the smile was already in his voice.

Phone · Ham Eun-jung → Song Ji-yeon

“Eun-jung. Calling at this hour — what’s up?”

“Nothing’s wrong, nothing—” Her voice came in laughing, with a background of girls chirping like a loaded aviary. “You said we’d all gather today — we’re at Ji-yeon’s, and we are dying of boredom. Ji-yeon refuses another dinner at home. She wants to go camping. And she is making me ask whether you’re coming.”

“Camping?” A beat — then the laugh got out. “You lot certainly know how to pick a day.”

“So are you coming or not?” The tone went soft at the edges, the way it did when she stopped performing. “Ji-yeon says — we all promised, remember? You bought us, we celebrate. Come make it a real outing. If you’re free.”

“I’m free. I’m in.” No ceremony. “You six handle the gear and the food. I’ll close out a few things here and drive over to meet you.”

“Great!” — and behind her, instantly, a wall of cheering, from girls who had clearly been crammed against the same phone eavesdropping the entire time.

“We’ll wait at Ji-yeon’s, then. Drive carefully.”

“Mm. You lot mind yourselves too.”

“Ne! See you later, then—”

“Hold on. Don’t hang up — one more thing, and it matters.”

“Samjin can be sold now. You’re all in one room and Ji-yeon has a computer — sit everyone down and place the orders. Everything out before four o’clock. No stragglers.”

“Ah?! Isn’t it climbing all week? Sell now?”

“It climbs this week. Next week it comes off — and the day it comes off, the exits get narrow and crowded. Right now, with half the retail market camped under the chart waving buy tickets, is precisely when you offload. First rule of this trade: never take the last bite of the move—”

“I don’t understand one word of the theory.” A pause — the sound of a woman deciding to trust the man attached to it. “Ne. I’ll make all five of them sell everything, right now.”

“Good. See you soon.”

“Mm. See you soon.”

The call ended. The smile went with it. His eyes came back to the screen already in a different climate.

Now — his own exit.

He opened the order panel and began listing the position out, block by block. He’d watched this stock long enough to know what altitude does to risk: at this height, the only honest move was to bank it.

And his own book was only one of three on the desk. Goo Hara’s account and Sulli’s account both lived on this terminal too — ₩1.5 billion of the little mermaid’s money, ₩360 million of the peach’s. A full liquidation meant all three, cleaned to the last share. He was not going to let either of them sleep through the exit window and wake up holding the loss.

An hour later, it was done. Every share out.

The week’s rise had been so obscene that a standing crowd of chasers had formed under the chart, and every block he listed was snapped up on contact — one blink, one print. Without that crowd, a clean full exit inside an hour would have been a fantasy, not a plan.

He checked the balance. Five billion had become nine-point-four.

Goo Hara account₩1.5B → ₩2.16B (+44%) — liquidated in full

And yes — it pleased him. Sue him.

People said that past a certain point money becomes just numbers, that you stop feeling it. He disagreed with the people. Watching that number climb, day after day, digit by digit — that was a pleasure in its own right, and he saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

The pleasure then lasted approximately one minute, because the next item on the agenda was outbound.

He wired ₩4.4 billion out of the trading account — the ShiningYouth development fund — and topped it up with ₩600 million from his own pocket, squaring the round number: ₩5 billion, the new company’s operating budget for the year.

Run the arithmetic on the whole campaign and it came out like this: ShiningYouth alone had now swallowed nine billion won of his money. With no visible date on which any of it came home.

Aigoo. A long road, heavy luggage.

And behind that, already forming a queue: the soju plant, and every other cash-hungry monster on the master list. Just previewing it made his heart hurt.

He shook it off. Everything was trending in the right direction — that was simply a fact. One step, then the next. T-ara was bought. The agency was hours from standing up on its own legs. Somewhere out ahead, a genuinely beautiful future was waving at him with both arms.

Hold the line. Persistence is how it gets won.

He closed the laptop, stood, and took the car keys off the desk. There was nothing left in this building that needed him personally — and no reason to sit out the afternoon performing attendance.

He said a word to his father, Song Min-seok, and walked out of SAVORY F&B.

The car pointed itself toward Cheongdam-dong, where six idols and a mountain of equipment were waiting to be fed.
