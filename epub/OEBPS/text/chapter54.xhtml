Chapter Fifty-Four

How to Be a World-Class Wingman

SUNDAY · EARLY AFTERNOON Cheongdam-dong, Seoul the flat, lunch underway

Park Ji-yeon looked across the table at Eun-jung’s nothing-to-do-with-me expression, and the abacus in her head went off like a slot machine.

Unnie’s strategy was obvious: let nature take its course. Sit still. Wait for the universe.

The more she turned it over, the more she felt it — as the maknae of T-ara, she bore a solemn duty to personally contribute bricks and mortar to her unnie’s lifelong happiness.

Song Ji-yeon-oppa: rich. Handsome. Cooked like that. And — the load-bearing fact — he treated Eun-jung-unnie exactly the way she deserved. A man like that did not come around twice in one lifetime. Miss this village, and there was no inn further down the road.

And unnie was a blockhead. A blockhead with tissue-paper skin on her feelings. Wait on her to make a move, and the moment would die of old age, get up, and die again.

Conclusion: the genius maknae would have to take the field personally.

A small, unmistakably wicked smile curled up at the corner of her mouth, and upstairs, in the war room, the grand blueprint began unrolling — working title: How to Gift-Wrap My Blockhead Unnie and Deliver Her to My Future Brother-in-Law.

All of which was drafted in parallel with lunch, one hand shoveling rice, the mouth running its own channel.

“Oppa, this beef is impossibly tender — it beats the most expensive hanwoo place under our company’s office!”

“And the soup! How is the soup THIS fresh?!”

He laughed. Since when had T-ara’s maknae been moonlighting as a full-service flattery machine? He picked up a slice of the beef and dropped it into her bowl.

“If it’s good, eat more.”

For one entire meal, Park Ji-yeon carried the atmosphere of the whole table on her back, solo. The compliments came in such density that even the chef started going a little pink.

Eun-jung, meanwhile, spent the meal with her head down, eating quietly — and now and then her eyes came up, just for a beat, to the man across from her laughing and trading jokes with the maknae. There was a faint, unaccountable grievance in the look. When do I get something dropped into my bowl?

✦ ✦ ✦

Afterward, Park Ji-yeon melted into the sofa, both hands stacked on a belly gone perfectly round, and let out a long, ceremonial sigh.

“Ahhh. Full. I have officially eaten the next week’s meals in advance.”

Song Ji-yeon looked at her — half despair, half fondness — and started stacking the dishes.

Eun-jung was up at once.

“I’ll help.”

She crossed to him quick, reaching for the plates in his hands.

“No — go sit with Ji-yeon. It’s a few bowls.” He turned a shoulder out of her reach.

“It’s fine. Two people, twice as fast.” She was already lifting the empty plates off the stack, non-negotiable, and turning for the kitchen.

He watched her go and gave up on the argument with a smile. Three dishes, one soup, three rice bowls — what was that, five minutes of one man’s evening? But when Ham Eun-jung set her mind, gravity renegotiated. He gathered the rest and followed her in.

In the living room, Park Ji-yeon watched the two of them file into the open kitchen together — and the auntie smile arrived.

Content. Premium, first-run, couple content.

This is just everyday newlywed life, isn’t it.

She slid her phone out without a sound, opened a search app, and typed in one clean line: how to be a world-class wingman.

The sink wasn’t large. Two people at it, and the space went scarce.

Eun-jung stood at the basin and opened the tap; warm water ran over the white porcelain. Head down, she scrubbed at the grease with full attention, long lashes laying a shallow shade beneath her eyes. Song Ji-yeon stood beside her, receiving each washed bowl, drying it with a clean towel, piece by patient piece.

Neither of them spoke. Only the water, and now and then the bright small chime of china touching china, moving through the little room. The air held the lemon of the dish soap — and under it, a thread of something fainter, which neither of them was going to name out loud.

She could feel him there at her side without looking — the warmth coming off him, the slow rise and fall of his chest as he breathed.

Her pulse, for no reason it was prepared to defend, started climbing.

“The thing is—” It was Song Ji-yeon who finally broke the silence. “Ji-yeon’s quite the live wire, isn’t she.”

“Mm. She’s always been like that. A child who never finished growing up.” Her voice came soft, carrying a nervousness she’d have denied under oath.

“It suits her. She’s cute.”

He meant it — you could hear that he meant it.

Eun-jung’s hands paused in the water, and a small sourness arrived from nowhere with a valid ticket.

He thinks she’s cute.

Well. Naturally. Ji-yeon’s beautiful, and sweet, and easy to love. Who wouldn’t like her?

Somewhere in the middle of that spiral, her hand slipped — and a plate slicked with foam skated right out of her grip, dropping—

“Careful—!”

His hand shot out and took the plate out of the air, an inch from the floor.

And with the plate, her hand.

His palm was wide, and warm, and simply resting over the back of hers.

Eun-jung went rigid — the whole woman on pause. The heat of his palm was dry, and almost hot, and it moved like current: from that one small patch of skin, out along every wire in her at once. Her heart took a hit, skipped a full beat — then broke into a dead gallop.

For one unguarded moment, it moved. Genuinely, freely, without her permission.

Goddess Handbook · Contact with Ham Eun-jung — Goddess Ability triggered successfully. Obtained: one Stock Forecast Card.

A chime only Song Ji-yeon could hear, soft and matter-of-fact, in the middle of his skull.

He went still for half a second, and a flash of pure, secret delight crossed his eyes.

Stock Forecast Cards were the dividend Eun-jung’s contact-triggered Goddess Ability paid out. He already held one. Add the one arriving now, and that made two, sitting pretty in the card wallet.

“You okay?” He surfaced from the arithmetic and remembered, somewhat belatedly, that he was still holding her hand. The feel of it under his palm — fine, smooth, warm — registered all at once. His fingers tightened by a degree. He was in no obvious hurry to let go.

“I— I’m fine.”

She came back to herself and pulled her hand back as if off a live wire, head dropping, her eyes suddenly very interested in the drain.

“I… I’ve got it from here.”

The air had gone awkward on them. He cleared his throat and took the plate out of her hands, and this time she let him.

She stepped aside and watched him rinse, easy and practiced, and her heart stayed exactly where it had been: a mess, top to bottom.

✦ ✦ ✦

Out in the living room, Park Ji-yeon had now mainlined a good dozen articles from the “Wingman Crash Course” genre, cross-referenced the sources, distilled the wisdom, and graduated — summa cum laude — into battle doctrine of her own devising.

The Little Dragon’s Field Manual — Wingman Operations, v1.0

Step One: manufacture the alone time.

Step Two: deliver the assist. Loudly. On message.

Step Three: at peak atmosphere — vanish, gracefully, and take the credit later.

She checked the kitchen. The two of them were still in there, “washing dishes,” at a pace better suited to museum work.

Execute. Immediately.

“Aigoo— I just remembered, I have to call my eomma about the move!” She sprang off the sofa mid-sentence and was already drifting toward the entryway on it.

Two heads appeared around the kitchen doorway.

“Ji-yeon-ah — you’re leaving?”

“Ne, unnie.” Shoes on, one hand on the door, waving. “I’m off! You two — take your tiiime.”

Then, as if remembering something, she turned back. At Eun-jung she aimed one slow, unmistakable wink — and mouthed two soundless words across the room:

Fighting.

Then to Song Ji-yeon, a smile at full sweetness.

“Oppa — thank you for having us. The food was unreal! Oh, and right: I move in tomorrow. You’re coming to mine for dinner. That’s not an invitation, it’s a calendar entry.”

Gone before either of them could answer — the door pulled shut behind her with a cheerful bang.

The big living room was, all at once, down to two.

The air went to stone.

And the soft, unfinished something that had been gathering all afternoon — instead of dispersing with the maknae’s exit — settled in thicker.
