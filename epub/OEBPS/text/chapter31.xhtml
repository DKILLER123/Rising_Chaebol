Chapter Thirty-One

Weekly Meeting: Debrief and Orders

MONDAY MORNING Mapo-gu, Seoul SAVORY F&B — third-floor conference room

Monday morning arrived in Seoul with a clean, cool bite in the air.

In the underground garage at Cheongdam-dong 129, the black Mercedes turned over with a low growl and rolled up the ramp. Weekly meeting day — SAVORY F&B, full attendance.

ONGI had opened its doors the previous Thursday and hadn’t closed them since: four straight days of trading. Ji-yeon had spent those days ping-ponging between Jeju and Yeouido, but the sales-data alerts on his phone had never once gone quiet.

The car pulled up in front of the four-story office building in Mapo. He stepped out with his light-grey jacket folded over one arm, the white shirt open at the top button — a degree less boardroom, a degree more young Sajangnim in motion.

“Sajangnim, good morning.”

“Morning.”

Reception and planning-department staff bowed him in through the doors one after another. Last week’s win had put a spine in the whole building. The old hands who’d once muttered about the twenty-two-year-old parachuted in from the States now watched him pass with something that had crossed all the way over into awe. A record Saturday night will do that to an office.

✦ ✦ ✦

Third floor, conference room. The oval table was full — finance, front-of-house operations, marketing, legal — every seat fortified with a fist-thick report, every face carrying the flush of an overnighter, and every pair of eyes lit bright enough to be slightly alarming. Nobody had slept much since Thursday, and nobody looked like they minded.

Ji-yeon took the head chair and slung his jacket over the back of it.

“Right — save the ceremony. Old Kang. Numbers.”

Kang Jin-ho took his gold wire-rims off, polished them once, and stood. The deck went up on the big screen behind him.

“Sajangnim. Combined operating report — the seven Seoul ONGI stores, last Thursday through Sunday.” His voice had a tremor in it, and he didn’t bother fighting it. “Four days of trading. Seven stores. Total revenue — ₩480 million.”

“Per-store daily average is holding at roughly ₩16 million. And the Apgujeong flagship — location doing the work there — set a single-day record of ₩21 million on Saturday night.”

A low ripple of gasps went around the table.

Four days. Just under half a billion won. In 2012, before hotpot had properly conquered the country, a number like that wasn’t a result — it was a depth charge dropped into the dead water of the Korean restaurant industry, blowing a hole clean through it.

“Excluding food costs, opening-period discounts, and labor and rent amortization—” He stopped, drew a breath, and gave the room the figure they were all leaning toward. “Four-day net profit: ₩78 million.”

“Daebak…” The murmur slipped out of Planning Director Park Seon-young before he could catch it, his eyes gone full of cartoon dollar signs.

Four days, pushing ₩80 million net. Let the opening discounts lapse, let full pricing walk back in, let the word of mouth start compounding — at this trajectory, the year-end profit figure might simply buy the building they were sitting in.

Ji-yeon curled a knuckle and rapped the table, twice, crisp. The room shut itself. “Good numbers. And this is the beginning of them, nothing more.”

His gaze made one full lap of the table. “Today I’m calling out two departments.”

“First — marketing. That ₩100 million was spent like a scalpel. Old Park: the campus-forum seeding, the Kakao influencer pushes, and that free-birthday-meal-with-a-manicure campaign — the targeting was dead center. College students and young women are our core base, and you caught that wave without spilling a drop.”

Park Seon-young shot upright and bent nearly double, flustered by the honor. “It — it was all under Sajangnim’s guidance—”

“Sit.”

Ji-yeon waved him down, eyes already crossing to the other wing of the table. “Second — front-of-house operations. Four-day average table turnover: three point eight. In this country, that number is a rumor, not a statistic. Service quality never sagged under the crush — the greetings, the waiting-area snack refills, all of it stayed at standard. Those 180 new hires: you trained them beautifully.”

Lim Woo-hyun, the front-of-house director, had gone red to the ears with the thrill of it.

“Ne! Loyalty!”

“Enough — check the army stuff at the door.” Ji-yeon laughed and waved him back into his chair.

“This week’s targets are simple. Hold the service standard. Supply chain gets watched around the clock — food-loss rate stays under three percent. Old Kang, draft the bonus plan: everyone who pulled overtime this week, month-end salary doubled.”

“Long live Sajangnim!” — a cheer that tried to escape at full volume and was heroically strangled down to office-appropriate somewhere on the way out.

That was the privilege of a young boss: no painted pies on the sky, no vapor. Produce results, and the money arrived faster than any payroll in the country.

“Meeting adjourned.” He closed his folder. “Min-hyun — stay behind.”

The room emptied in a shuffle of papers and coats, every departing employee stealing one curious glance back at the legal director being held behind.

Kim Min-hyun: thirty-five this year, Seoul National University law school, five years at one of the big Seoul firms — and then poached, at a salary that had made his old partners physically ill, by Song Min-seok himself, to build and run SAVORY’s legal department.

“Sajangnim.” He closed his briefcase, moved into the seat beside Ji-yeon, and nudged his glasses up his nose. “Is there a particular legal matter to handle?”

Ji-yeon leaned back, a signature pen clicking on and off under his thumb. “Min-hyun-ah. How much do you know about the entertainment industry?”

A blink — the question had arrived from a completely unrelated county. “Some. At the firm, I handled several artist-agency contract disputes. Is Sajangnim thinking of… investing in entertainment?”

“Not investing.” The pen went still; his face went serious. “I need you to make contact with a company for me.”

“Which company?”

“CCM Entertainment.”

Kim Min-hyun’s pupils tightened — a small contraction, professionally hidden. He lived in Seoul and he read the papers; of course he knew CCM.

“Sajangnim means… the CCM currently on fire on every forum in the country. T-ara’s agency.”

“That one.” A nod; he leaned in over the table. “Approach Kim Kwang-soo, and sound out his hand.”

“Our target is the exclusive management contracts of T-ara’s six members. And not just the contracts — the group-name trademark, secondary licensing rights on every released album’s sound sources, and the MV copyrights. All of it, packaged, walking out the door together.”

For a moment the conference room held nothing but the occasional footstep out in the corridor. Kim Min-hyun frowned, running the deal’s feasibility at lawyer speed.

“Sajangnim — with respect.” The glasses got another troubled push upward. “T-ara’s public standing in this country is wrecked past repair. Commercially, buying them now is not a defensible decision. And approaching CCM at this angle — Kim Kwang-soo is an old fox. He won’t simply open his hand.”

A legal man keeps up with the news; he had followed the T-ara bullying scandal closely enough to have opinions about it.

“He’ll open it.” Ji-yeon shook his head. “Kim Kwang-soo is a man standing very still, propping up a wall. Go in first and take the depth. Tell him a foreign-capital dining giant has taken an interest in T-ara’s overseas profile and wants to acquire the group outright — for developing the Southeast Asian and Chinese markets.”

“As for budget — open the conversation at ₩3 billion.”

“₩3 billion…” Kim Min-hyun swallowed, audibly.

In a year when that still converted to a couple of million US dollars, ₩3 billion was nobody’s rounding error — and for a dead girl group with zero domestic commercial value, one that Antis were mailing rotten eggs to, it was an astronomical, indefensible, beautiful number.

“Understood.” He nodded, once, hard. “I’ll go in through a third-party channel first — framed as an overseas food-and-beverage brand scoping out a Greater China endorsement deal — and take Kim Kwang-soo’s temperature. The first crack that shows in the old fox, we move.”

“Good.” Ji-yeon stood and rested a hand on his shoulder. “And this stays sealed. Until there’s ink drying on a contract, I don’t want one syllable of it in any entertainment section, anywhere. Understood?”

“Ne, Sajangnim. Leave it to me.”

A deep bow, and the door clicked shut behind him.
