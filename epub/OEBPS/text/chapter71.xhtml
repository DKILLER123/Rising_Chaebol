Chapter Seventy-One

Making It Official

A LITTLE BEFORE SEVEN Apgujeong-dong, Seoul PIEN PLUS, sixth floor — Eun-jung’s apartment

Inside, Ham Eun-jung was folded cross-legged on the carpet in a loose black T-shirt and shorts, eyes half on the television, one hand drifting absently through fur.

Mushroom lay blissed out against her leg, eyes shut to slits, dozing through whatever she was pretending to watch.

The doorbell rang.

The quiet broke like glass. Mushroom’s head came up — he knew that smell, he knew it from the entryway to the sofa — and he sprang off her leg and barked once at the door, tail already going.

Eun-jung went still.

She got up, barefoot, and padded to the door. Leaned in. Peered through the peephole.

Song Ji-yeon.

She hadn’t needed the fisheye lens to know. She took one breath, deep and quiet, wrapped her hand around the knob, and opened the door.

He stood there in the corridor light. And she stood there in her black tee — face bare, hair a little undone — and neither of them said a word.

Her lips moved. Nothing came out.

So they just looked at each other across the threshold, while the air between them settled into a strange, fine-grained hush — delicate, charged, and somehow not the disaster of awkwardness she had spent a whole day dreading.

It was he who broke it.

“What are you up to?”

Asked lightly. As if this were any doorway on any evening, and he were any neighbor.

She blinked, then shook her head.

“Nothing. Watching TV.”

He nodded — and raised the hand holding the bag, giving it a small shake. A convenience-store bag, snacks visible through the plastic.

“I picked a few things up. I was passing by anyway, so I came up to see you.”

Which was, of course, an excuse assembled on the spot. She was in one of her moods of resistance where he was concerned; a man had to bring a reason to the door the way other people brought wine.

Eun-jung looked at the bag. Then at him. And somewhere under her ribs, a warmth rose that had no name she was willing to give it yet.

Passing by. Sure. He’d crossed the city for this doorway.

She didn’t say any of that. She just stepped aside.

“Come in.”

He changed his shoes and followed her in, Mushroom orbiting his ankles twice at cheerleading speed, tail whipping — it’s him, it’s him, it’s him.

She took the bag from him, set it on the coffee table, and folded herself back down onto the carpet. He settled onto the floor beside her — and his eye fell on the coffee table’s other residents: snack wrappers. Several of them. All of them empty, all of them beyond questioning.

“Have you eaten anything today?”

She blinked, and shook her head — on pure reflex. And now that he’d asked, her body filed its report: she was, in fact, hungry. Since dragging herself out of bed she had subsisted on crackers. Not one actual meal all day.

She shot him a glare, half accusation, half pout.

“Whose fault is that? I’ve been aching all day. I didn’t even want to get up and cook.”

He understood the charge in full. Every clause of it.

The laugh nearly got out; he wrestled it down to a smile and kept his voice level.

“Then should I take you out? I think there’s a new samgyetang place downstairs.”

He remembered her liking samgyetang. She’d put a whole golden bird on the table with her own two hands that time at Park Ji-yeon’s place.

She shook her head and burrowed deeper into the sofa, some ancient house cat refusing the outdoors on principle.

“Don’t want to go out. Too lazy to move.”

“What do you want, then? I’ll go down and buy it.”

“Don’t want anything. Just want to lie here.”

He regarded this portrait of total inertia, sighed, took out his phone, scrolled a while through the contacts — and dialed.

Two rings. Picked up.

Phone · Song Ji-yeon → ONGI Apgujeong

“Min-suk noona.”

“Sajangnim? Did you leave something at the store—”

“Could you have the kitchen put a delivery together for me? This building, sixth floor, unit 603. Classic broth — and the dishes, just build it off my usual order.”

“Right away, Sajangnim. I’ll arrange it now.”

He hung up, and Eun-jung turned her head to look at him, surprise written plainly across her face.

“Since when does ONGI do delivery?”

“It doesn’t.” He pocketed the phone. “But then, I do own the place. Should I give you Min-suk noona’s number? Then next time you don’t feel like going out, you can order straight in.”

She flushed, embarrassed — it wasn’t as if she was like this every day — and delivered her rebuttal via foot, kicking him lightly in the shin.

“Who told you to wear me out like that yesterday? I’m doing well to be sitting up today.”

Conscience: struck. He rubbed his nose and, for once in his life, chose silence.

Roughly twenty minutes later, the doorbell went. Mushroom barked once and sprinted for the door, tail a blur.

Song Ji-yeon got up and opened it.

Park Min-suk stood in the corridor holding two insulated bags, the ONGI logo crisp on both.

“Your delivery, Sajangnim.” She handed the bags over — and took one long, meaningful look past his shoulder. The apartment announced itself instantly as a woman’s: the soft lighting, the little arrangements, the whole grammar of the place. Her interior commentary was swift and merciless. Aigoo, Sajangnim. What a piece of work. Two beauties at the restaurant not an hour ago — and now a third, privately, at her own door.

“Thanks for the trouble.”

“No trouble at all! Then I’ll head back, Sajangnim.” She bowed herself out at speed — briskly, efficiently, at the exact velocity of a woman who did not want to know one single thing more about her boss’s private life.

He carried the bags into Eun-jung’s kitchen, set them on the island counter, and unpacked: sealed packets, boxed ingredients, two portions of rice. A circuit of the kitchen produced one electric hotpot. He tore the broth packet open, tipped it in, and ferried the whole pot out to the coffee table in front of the sofa.

The smell of it bloomed across the living room — and Eun-jung’s stomach betrayed her, loudly, in front of both of them.

He laughed before he could stop it.

“Not hungry, were we?”

Her face went red; she grabbed the nearest cushion and fired it at his head.

“Shut up!”

He caught it one-handed, grinning, and went back to laying out the ingredients from the bags, one by one across the table. When the broth came up to a boil, he held a pair of chopsticks out to her.

“There. Go.”

She took them, picked up a slice of beef, dipped it into the rolling broth — held it for exactly thirteen seconds — and ate it.

“Mm… that’s the ONGI taste, all right.”

Watching her, he picked up his own chopsticks and swished a slice through the pot himself. And that is how the two of them ended up sitting at that coffee table, eating — and neither one of them brought up that night, and neither one of them brought up the message that had been read and never answered. As if none of it had ever happened. As if the table were simply a table and the evening simply an evening.

And that unspoken treaty — that elaborate, mutual, perfectly synchronized silence — loosened something in the room. The air went soft. Easy. Warm at the edges.

They ate a while longer. Then Song Ji-yeon set his chopsticks down, looked across the table at Eun-jung, who was eating with her whole heart — and spoke.

“Eun-jung.”

His voice was different. No grin in it. No offhand anything. A voice she had heard maybe twice — the one he kept for things he meant all the way down.

She looked up, mid-chew, a piece of tangsuyuk in her cheek.

“Mm?”

There was sauce at the corner of her mouth. It was, objectively, adorable — and he didn’t smile at it. He looked into her eyes instead, steadily, the way a man looks when he has put down every other thing he was carrying.

“Let’s be together.”

Eun-jung’s chewing stopped — the piece of tangsuyuk in her mouth stopped — everything stopped, and then lodged sideways in her throat. She grabbed the water glass, gulped half of it, thumped her own chest twice with a fist, and barely survived the transaction.

“You— you just said what?”

The glass came down. Her ears were going red at a speed visible to the naked eye.

He watched that happen, and did not retreat one centimeter. If anything, his eyes went steadier.

“I said — let’s be together.”

A second time. Deeper in the voice than the first.

Her fingers tightened on the chopsticks. She didn’t answer — not right away. Her head bowed, and she looked down at the rice left in her bowl as if it required supervision.

“You… you’re serious?”

Barely a voice. All the certainty she didn’t have, folded into three words.

“When have I ever not been serious with you?” He shook his head, half a laugh, wholly helpless. “From the day we met until right now — everything I’ve done. Show me which part of it wasn’t serious.”

Eun-jung went quiet.

And the memories came, uninvited, in order. The first meeting — the most disastrous first meeting in Seoul, him pinned under her glare like a specimen. Jeju — her most broken hour, and the things this man had said into it when no one else was saying anything at all. And later — the promise, absurd on its face, delivered with a straight face: I’m buying T-ara.

She lifted her head. Her eyes had gone red at the rims.

“Pabo.”

That was the whole speech. And there was no anger in it anywhere — only something soft and nasal and dangerously close to tears.

Inside Song Ji-yeon’s chest, the stone he had been carrying since Tuesday morning touched the ground at last.

“So… that means…?”

She didn’t answer. She picked up a piece of tangsuyuk with her chopsticks — and set it down, with great ceremony, in his bowl.

“Eat.”

And then she took a piece for herself, ducked her head, and refused to look at him ever again.

But he had already seen it. The corner of her mouth had curved — a small, shallow, entirely unguarded arc.

Neither of them said it out loud. Neither of them swore anything. And both of them knew, completely, what had just been signed.

Some time later Eun-jung set her chopsticks down, leaned back into the sofa, laid a hand over her faintly rounded belly, and sighed the sigh of the deeply satisfied.

“Full?”

He was smiling again.

“Mm. Full.” She patted the little belly once, twice, and nodded, conclusive.

He got up and started clearing the coffee table — bowls stacked, leftovers packed back into the insulated bags, everything squared away.

Eun-jung watched his back move through her kitchen doorway, and the warmth rose in her again — bigger this time, past all naming.

She leaned against the sofa arm, head tilted, and watched him work.

“Tonight… are you staying?”

She made it sound like small talk. The most casual question in the world. Her face, on the other hand, had already gone red to the hairline.

His hands stopped over the bags. He turned his head and looked at her, and for a second he didn’t answer — because the answer was already moving through him like weather. He had no interest in leaving. None. Warm and soft and here beat a cold, cavernous flat in Cheongdam by any measure a man could name.

“Do you want me to go?” He smiled and handed the question back to her.

She glared at him. Five full seconds of glaring. Then she got up, went to the cabinet, dug around — and came back with a brand-new toiletry set, packaging intact, and a loose T-shirt, which she laid on the sofa without ceremony.

“Bathroom’s over there. Give the water heater a minute and you’ll have hot water.”

And having said it, she scooped up Mushroom, carried him into the bedroom, and added exactly nothing further.

Song Ji-yeon stood there looking at the toiletry set — brand new, packaging still sealed — and the corner of his mouth curved up, slowly, all the way.

He picked up the T-shirt and went to shower.

When he came out — loose tee, hair still damp — the living-room light was off. One dim floor lamp held the room. And the bedroom door stood ajar, a warm thread of light showing through the gap.

A woman did not issue fresh toiletries and a spare shirt in order to have a conversation. Sainthood was for other men. He didn’t hesitate; he crossed the room and pushed the door open, gently.

Eun-jung was in bed, back to the room, wrapped to the eyebrows in the blanket, one small stretch of the back of her head on display.

He stood at the bedside a moment. Then he lifted one corner of the blanket, carefully, and lay down.

The bed was large. A full body-width of demilitarized zone lay between them.

The bedroom was quiet — the particular quiet in which you can hear another person breathing, in and out, and they can hear you.

A long while passed. Then, out of the blanket, muffled, her voice arrived.

“…How did you find my place?”

“Ji-yeon told me.”

“Yah. Ji-yeon. That little traitor.”

No heat in it whatsoever.

Silence again. The lamp-thin light. The breathing.

And then she turned over — faced him in the dark — and said it softly.

“Good night… oppa.”

He blinked in the dark. Oppa. She was two years older than him, and the word was, strictly speaking, illegal — and she’d said it anyway, claimed it like a girl stealing a boyfriend’s hoodie, three sizes too big and hers now. It should not have worked on him as well as it did. It worked alarmingly well.

Not that tonight was a night for noticing. He’d already put her through one full campaign yesterday; he was many things, but not livestock, and a man who didn’t know when to simply hold the prize and sleep didn’t deserve to have won it.

His right arm reached over, gathered her in — her hair soft under his chin, her breath against his collarbone — and he answered, quietly.

“Good night.”
