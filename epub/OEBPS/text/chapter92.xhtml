NIGHT · PAST NINE Park Ji-yeon’s apartment — 2F, Cheongdam Daewoo the signing dinner, running long

“Well then — from now on, we’ll be in your care, Sajangnim!”

Lee Qri held out her signed consent form, and the smile that came with it was pure, unfiltered charm — eyes curving, one brow lifting a fraction, the whole face suddenly lit from inside.

Song Ji-yeon blinked and took the paper.

Wait. Since when is Lee Qri this… bewitching? I had her filed under cool and unreadable — the elegant older-sister type who communicates in eyebrow movements. Nobody warned me the smile had this much voltage in it.

But that was a visual group for you. Six faces, six completely different weapons, every single one of them load-bearing.

“It’s nothing. Eat — you’ve barely had two bites all evening, and if it goes cold, that’s Hyo-min’s labor down the drain.”

“He’s right.” Eun-jung laughed from her seat. “The paperwork’s done. Eat, everyone — we are not wasting Hyo-min’s cooking.”

Song Ji-yeon gathered the six consent forms back into the briefcase, snapped it shut, set it on the coffee table, and returned to the table — where Park Ji-yeon had somehow already produced an entire flat of beer from somewhere and was dealing out bottles to each of her unnies like a croupier.

She spotted him sitting down and pressed a bottle into his hands before his chair had finished sliding in.

“To celebrate the official founding of our ShiningYouth family — and the new life of T-ara! Geonbae!”

A dinner party meant drinking. There was no version of a dinner party that didn’t.

And on a night like tonight — historic, once-in-a-lifetime — drinking went without saying.

He took the bottle, and privately, he wanted to laugh.

How is this kid so thirsty, all the time? It was her idea last time too. Look at her — she’s already glowing. Ten to one she blacks out again tonight, and ten to one she wakes up tomorrow aching everywhere and squints at me like a detective reviewing a suspect. No, thank you. Not tonight.

Because tonight he had plans, and the plans were shaped like Ham Eun-jung, and they involved walking her home — properly, and at length. Too much beer and those plans would dissolve into carbonation.

He was still drafting his refusal when all six of them popped their tabs in a neat ripple and stood up around the table.

Six pairs of eyes turned to him.

Ah. So this round doesn’t come with an opt-out.

The atmosphere had already closed over his head; refusing now would be social vandalism. He gave up on the evening’s strategy with an inward sigh, cracked the tab, and stood.

“Fine — to ShiningYouth, and to T-ara. Geonbae!”

Cans touched cans, and the small bright clatter of it went around the little living room like applause.

Glug-glug-glug — Park Ji-yeon tipped her head back and took down half the can in one go, then wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, bold as a general. And out of the corner of her eye, she clocked her oppa — perfectly composed, sipping like a man at a tasting.

A scheme lit up inside her skull like a pachinko machine.

Last time, I was the only one who got wrecked. This time, oppa goes down. This time, HE does something embarrassing, and I get to be the one holding the camera.

She ran the logistics. One-on-one, she didn’t like her odds. But surely — surely — six of them could out-drink one man.

She turned to Bo-ram with a grin that should have been registered as a weapon.

“Bo-ram-unnie! You’re our eldest! Shouldn’t you be showing oppa some proper respect? You can’t just sip! You have to toast oppa — on behalf of T-ara!”

Bo-ram’s neck retracted into her shoulders like a turtle hearing thunder. Her small face went pink in real time.

Everyone knew Bo-ram’s tolerance was decorative. At dinners she took a ceremonial sip and spent the rest of the evening on barley tea. Being called out by name, in public, to lead a toast — her hands stopped working.

This is revenge. This is Lee-Bo-ram payback, is what this is. Wasn’t the entryway enough for you? Wasn’t today already humiliating enough? And now you want me to — to toast him? To look him in the eye and raise a glass? After what happened at the door?

Park Ji-yeon! Are you trying to actually kill your unnie?! Fine. Watch closely. Unnie is dying. Right here. On display.

But — she was the eldest. That was the trap of being the eldest. Somewhere in the bylaws of the group it was written that the big sister carried the honor of T-ara in front of new management, and there was no clause about being excused for having recently collision-tackled the new management.

Grumbling in the name of duty, armed with the courage of the doomed, Bo-ram raised her can.

“Yah! Park Ji-yeon! Fine — I’ll drink it! I’ll drink it, okay?! Song Ji-yeon-ssi — one more. From me.”

He blinked — but his arm was already up, can meeting can on reflex.

Gathering herself, Bo-ram copied the little dragon’s form exactly: head back, throat open, a big brave swig—

— and two seconds later the coughing hit like a fire alarm. Her pale face flooded pink at a speed visible to the naked eye.

“Khh— khh— khh—!”

Park Ji-yeon scrambled for tissues and held them out, helpful and shameless.

“Yah! Ji-yeon! Quit bullying your Bo-ram-unnie.” So-yeon had seen enough. She smacked the little dragon’s arm with a straight face and slid a soft drink down the table to Bo-ram.

Bo-ram seized it and drank, and the coughing finally released her.

Park Ji-yeon only grinned — the rebuke rolled off her like rain off vinyl — and rotated her targeting scope to Song Ji-yeon.

“Oppa! Bo-ram-unnie toasted you and everything! If you don’t respond to that, it’s seriously rude!”

He looked at her over the rim of his can, speechless.

“Wasn’t bullying Bo-ram-unnie enough for you? Now it’s my turn? Don’t listen to her, everyone — she just wants the world on fire. Drink at your own pace.”

He shook his head, half amused.

Where is she even learning this? Round after round, everyone toasts the boss, one per customer — for a second there I was back at Dad’s staff dinners in LA Koreatown. The aunts at the restaurant used to run toasts exactly like this. All that was missing was someone forcing extra meat onto my plate.

He studied the dragon for a moment and understood the whole plan at once: she simply wanted him drunk.

And what did she imagine would happen after he was drunk?

He looked at her evil little grin and declined to follow the math any further.

Three days without a poking and she’s rebuilding the furniture. Noted. Sooner or later this dragon gets the disciplining of her life.

“How is that bullying?” Park Ji-yeon’s mouth twisted, entirely unrepentant. “Bo-ram-unnie drank too fast and choked all by herself. I said nothing.”

Then she beamed at him and raised her can again.

“But oppa, look — you’re our Sajangnim now. Tonight of all nights, each of us gets to toast you once, right? That one counts as Bo-ram-unnie’s. This one’s mine —”

The look on her was the look of a girl who would accept surrender, but only in writing, notarized.

He glanced around the table for backup and found none. The others said nothing, but every eye at the table had the exact same shine in it — anticipation, delight, the standing ovation they were giving in advance to whatever embarrassment Park Ji-yeon was about to inflict on their new boss.

Lee Qri had actually propped her chin on her hand to watch, the posture of a woman at a tennis final.

“…Fine. That’s fair.”

He gave up on dignity as a strategy, lifted the can, and tipped it toward the table.

“Since we’re one family from tonight — this one, I drain. May ShiningYouth live up to its name. Always shining. Forever young.”

He tipped his head back and took the rest of the can down in one unbroken pull.

“Wah — now THAT’S a Sajangnim!”

Park Ji-yeon led the applause, and the living room broke into cheers and laughter.

With the dragon drumming at the front, the party caught fire for real — and everyone was more than happy to march behind her.

So-yeon rose next, can in hand.

“Sajangnim — since Ji-yeon and Bo-ram-unnie have both toasted you, allow me one as well.”

Another can went down his throat.

He set the empty down without a word and surveyed the field: Eun-jung and Hyo-min both radiating the restless energy of women drafting their own toasts, Lee Qri thoroughly entertained, Bo-ram recovered and clutching her barley tea like a life vest.

I have walked into a web, he thought. A very cheerful, very well-lit web, and the spiders keep refilling my glass.

The dinner was winding down, but the table was still warm with talk when Song Ji-yeon checked the time — past nine — and set his can down and stood.

“It’s late. I should head back — work tomorrow.”

He’d drunk more than he’d meant to. The little dragon had kept dragging him into “one more round,” and the others had kept orbiting in with toasts of their own. It was only beer — but beer adds up, and it adds up sideways, in volume. He was pleasantly, mildly fogged now, and — more decisively — he felt like a water balloon.

So: no more.

At the word “leaving,” chairs scraped around the table as the others rose to see him off.

All except one.

Park Ji-yeon stood up in stages — sway, catch, sway — face flushed traffic-red, and flung both arms wide across the room, blocking Eun-jung’s path with the authority of a checkpoint.

“No… nobody’s going anywhere! Nobody… leaves!”

One hand anchored on Eun-jung, the other finger sweeping the room, she pronounced through a thick tongue:

“I… I did the guest rooms up today! Tonight we keep drinking! Whoever… whoever gets drunk just sleeps here!”

Song Ji-yeon had to admire it, in a horrible way.

So THAT’S what the bedding trip was about. Last week, the home-goods mart, two hours of her testing pillows against her own face — I thought I was just being a pack mule. She was stocking the barracks. The whole thing was premeditated.

Hyo-min and So-yeon traded a look that said our member, our responsibility, and moved in to gentle her down.

“Ji-yeon-ah, it’s late. The Sajangnim has work tomorrow — and so do we.”

“Exactly. You’ve had way too much. Come on, bed.”

“No!”

She grabbed both their hands at once, a small fierce animal guarding its food bowl.

“Tonight… nobody goes anywhere!”

Song Ji-yeon laughed, stepped in, and tapped her once on the forehead — lightly, dead center, the fingertip of a man who has exhausted every other tool.

“Okay. That’s enough out of you.”

It was a gentle motion, and the voice under it was gentle with her in a way he didn’t bother disguising.

“Tomorrow, my legal department sits down with Kim Kwang-soo to start the acquisition in earnest. If it goes smoothly, the company will call you in for a briefing. So no more drinking tonight — go sleep, and store up your strength.”

He was leaving because he wanted to leave, and what T-ara did after that was beyond his jurisdiction — but they deserved a head’s-up on tomorrow, so they wouldn’t be ambushed by their own future.

The sentence landed, and — except for the dragon, who was past processing sentences — five women sobered on the spot.

They’d known the negotiation would come, that meetings would be called once Song Ji-yeon’s side moved. But tomorrow?

“That fast?” Lee Qri’s brows went up.

“My legal team has actually been in contact with Kim Kwang-soo for about a month.” He nodded. “The essentials are already settled. Tomorrow just makes it official.”

So-yeon absorbed that, and her posture changed — the team leader arriving on top of the buzz.

“Understood, Song Ji-yeon-ssi. Then we’ll go home tonight and rest properly — and wait for the company’s notice.”

Bo-ram nodded so fast and so many times she resembled a pecking chick, eyes shining.

“Ne! We’ll be fully charged!”

“The moment the company calls,” Hyo-min added, smiling, “not one of us is late. Not one.”

Lee Qri, though, was in no hurry to pledge anything. Her peach-blossom eyes curved, and she looked at Song Ji-yeon with something that was almost, but not entirely, a smile — drawing the last syllable out like taffy.

“Sajangnim… you’d better keep your word, hmm? Don’t keep us waiting too long — or we’ll start thinking you only said it to make us happy.”

Why is Lee Qri talking like that? All syrup and drawn-out vowels? She wasn’t like this the first few times — she barely talked at all. Is this a policy change? Did the group vote on it?

He kept the thought off his face and smiled.

“When have I ever strung you along? The day the ink dries on the real contract — celebration drinks, on me. Tonight, we call it here.”

“It’s a deal!” Hyo-min took it first, and the rest of the table dissolved into laughter.

With business as the official excuse, all thoughts of a second round evaporated. Coats came off hooks, bags were gathered from the sofa, everyone funneled toward the door.

And then Park Ji-yeon — who had been swaying against the doorframe with her eyes at half-mast — suddenly snapped wide awake, as if the conversation had only just now reached her through deep water. Her unfocused gaze tracked from face to face, and she hauled herself upright by the door itself.

“Wait… unnies… then when… when do we get to go to China?”

Eun-jung burst out laughing and patted her cheek, lightly, twice.

“You. The ink isn’t even dry and you’re already packing for the world tour. Let’s get YOU into bed first.”

Park Ji-yeon’s mouth went into a pout; she mumbled two more syllables of protest; then her whole body listed sideways toward the floor.

Eun-jung’s hand shot out and caught her by the arm before gravity could file the paperwork. She looked back at the others.

“I’ll stay with her tonight. You all go ahead.”

So-yeon nodded — every one of them knew exactly how impossible a drunk Park Ji-yeon became after lights-out.

“Then we leave her to you, Eun-jung-ah. Thank you.”

Eun-jung steered the dragon onto the sofa and laid her down flat.

Then she and Song Ji-yeon saw the remaining four — So-yeon, Bo-ram, Hyo-min, Lee Qri — down to the street.

The night air had teeth in it, the good kind, the kind that cleans a head full of beer fumes.

The four of them walked out past the guardhouse of the Cheongdam Daewoo complex to the corner, where So-yeon raised a hand and flagged down a taxi.

“Sajangnim, Eun-jung-unnie — we’re off. Thank you for tonight.” Hyo-min reached the car first and turned, waving. “When the contract’s really signed — we’re counting on you to look after us.”

“You can count on it.” He nodded, smiling.

Bo-ram stood a little behind the others, cheeks still carrying their evening pink. She bowed to him — small, precise, mortified — and her voice came out at a volume normally reserved for libraries.

“Good night, Sajangnim… and — about earlier… I’m really sorry.”

Each word came out smaller than the last. By the end of the sentence she was addressing the ground, and appeared to be attempting to fold herself entirely into her own collar.

That flying pounce was, hands down, the single piece of footage she would most like to delete from her entire career.

“It’s nothing. That one was my fault — I startled you.”

“N—no! Not at all!” Bo-ram’s hands waved frantically in front of her, face going from pink to red, and she tried to escape into the taxi before the conversation could develop any further.

Lee Qri, watching from beside the car, was enjoying herself so much it was practically audible. She caught Bo-ram by the sleeve and held her in place.

“Bo-ram-unnie, what’s the fire? Where are you running? Song Ji-yeon-ssi doesn’t bite.” A beat. The smile sweetened by one full degree. “Or… did you want to go for one more pounce?”

“LEE QRI!!!”

Bo-ram stamped her foot, scarlet to the ears, and had to be physically bundled into the car before she combusted.

“All right, all right — leave Bo-ram-unnie alone, you menace.” So-yeon laughed, pushing the last of the flailing Bo-ram in, then turned back, all business again. “Sajangnim — truly, thank you for tonight. And Eun-jung-ah, we’re leaving the patient to you. Anything comes up — the phone works both ways.”

Lee Qri got in last. But just before she ducked into the seat, she turned — and looked at Song Ji-yeon and Eun-jung, standing together in the streetlight, for one long, deliberate beat. The smile riding her mouth had a hook in it.

“Eun-jung-ah… take good care of yourself too, hmm?”

The emphasis was a fraction of a gram off dead center, and it landed exactly where she’d aimed it.

And she knew perfectly well that Eun-jung was the older of the two — older by years, not months. She wasn’t going to say it out loud. Not yet. She wanted, first, to see exactly what those two thought they were doing — and how long they thought they could keep doing it under her nose.

“…Got it. Drive safely.”

The four of them piled in. The door slid shut; hands waved from the open window as the taxi pulled off the curb.

Song Ji-yeon and Eun-jung stood side by side at the corner and watched it thread into the traffic, all the way until its taillights dissolved into the general red of the city. Then they looked at each other — and both smiled at exactly the same time — and turned back toward the lobby.

The stairwell was silent. Only their footsteps, the two sets of them, soft on the tile.

At the elevator, he pressed the call button, and they stood in the little pool of its light. And in that gap — the wait, the hum, the pleasant three-percent fog of good beer — a bad idea arrived, considered the available terrain, and moved in.

His arms came around her from behind, closing her into him whole — a full, tight circle, chin coming down into the hollow of her shoulder. His breath, warm, arrived at the side of her neck, and his voice came out low, magnetic, with a smile folded inside it.

“Want to… come over to my place for a bit?”

She knew precisely what kind of “a bit” that was. Her face went hot on the spot — and under the heat, her heart did something humiliatingly sweet.

But she had learned things, thank you. She had been to this exact crossroads before, and she knew what it cost to improvise.

Her hand reached back and found the soft part of his waist, and pinched — calibrated: not too light, not too hard — the pinch of a woman filing a formal complaint in the language of affection.

“Oppa. Let me go up first and put Ji-yeon to bed. Then I’ll come down.”

She tilted her head, just slightly, voice dropping lower — shyness and grievance braided together.

“Last time I stayed at your place, I left my things at Ji-yeon’s. The next morning I didn’t dare go back for them — I was terrified she’d figure something out. They’re still sitting up there, in her living room, gathering evidence.” A breath. “Once she’s properly asleep, I’ll come down.”

He looked at the faint rose still riding her cheeks, and he did not let go. If anything, the circle tightened.

“I’ll come up with you.”

Something in her chest went soft and warm, and she didn’t even try to refuse.

They rode up together and let themselves into Park Ji-yeon’s apartment.

Inside, the living room still held the heat and the wreckage of the party — empty cans, abandoned side dishes, the ghost of laughter. And the person who had generated ninety percent of that noise was now flat on the sofa, out cold, sleeping like a hostage to her own enthusiasm.

Song Ji-yeon and Eun-jung looked at her, then at each other. The same helpless look, passed silently back and forth.

No tolerance, all thirst. That’s the dragon, all over. Never again. Never, ever again are we drinking together.

He bent, gathered the sleeping Ji-yeon up off the sofa in both arms, and carried her into the bedroom, laying her down on the bed with the care of a man moving museum stock.

Eun-jung worked beside him with practiced hands: coat off, folded; a towel wrung out warm from the bathroom; the careful wiping-down of a face and two hands — a mother’s technique, an unnie’s patience.

Song Ji-yeon drew the blanket up over her, easy and unceremonious.

Maybe some of it reached her through the dark, because the sleeping girl turned over, mumbling into the pillow.

“Eun-jung-unnie… don’t go…”

Then, after a pause, with the gravity of a final request:

“…I still want to drink…”

She smacked her lips once, twice, and then dropped clean through into deep water. Her breathing stretched out, slow and even.

Eun-jung looked down at the sleeping face — pure child, every idol defense stripped away by sleep — and smiled the smile you only smile at people you’d forgive anything. She tucked the blanket edge in properly, confirmed the dragon would not freeze in the night, and stood.

“Okay.” A whisper. “Let’s go.”

They slipped out of the bedroom and drew the door closed behind them with a click no louder than a blink.

Down one floor. Through his own door.

And as the door shut — soft, final, the corridor’s light cut off at the seam — he gathered her in from behind, and the apartment, unlit, folded itself around the two of them.

He didn’t reach for the switch.

Neither did she.

No champagne this time. No necklace. No door I had to be brave at. Just me, and a flight of stairs, and him waiting at the bottom of them. I chose this. I walked down those stairs on my own two feet, and I’d walk them again.

She turned around inside the circle of his arms — the small revolution that had taken her months, and several miracles, to be able to make — and found his mouth in the dark by memory.

He tasted of beer and the cold night air, and underneath both, faintly, of the dinner she’d cooked half of. She decided this was information worth keeping.

“You’re punctual,” he murmured against her lips, and she felt the smile in it.

“Don’t get used to it.”

She learned him the way he’d learned her: slowly, by touch, with both hands and a certain ferocious attentiveness — every place that made his breath snag filed away, cross-referenced, revisited. She found that she loved his stomach jumping under her palms. She found that she loved, loved, the sounds a composed man makes when composure stops being available to him.

When she finally sank down onto him, they both made the same sound — relieved, destroyed, identical — and her head dropped forward against his collarbone, and for a while neither of them moved at all, joined in the dark, learning to breathe around the size of the feeling.
