Chapter Forty-One

Sulli: Leave It to Me

SAME EVENING Yeongdeungpo-gu, Seoul ONGI Yeongdeungpo — the Geumseong room

“So — roughly how much longer does your drama shoot?”

Sulli was deep in single combat with a beef tendon ball, and she answered around it, magnificently unintelligible: “Mmf… the director says if everything goes well, we wrap this month.”

She got the meat down, chased it with a swallow of juice, and carried on. “Then I rejoin the group. The company says f(x) has a comeback planned for next year, so it’ll be music-show stages and variety schedules, the whole circuit, all over again.”

“Oh? f(x) is coming back?” He put a fair copy of surprise into it. There it is. The opening I was fishing for.

“Ne!” And now she was bright-eyed — though the brightness came with a small complaint riding sidecar. “Finally, a stage again. Filming’s fun and everything, but I like it better with the members. It’s just… comeback weeks are brutal. You sleep three hours a night. Four, if the company is feeling merciful.”

“Your group is five members, right?” He kept his tone idle, walking it forward. “Besides you, I honestly don’t know any of them.”

“Ne — five!” The subject hit like a switch. Sulli sat up straighter and began laying out her members with the pride of a shopkeeper presenting stock, one finger at a time. “Amber-unnie’s our rapper — she’s SO cool, and she looks after the rest of us constantly. And Luna-unnie is main vocal — her high notes are insane, seriously. And Krystal’s our maknae. Little Soo-jung’s even younger than me!”

A finger went down for each name. The last finger belonged to the only one he actually wanted to hear about.

“And then our leader — Vic-unnie.” Her whole voice changed on the name: softer, dependent, the tone of someone who has been genuinely taken care of for years. “Vic-unnie takes care of everyone. Oh — and she cooks! I love her cooking. Her Chinese food, especially—”

“Victoria, you don’t need to sell me on.”

He kept his eyes on the rolling broth while he said it.

“Her posters are hanging on every street in this city right now.”

And they were. Victoria — Song Qian — was at this exact moment the hottest female idol in the country, and the stretch of time that later generations would canonize as the legendary Victoria era was, in fact, this one. He was, in the most literal sense, eating dinner at the edge of it.

“Yah, oppa!” Her showcase had been yanked out from under her mid-flourish, and she pouted about it. “Can’t you at least let me finish?”

He smiled and kept the rest to himself. I might know your Vic-unnie better than you do, Peach. I’ve got a dozen-some years of extra memories on you — and most of those years were spent online.

“Actually — since we’re on the subject.” He set his chopsticks down. “I’m preparing to launch a soju. New product. I’ve been chewing on who to put in front of it for weeks. What would you say to Victoria?”

“Soju?” Sulli blinked, big eyes filling up with pure curiosity. “Oppa, aren’t you the hotpot guy? When did hotpot become soju?”

Across the table, Kim Ji-won had stopped eating too. She looked up at him, chopsticks paused, something quietly investigative in those clear eyes.

“Hotpot and soju are a natural pair.” He laughed it off with half a line. “The product’s called Saero. It’s a zero-sugar soju.”

He put deliberate weight on the words zero sugar.

It landed exactly the way he’d designed it to. Both women’s expressions flickered — a small, synchronized shiver of professional instinct. For female entertainers who managed their bodies for a living, the word sugar was less an ingredient than a curse. Practically the devil’s own name.

“Zero-sugar… soju?” Sulli’s interest was now fully, catastrophically hooked. “So you can drink it and it won’t make you fat?”

“In theory, that’s the idea.” He nodded, and slid the real question in under it. “So — do you think she’d fit?”

“Of course she’d fit!” The answer was out of her mouth before her brain had even finished waking up. Then her brain caught up, and she tacked on, more carefully, “Only… Vic-unnie already has so many endorsements. I don’t know if she’d take another one. An alcohol one, at that.”

Sulli knew perfectly well what Victoria’s endorsement portfolio looked like. Famous cosmetics houses — the kind whose names every woman on the Peninsula knew by heart. And oppa’s company was… well, it was small. Seven restaurants, however delicious. Would Vic-unnie even look at a deal from a company that size?

But then she turned it over once more, and it came up differently. Oppa grew up over in America, and Vic-unnie came all the way here from China. The two of them know exactly what it is to be the outsider in this industry — the one nobody quite knows what to do with. They’d understand each other. If anyone would hear oppa out without asking how big his company was, it’d be Vic-unnie. Might as well introduce them.

She leaned across the table, all playfulness set aside, and asked the question properly.

“Oppa. Are you serious? You really want Vic-unnie for this?”

Song Ji-yeon looked at the gravity on her face and privately wanted to laugh. Outwardly he wore the calm of a man discussing quarterly figures.

“I’m giving it real thought. Miss Victoria’s image — her overall feel — is honestly the closest fit to what the product needs.”

He paused, and let a sigh carry the next part out of him.

“But I have no ties to SM. No channel to her, either. Which makes it… not simple.”

Cooperating with SM Entertainment was never going to be a small thing. SAVORY F&B was a company whose entire empire was seven restaurants. SM was a giant of the Peninsula’s entertainment industry — one of its three heads. He didn’t need to close his eyes to know what would happen if he mailed a partnership proposal into SM’s business affairs office under the SAVORY name. It would go onto a shelf in somebody’s cubicle, and it would die there, mid-sentence, un-mourned.

It would be like a strip-mall taco counter cold-calling a Hollywood A-lister’s agent about a coupon flyer. The money wasn’t even the issue. Taking the job would cheapen the star — so nobody would return the call.

Which was precisely why he wanted to reach Song Qian privately. Have her carry the intent home to her company herself, so that when the conversation started, he’d already have a seat at the table. Otherwise he couldn’t even get through SM’s front gate — not with seven restaurants behind his name.

He’d barely finished saying it when Sulli’s eyes lit up like signal flares and she slapped her own chest.

“What’s so hard about that?! Leave it to me!”

Leave it to me!

Her whole face said trivial. “I’ll go talk to Vic-unnie for you! Only — she’s not even in the country right now. They’re flying to Shanghai for a concert, and after Shanghai it’s straight to Jakarta… I honestly don’t know when she’ll be back in Korea.”

“Then I’ll owe you one, Sulli. Take your time — I’m in no hurry.” He smiled as he said it. No hurry whatsoever. The distillery doesn’t so much as exist yet.

“Aigoo, it’s nothing!” She waved both hands, magnificently careless. “Vic-unnie and I are close. Passing along a message — it’s one phone call. Piece of cake!”

Done.

Now it came down to talking Song Qian around, and then putting the right number on the table. After that — surely that old fox Lee Soo-man wouldn’t say no. Right?

✦ ✦ ✦

By the time the meal finally wound down, Sulli was leaned all the way back in her chair, one hand resting on the faint, contented curve of her belly. She produced a small, extremely dainty burp, then looked proud of it.

“I can’t, oppa. I genuinely don’t think I can walk.”

Song Ji-yeon looked at her — boneless, satisfied, lazy as a cat in a sunbeam — and then at the battlefield of the table, where the meat platters had been reduced to their rims, and he laughed.

“Didn’t somebody announce a figure to maintain? Somebody put away quite a bit of food tonight.”

“It’s an occasional indulgence!” The tongue came out, entirely unrepentant. “Besides, your hotpot is too good. Self-control wasn’t on the menu. I checked.”

Kim Ji-won dabbed the corners of her mouth with a napkin, quiet and exact, and offered her verdict along with it.

“It really is excellent. I’ve never eaten anything like this before.”

The three of them came out of the Geumseong room to the front counter, and Song Ji-yeon was already lifting a hand to summon the manager when Kim Ji-won stepped past him — and produced, from her wallet, a black card.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi.” She held it out toward the waiting manager, and her voice left no room for negotiation. “You saved me today. This meal is mine to buy.”

“Kim Ji-won-ssi — there’s really no need. This is my restaurant. There’s no version of tonight where the guest pays.”

“Which is exactly why it has to be this way.” Her eyes stayed level on him, clear and completely serious. “If you paid for tonight’s dinner on top of everything else, it would sit on my conscience for a very long time. Please. Let me say thank you properly.”

She extended the card toward the manager again, arm unwavering. The manager froze in the no-man’s-land between them, looked at his Sajangnim for instructions, and received no rescue whatsoever.

“Oppa, just let unnie pay!” Sulli was enjoying this far too much. “It’s her treat — it’s from the heart!”

Song Ji-yeon looked into Kim Ji-won’s eyes, found them written end to end in stubbornness, and finally surrendered with a smile.

“…Fine. This once, we do it your way.”

Only then did the manager accept the card with two respectful hands and withdraw. He returned shortly with the slip and the card, both presented back to Kim Ji-won with a bow.

“All settled. Thank you for your patronage.”

The three of them came out of the ONGI and climbed into the Mercedes, night-time Yeongdeungpo still blazing away outside the windows.

Sulli hadn’t come down from the food at all. Wedged into the back seat with her arm looped through Kim Ji-won’s, she talked the entire way, bright and unstoppable.

“Ji-won-unnie, did you try the hand-pounded shrimp paste?! It’s SO bouncy! And the beef tongue — I have never in my life had tongue that tender—”

“Mm. I tried everything.” Kim Ji-won smiled and answered her gently — but her gaze, of its own accord, kept drifting forward to the back of the man in the driver’s seat.

They reached the production’s hotel soon enough: a plain, no-frills eighteen-story building beside the Han River Park. He brought the car to a smooth stop at the curb and cut the engine.

“We’re here.”

“Oppa — thank you for the desserts AND the hotpot today! I am seriously SO happy right now!” Sulli unbuckled and draped herself over the window frame, eyes bent into crescents.

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi.” Kim Ji-won leaned forward from the other side and dipped into another small bow. “Today… thank you. Truly. You saved me — and then you fed me a meal like that on top of it.”

There was nothing performative in it. The careful distance she’d kept all afternoon was simply gone, and what was left was plain, unguarded sincerity.

“It was nothing. Go up and rest — early call tomorrow.”

“Ne! Good night, oppa!”

“Song Ji-yeon-ssi — drive safely.”

The two girls climbed out, stood at the curb, and waved until the car pulled forward. Then they turned together and walked in through the hotel’s front doors.

Song Ji-yeon watched until both of them had vanished into the lobby. Only then did he turn the ignition over, swing the Mercedes around, and point it toward Cheongdam-dong.
