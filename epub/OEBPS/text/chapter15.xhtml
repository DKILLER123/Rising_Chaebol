Chapter Fifteen

The Head Pat

SEPTEMBER 2012 Gangnam-gu, Seoul Umi barbecue house — a private room

Inside, the restaurant was all appetite — charcoal smoke and the char of rendered fat, meat hissing on grills, laughter leaking out of the rooms along the corridor. Nobody could walk in hungry and stay calm.

Sulli was clearly a regular. She greeted the owner the way you greet family, and the owner — a round-faced middle-aged woman in a navy apron — lit up like a porch light, patted Sulli on the shoulder as if she were a niece home from college, and personally walked them back to a private room.

Sulli had that kind of face. Ajumma bait, pure and simple.

The room was small and immaculate. A wooden sliding door shut the noise out; a few Korean landscape prints hung on the wall, pale and calm in the warm light. The low table came already laid — banchan, sauces, and a kettle of iced barley tea beading with condensation.

The charcoal arrived shortly after, briquettes glowing red in the brazier, crackling and throwing the occasional spark.

Sulli laid beef belly on the grill with the confidence of long practice. The meat hit the hot iron and started talking — sssss — fat dripping through onto the coals, little flames flaring up a hand-span and dying back down.

“Song Ji-yeon oppa, try this.” She flipped a slice off the grill at the exact moment its edges charred, rolled it through the dipping sauce until it wore a dark-red glaze, glistening, and set it down on his plate with a care usually reserved for fragile things. Then she looked up, eyes bright. “Beef belly’s the house specialty. Vic-unnie and Soo-jung order it every single time.”

Soo-jung — that had to be f(x)’s Krystal. And Vic-unnie could only be Song Qian. Victoria.

Now there was a name he hadn’t expected to hear over a grill in Seoul. In his old life he’d watched enough f(x) stages that hearing it felt oddly warm — like spotting a familiar face in a train station in another country.

“Not bad.” He tried the slice she had grilled herself — crisp at the edge, juicy underneath, the sweet and salt of the sauce opening up against the richness of the fat. He nodded. For him, that was a standing ovation.

Sulli beamed like she had been handed a trophy. Her eyes folded into two crescents, and her hands sped up — flipping with one, fetching with the other, running a continuous supply line of beef from grill to his plate until it rose into a small mountain.

“It’s good, so oppa has to eat more! I’m on a strict regime this week — every gram of this meat is legally yours.”

He looked at the mountain. He looked at her.

I’m not a pig. Why is every woman in my life doing this to me? Eun-jung, same story — “I have to watch my figure.” Do these idols have a schedule every single day of the year? Is the entire industry on a permanent diet?

“Something coming up? A comeback?” He asked it reasonably, and put a piece in his mouth.

“Yah!” The tongs went down in protest. “Does oppa not follow my activities at all?” Lower lip out far enough to rest a teacup on, eyebrows knotted, arms crossed — the full sulk.

“Mianhae. I’ve been buried. I haven’t opened an entertainment article in a month.” He genuinely hadn’t. Since the last time they’d met, ONGI had eaten his entire life — renovation, menu, hiring, every last detail through his own hands — and today was the first day he’d come up for air. And it wasn’t only Sulli’s schedule he’d missed. If he and Eun-jung didn’t text, he wouldn’t know hers either.

He was a man planning to become a chaebol. Time and attention were capital, and you did not spend capital on gossip.

“Aish… fine.” The sulk evaporated mid-breath, and what replaced it was the particular light that only came on when she talked about work she loved. “I’m in a drama! To the Beautiful You — six episodes have aired already. Oppa can even watch it at home.”

Acting flipped a switch in her. Chopsticks down, both hands free, she talked and talked — how good the script was, what kind of girl she was playing, how the character was nothing like her and exactly like her at the same time. When a part excited her, her eyes went perfectly round and her speech accelerated until the words piled up against each other, her hands sketching shapes in the air above the grill.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Song Ji-yeon received a free education in how television worked on the Peninsula.

Korean drama production ran on the writer’s word. Shows shot while they aired, and the writer steered week by week — the ratings came in, the viewers weighed in, and the plot bent to match. Whatever the audience wanted, the audience got.

It made for astonishing flexibility. It also meant an actor could be handed a script still warm from the printer at any hour, and everything memorized yesterday could be waste paper by breakfast.

He had to hand it to them. The system was brutal on everyone inside it — actors, writers, all of them — and the Peninsula kept producing runaway hits anyway. Built for the market, by the market, at a cost.

“Oppa — do you want to visit our set?” She had talked herself up to full momentum: hands clasped now, leaning across the table, eyes locked on his. The invitation had plainly been sitting in her pocket all evening, waiting for daylight.

“Where are you shooting? If I can find a window, I’ll come by.” He said it easily, fingertips ticking against the table edge.

In truth, the calendar was a wall. In a couple of days he was flying to Jeju for the ICC concert — the show Eun-jung had been pouring herself into, rehearsing until past midnight every night. What he was actually scheduling was the aftermath.

Because that girl still believed — bless her entire stubborn heart — that the hate online was a loud minority. That the real fans were still out there. That a concert hall full of them would show up and stand on T-ara’s side. She was betting everything on one night, ready to flip the group’s whole reputation in a single encore.

He understood the math she was doing. The math just didn’t work.

Eun-jung-ah, you’re thinking too simply. Ten thousand hearts don’t turn around on a dime.

“Chincha?!” Sulli’s eyes went bright, palms pressed together under her chin. “We’re shooting in Yeouido right now — oppa, text me before you come, I’ll send my manager down to meet you!”

“Deal.” He nodded and drank some of the iced barley tea, the cold cutting cleanly through the richness. Behind the calm, he was already assembling the Jeju trip: dates, flights, a hotel — and the part that came after. How, exactly, you comforted a girl who was about to be disappointed by ten thousand people at once.

“By the way, oppa—” the grin crept back, tongs back in hand, though her eyes kept sliding over to him, “—is Samsung going to keep going up? Watching that stock is taking years off my life. Red one minute, blue the next. My heart can’t take it.”

“Samsung Electronics you hold, and you hold. It’ll climb all year, on balance. If you’ve got spare cash, add to it without fear, and take your profit at New Year’s.” He said it the way other men read tomorrow’s weather.

Samsung was load-bearing for the whole country; the government would let it collapse over the government’s dead body. If he were investing rather than harvesting, he would hold it for the long run too. But bigger things were coming, and capital tied up in one stock was capital asleep at its desk.

“Chincha?” She blinked — and leaned in over the table, voice dropping to a stage whisper, absolutely dripping intrigue. “Oppa. Do you have… insider information?”

“You think too much.”

He reached over and ruffled her short hair.

Softer than he’d expected. It slid between his fingers and carried that faint peach sweetness with it.

Sulli went completely still.

A rabbit, touched without warning. The heat came up her neck in one motion, and her heart was suddenly loud enough to hear — drum-on-ribs loud, filling both ears.

But she didn’t duck away from the hand.

The warmth of his palm spread out from the crown of her head, slow, all the way down. It was a warmth that made you lean in, not pull back.

She hadn’t been patted on the head in a long, long time.

There had been a season for it, once. Vic-unnie used to do it, and Hara-unnie too — back when she was the baby of every room she walked into. Then she’d grown, and grown, and somewhere along the way the pats had quietly stopped coming. Everyone had apparently convened and agreed that she was grown-up now. That she didn’t need it.

Except sometimes she missed it. Being the one someone fussed over. Being somebody’s little one.

So when Song Ji-yeon’s hand settled on her head, she nudged into it — quietly, just slightly, an inch of movement she would deny under oath. A cat leaning into the hand that pets it.

Song Ji-yeon, for his part, was experiencing internal difficulties.

This was their second meeting. Second. “Friends” was a generous word for what they were. Reaching across a table and ruffling a girl’s hair like he’d earned the right to — there was a word for men who did that, and he had spent an entire previous life trying not to be that word.

But the Handbook’s reward wasn’t going to collect itself.

And the reward — notably — did not collect.

No ding. No golden card. The Handbook sat at the back of his skull like a stuffed toy: silent, inert, taking up space.
